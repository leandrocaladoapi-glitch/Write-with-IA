# Write with AI

**A Practical Manual for Beginners: From First Idea to Finished Story, with an AI Writing Partner**

---

## Preface

Every year, millions of people decide they are going to write a book. Most of them do not finish. Not because they lack ideas — ideas are the cheapest part of writing — but because between the glowing first sentence and the last page lies a desert of decisions: What happens next? Is this character believable? Is this paragraph any good? Should I keep going or start over? The desert is where drafts go to die.

Now there is a new traveler for that desert: an AI writing assistant. It is tireless, fast, endlessly patient with your questions, and ready to brainstorm at three in the morning. It is also confident when it should be unsure, generic when you need it to be strange, and — if you hand it the wheel — capable of driving your story straight off the map into a swamp of bland, agreeable prose that sounds like nobody.

This book is about crossing that desert with a partner you can actually trust — because you built the trust yourself, prompt by prompt, decision by decision.

### Who this book is for

This book is for beginners. Specifically:

- **You have never finished a story** and want to, with or without technology.
- **You have written some fiction** but feel stuck at the middle, the ending, or the revision stage, and you have heard that AI might help — you want to know *how*, honestly.
- **You already use an AI assistant** for writing and feel the results are… fine. You suspect you are leaving most of its value on the table, and that some of what it gives you is making your writing worse.

You do not need any prior knowledge of writing craft or of how AI works. Jargon is defined the first time it appears. You do not need expensive tools: everything in this book is written to work with a generic AI chat assistant — whichever one you already have access to.

### What this book will not do

Let's be blunt about the promises this book refuses to make.

**This is not a "get rich with AI" book.** There is no chapter about churning out fifty novels a month. That approach treats writing as a content-faucet problem, and readers — the only people who ever buy books — can smell it. If your goal is to produce text at industrial speed with no care for the reader, this book will genuinely bore you.

**This is not a replacement for reading.** A writer who does not read widely is a cook who does not taste. No tool substitutes for the thousands of hours of reading that quietly teach you what a good paragraph feels like in the body. If anything, this book will ask you to read *more*, and to read more deliberately.

**This is not a shortcut around skill.** AI collapses some drudgery and expands some options. It does not collapse the need for taste, judgment, or practice. The writers who benefit most from AI assistance are, as we will see in Chapter 1, the ones who already know what they want — and the ones who use the tool to *learn faster*, not to skip learning.

**This is not a legal treatise.** Chapter 15 covers copyright, disclosure, and contracts at the level a working writer needs, with sources and dates — and it says "check the current rule" and "consult a lawyer" exactly where those phrases are honest.

### Why this book exists

Two gaps meet in the middle. Craft books teach story structure beautifully, but they predate the tool you now have open in the next tab. AI books teach prompting, but they treat writing as text generation — as if a story were a formatting problem. Nobody was standing in the seam, teaching *both*, with the craft first and the machine second.

So that is the architecture of this book. Part I makes you dangerous with fundamentals — premise, stakes, character, scene — using no AI at all, because you cannot judge a machine's suggestions if you cannot judge your own. Part II demystifies the machine and gives you prompt craft and a real project setup. Part III walks one complete workflow: idea to outline to draft to revision, with every prompt shown, every transcript annotated, and every failure named. Part IV protects what you built: your voice, your ethics, your rights, and your next twelve months.

### What is actually new here

Every generation of writers gets a new instrument and a new panic. The typewriter was going to kill literature; so was pulp paper; so were paperbacks, television, word processors, spell-check, and the internet. In each case the instrument changed the *economics* of writing — who could produce, how fast, at what cost — and left the *craft* stubbornly intact. A fast bad paragraph is still a bad paragraph. A fast paragraph in service of a clear dramatic aim is just a paragraph that arrived sooner.

Large language models are a bigger jump than spell-check, and honesty requires saying so: the instrument can now propose sentences, not merely flag their spelling. That is new. What is *not* new is the shape of the problem on the other side of the sentence. A story still needs a protagonist who wants something and pays for wanting it. Scenes still need conflict. Characters still need to want and need different things. Endings still have to be earned. None of that moved. The craft aisle of your bookstore is, if anything, more true now than in 2015 — and that is exactly why this book puts four chapters of it *before* any prompting instruction.

What is genuinely new is the loop available to a beginner alone in a room. Previously, the only way to get rapid feedback was other people: a critique partner with a day job, a group that meets monthly, an MFA you may not be able to afford. The loop has compressed from weeks to seconds. That compression is the opportunity — and the trap. Seconds-fast feedback tempts you to skip the part where you form an opinion first. One of the recurring disciplines of this book is the pause before the prompt: *what do I think is wrong here?* Ask the machine only after you have a hypothesis, and the collaboration becomes a duel between two readings instead of a surrender to one.

There is a second thing that is new, and this book treats it as a first-class concern: at scale, lazy use of these tools produces a recognizable sound — the hedged, thesaurus-flourished, rhythmically flat voice that readers have already learned to distrust. Voice is no longer just a virtue; it is a differentiator. Chapters 14 and 15 exist because a writer in 2026 needs to protect their sound and their rights with the same seriousness they once needed only for their plot. We will get there. First, the fundamentals — because a tool in the hands of someone who cannot yet judge its output is just a faster way to get lost.

### What this book assumes about your tools

Concretely, you need three things, and none of them are expensive:

1. **A place to write.** Any word processor with decent version history. Plain text files in a folder work beautifully; Chapter 7 shows the folder anatomy used throughout the book. This is where your manuscript lives — never only inside an AI chat window.
2. **An AI assistant with a chat interface.** Any mainstream large language model behind a chat box will do: the ones bundled with office suites, the standalone research assistants, the models you access through developer platforms with a simple chat front-end. This book is deliberately *model-generic*: prompts are written in plain language, without brand-specific syntax, because brand-specific tricks expire every few months and books do not. When a technique depends on a specific capability, it carries a dated test note, and Appendix F explains the re-test habit.
3. **A folder for this book's materials.** Your baselines, decisions file, prompt cards, and exercise answers. Treat it like lab notes; you will accumulate more judgment in that folder than in any highlighter-colored paperback.

Optional and useful: a timer (the Try exercises use real minutes, not vibes), and one human you trust for the feedback round in Chapter 13 — AI-simulated readers are a pre-flight check, never a replacement for that person.

You do *not* need: programming skills, a paid subscription to seventeen tools, an art-history degree, or prior finished manuscripts. You do not need to know what a token is before Chapter 5 explains it. You do not even need to be sure AI belongs in your process — half this book's readers arrive skeptical, and skepticism, kept honest, is excellent editorial equipment.

### How to read the honesty markers

This book makes promises it can keep and declines the ones it cannot. So you will notice a deliberate texture in the prose: claims arrive with hedges that are *doing work* — "often," "in our tests," "on current mainstream models," "studies of short writing tasks found" — and when a figure appears, it appears with its population, its units, and its date, or it does not appear at all. Every factual claim with consequence is registered in the public claim register behind Appendix E, with its confidence level and re-verification date. That may look like fussiness. It is the same standard we ask of you in Chapter 15 when you paste a venue's disclosure policy into your submission notes instead of assuming.

Two categories of marker deserve a word now:

- **REALITY CHECK** boxes state where the tool falls short — not to scare you, but because a limitation you can name is a limitation you can design around. Boxes marked REALITY CHECK are the paragraphs most worth photographing; they are where the book refuses its own marketing.
- **ETHICS NOTE** boxes appear where a practice touches other people's rights, privacy, or trust — mostly in Chapters 13 and 15. When you see one, slow down; those are the places where a beginner's innocent shortcut becomes someone else's problem.

If a passage of this book ever sounds like a product launch, it is a bug. Tell it to your decisions file and keep reading.

### How to disagree with this book (please do)

A manual that cannot be argued with is a catechism. You will meet advice here you dislike: the voice-anchor discipline will feel paranoid the first week; the decisions file will feel bureaucratic; the refusal to promise shortcuts will feel like false modesty from a book that uses the tools itself. Disagree actively — and use the book's own method while you do. When a recommendation rubs, do these three things in order. **First, run it as written for one session.** Untested dislike is preference; tested dislike is data, and you have just produced your first row of counter-evidence. **Second, locate the load-bearing claim.** Under most recommendations is one testable proposition ("anchoring prevents voice erosion," "unaided baselines make drift measurable") — name it, and judge that rather than the packaging. **Third, log the dissent in your decisions file with one line of reasoning.** If you later reverse yourself, the log shows you why; if you don't, you have begun the personal style of argument that every good editorial room runs on.

This is also how the book wants you to treat *it* as a source: clearly, checkably, without deference. Appendix E exposes the research behind the factual claims precisely so the disagreement can go all the way down to the evidence. Books about judgment should not ask for faith.

### A note on how this book was made

We teach transparency, so we practice it. This manuscript was produced with substantial AI assistance — outlining, drafting support, structural critique, and consistency checks — under a logged human review process, and the full log is published as Appendix F. The craft arguments, the running examples, the adjudications of every AI suggestion, and the final wording are human decisions. Where we cite research, the claim is registered in a public claim register with its source and confidence level (Appendix E). Where a fact was too fragile to verify, it was cut or hedged rather than printed with confidence.

That process is itself a demonstration of the book's central rule, which you will meet properly in Chapter 1. For now, in one line:

> **You decide. AI proposes.**

## How to Use This Book

This is a practical manual, not a manifesto. It is built to be *used* — written in, argued with, and referenced with a highlighter. This section explains the machinery: the three reading paths, the exercise tiers, the prompt cards, and the callout boxes you will see on nearly every page.

### The three reading paths

**Path 1 — Straight through (recommended if you are new to both fiction and AI).**
Front matter → Chapter 1 → Chapter 2 → … → Chapter 16. This is the designed path. Part I builds your craft baseline without AI so that later chapters have something to preserve; Part II explains the tool before you rely on it; Part III is the full workflow; Part IV is voice, law, and career. If you skip Part I "because you already use AI," you will spend Part III accepting mediocre suggestions you lack the vocabulary to reject. Budget honest time: readers who do every Try exercise typically take six to ten weeks.

**Path 2 — Craft-first.**
Read Part I (Chapters 1–4), write the unaided short story that Part I culminates in, *then* continue with Part II onward. Choose this path if you are skeptical about AI — or if you have been burned by AI-dulled prose before. The unaided baseline you produce in Part I becomes the control group for every experiment that follows; Chapter 14 will have you compare against it directly. Your skepticism is welcome; this book's job is to convert it into judgment.

**Path 3 — AI-first (reference use).**
If you already write and only want the tool layer: read Chapter 5 (how the model works — it will save you from confident nonsense), Chapter 6 (prompt craft), and Chapter 7 (project setup), then dive into whichever workflow chapter matches your current stage — Chapter 8 for ideas, Chapter 9 for outlining, Chapter 10 for drafting, Chapter 12 for revision. Keep Appendix A (the prompt library) and Appendix C (troubleshooting) open in a second window. Come back to Part I the first time a suggestion from the machine makes your story worse and you are not sure why.

All three paths end in the same place: a finished, revised, *yours* story — and a practice you can sustain.

### How the exercises work

Every chapter ends with three exercise tiers. They are not decoration; the tiers map to how skill actually builds.

| Tier | Time | Purpose |
|------|------|---------|
| **Try** | ~15 minutes | A rep. Small enough that you do it today, not "later." |
| **Stretch** | 1–2 hours | A workout. Deepens the chapter's skill on real material. |
| **Project** | Ongoing | Your actual story. Each Project moves the manuscript forward. |

Sample solutions for selected Try and Stretch exercises, plus extra drills, are in Appendix B — but only *after* you attempt them. The keys are sample responses, not answer keys; writing is not multiple choice. The Project tier is never keyed, because it builds your book, not ours.

A realistic week, for readers who worry the tiers won't fit around a job: Monday, the Try exercise of Chapter 1 (fifteen minutes at a kitchen table); Tuesday and Wednesday, one drafting session each on the Project's 200-word baseline; Thursday, the Stretch's first half; Friday, its second half plus the four-line reflection. That is under five hours to complete a full chapter's practical layer — and it produces artifacts (baseline, revision note, decisions rows) that every later chapter reuses. Readers who skip the practical layer don't save time; they re-read Chapters 8 through 13 later without ammunition. The tiers are sized to be finishable precisely so that they get finished.

> TRY IT — The habit starts now: do the first Try exercise of Chapter 1 before you read Chapter 2. Readers who complete the first week of Try exercises finish at dramatically higher rates than readers who "read the theory first." (That last sentence is a hunch, not a study — hold it as motivation, not evidence. This book marks the difference between data and hunch wherever it matters.)

### How prompt cards work

Reusable prompts appear in fenced code blocks marked as **prompt cards**, each with an ID:

> PROMPT CARD PC-07 — What-if ladder
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-07 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic, no model-specific syntax -->
> You are a story ideation partner...
> ```

- **IDs are stable.** PC-01 is the scene drafter forever; you can write "try PC-01 but with crime-fiction constraints" in your notes and mean the same thing we do.
- **The comment line is real metadata.** `tested 2026-09` means the card was run against a mainstream assistant that month. Cards are written to be *model-generic*: no brand tricks, no secret syntax. When a card depends on a capability that changes with model updates, re-test it before trusting it (Appendix F explains the re-test habit).
- **All cards are collected in Appendix A** — one per entry, with "when to use," knobs to adjust, and cross-references. Chapters introduce cards in context; the appendix is your workshop drawer.
- **Cards are starting points, not spells.** Chapter 6 teaches you to debug and modify them. A prompt you cannot adjust is a prompt you do not own.

Here is the difference a card makes, shown once so the format is familiar. A beginner's prompt:

```text
Write a scene where two sisters argue about selling their father's shop.
```

The same intent, card-shaped (this is a reduced glimpse of the scene drafter you will build as PC-01):

```text
<!-- PC-01 (reduced example) · v1 · tested 2026-09 -->
Role: you are drafting fiction for me to revise.
Context: two sisters, 34 and 29, in their late father's repair shop the
night before the auction. Older sister wants to sell; younger has been
running the shop for a year and feels erased. Close third, past tense,
from the younger sister's POV.
Task: draft the argument scene, 400-500 words. End on a line of action,
not a reconciliation.
Constraints: no exposition paragraphs; each speech tag carries an action
or a physical beat; no one says "honesty" or "closure"; the older sister
is right about the money and wrong about the people.
Output: the scene only — no preamble, no title.
```

You can feel the difference without knowing anything about prompting: the second version names the POV, the stakes, the ending shape, the banned words, and the output format — and every one of those lines came from *someone's decision about the story*. That is the skill Chapter 6 installs, and by Chapter 10 you will be writing cards of your own.

### The callout boxes

Seven boxes appear throughout the book. You will learn them by page 50; here is the field guide:

- **TRY IT** — a small action to do right now. Do it; the book is written on the assumption that you do.
- **PROMPT CARD (PC-XX)** — a reusable, copy-paste prompt.
- **PITFALL** — a common failure, its symptom, and its fix. Pitfalls are cross-referenced to Appendix C, the troubleshooting guide, so you can diagnose by symptom when a draft goes wrong at midnight.
- **CRAFT NOTE** — a pure writing-craft aside. Everything in a Craft Note works with or without AI; it is the part of this book that will still be true in twenty years.
- **REALITY CHECK** — where the tool genuinely struggles. No doom, no hype — just the shape of the limitation, stated plainly.
- **ETHICS NOTE** — used sparingly (mainly Chapters 13 and 15) where a practice touches other people's rights or trust.
- **CHECKLIST** — the end-of-chapter readiness check. "Ready to move on" means ready, not perfect.

### What the transcripts are (and are not)

Where the book shows the AI assistant in action, you are reading an *annotated transcript*: the prompt, an abridged response (marked with `[...]` where cut), and two to five lines of commentary explaining why it worked or failed. Raw, uncommented AI output is never printed as an example to imitate — because uncommented output teaches nothing except "the machine said words." The commentary is the curriculum. The transcripts are abridged for space; abridgments never remove the failure being illustrated.

A minimal specimen, so you can see the three parts work:

> **Prompt:** Name three settings for a breakup scene that are not "rainy bus stop," "airport," or "kitchen." Make each setting force a different ending.
>
> **Assistant (abridged):** 1. A ferry deck mid-crossing — no exit until dock; forces a held-in eruption. 2. A hospital waiting room during someone else's scare — forces postponement and dark humor. 3. A shared pottery studio with a kiln cycle to collect — forces cooperation with objects in hand. [...]
>
> **Commentary:** The exclusions killed the three defaults; the forcing clause ("different ending") turned a location list into a structure list — that constraint is the reusable part, and it graduates into PC-05. What it cannot judge: which ending serves *your* characters. That selection is still yours.

Three habits make transcripts work for you rather than at you. **Never paste output into your draft unopened** — read it as a proposal, the way you would read a critique from someone who writes well but has never met your story. **Keep the commentary habit from day one**: two lines of *why* under anything you save, because six weeks later you will not remember why version B survived. **Abridge brutally when you take notes** — the goal of your notes file is decision, not archive; the chat log already exists if you ever need the archaeology.

### What success looks like

Six weeks after finishing, the book will have succeeded if the following are true — and none of them are "you produced a lot of text fast." You can state a premise and defend its stakes sentence from memory. You have an unaided baseline *and* a revised piece, with a written comparison of what changed between them. You own four or five prompt cards you have actually tested and edited, and you can say what each one's knobs do. Your project folder contains a real draft with numbered versions and a changelog, a story bible, and a decisions file with more rejections in it than acceptances — the healthiest ratio in this book. You have run one structured revision pass with logs instead of free-floating tinkering. You know where to find any venue's AI-disclosure policy and what your own disclosure sentence says. And you have a 90-day plan with week 1 already scheduled.

That list is deliberately unglamorous. Text volume is the one metric this book never celebrates, because volume is what the tool gives for free and what the market increasingly drowns in. Judgment, structure, voice, and finish — the things that make a reader trust your name — are what the exercises are buying. Keep the list; check items off in the margins.

### Conventions for examples

Two running examples carry Part III so techniques are never demonstrated on abstract sludge:

- **Example A — "Low Tide,"** a literary-contemporary short story, developed from raw premise to revised draft across Chapters 8–13. You will watch the same story get ideated, outlined, drafted, populated with characters, and cut back to bone. The examples build on each other in order — Chapter 10's scene comes from Chapter 9's outline, which comes from Chapter 8's premise.
- **Example B — genre scenes,** short self-contained demonstrations (a detective-story interrogation; a science-fiction station emergency) used where a technique needs a different register: dialogue voice, action, worldbuilding.

Craft terms are defined on first use and used consistently afterward; Appendix D is the glossary when you need a term again cold.

### A word on the exercises' relationship to AI

In Part I, the exercises are deliberately unaided. Do them with the tool closed. The point is to install the skill in *your* hands first. From Part III on, prompts and exercises increasingly invite the assistant in — but every stage still expects your adjudication. If you remember nothing else from this preface, remember the shape of that arrangement, because the whole book hangs on it:

> **You decide. AI proposes.**

Now turn the page. Chapter 1 is about why this arrangement — and not the fantasy of a ghostwriter — is the one that actually works.
