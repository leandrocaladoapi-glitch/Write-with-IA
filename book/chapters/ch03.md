## Opening hook

Here are two versions of the same character. Both have a flaw. Only one has a story.

**Version one:** Detective Ray Ferrara is *impatient* — he interrupts people, taps his pen, hates waiting. The flaw appears on every page: barked replies, a ticking watch. At the climax he... is still impatient. He solves the case with a burst of impatient brilliance. The reader closes the book unchanged, because a flaw that never *costs* the character anything is not a flaw; it is a personality condiment, shaken on for texture.

**Version two:** Ray cannot admit he needs anyone — the same energy as impatience, aimed somewhere lethal. Because he won't ask his old partner for the sealed file, the wrong man walks. Because he won't tell his daughter why the retirement dinner matters, she doesn't come. Because he won't admit the "Rook" years were his choice and not just the job, the killer he hunts is the identity he protects. At the climax, the flaw *is* the obstacle: the case is solvable only if Ray does the one thing his flaw forbids — say *I need this from you*, on tape, in front of everyone. The flaw has grown teeth, connected to plot like rebar to concrete, and the story ends differently because of it.

The difference between the versions is not depth of psychology; it is **causality**. Version two's flaw runs a chain: flaw → choices → consequences → climax. That chain is this chapter's first craft target. Around it we build the other two pillars of character fundamentals: the **want/need split** (what the character pursues versus what would actually save them) and the **arc** (how the ordeal changes them — or fails to). By the end you will fill a character sheet that already contains the skeleton of your plot, because with real characters, structure is what you get for free.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- [ ] Separate a character's want from their need.
- [ ] Give a character a flaw that causally connects to the climax (the chain test).
- [ ] Choose an arc type — positive, negative, or flat — deliberately.
- [ ] Fill a character sheet for your protagonist, with voice notes.

## Want vs. need

The want/need split is the most portable idea in modern character craft: most published formulations of it descend from the same mid-century screenwriting and writing-teaching lineage, and its core claim is stable across schools — **characters pursue what they think will fix them and require what would actually fix them, and the gap between the two generates plot.** Call it consensus-with-variations; you do not need the lineage, you need the working definitions.

**The want** is external, concrete, and verifiable on the page: the case closed, the house sold, the funding won, the ticket to leave town. Wants are what characters *do*. They give your story direction (Chapter 2's goal field) and they are how readers track progress: every scene can be checked against "is the protagonist closer to the want?"

**The need** is internal and almost always the want's opposite or its price: Ray needs to need people *as he* wants to close the case without needing anyone. Mara (Example A) wants to sell the house and leave — her need is to *stay long enough to be present* for her brother. The need is what the character cannot give themselves in Chapter 1 by declaration; it has to be extracted by events, usually at the climax's cost.

**Three tests to keep the split honest:**

1. *The one-line test.* Can you state want and need in one line each, without using the same verb? "Wants to sell the house; needs to keep it" passes only if you can say what the keeping *means* (presence, roots, repair of the abandonment). "Wants to sell; needs closure" fails — "closure" is fog.
2. *The plot test.* Does the want drive scenes (she calls the notary, she packs boxes) while the need is violated by her behavior in those very scenes (each packed box is another refusal to sit with Theo)? If want and need move together without friction, you have two wants.
3. *The climax test.* At the climax, does the character have to *choose* between want-satisfaction and need-satisfaction — or pay the want to gain the need? If the climax lets them have both at no cost, the split was decoration. (Bittersweet and costly endings fall straight out of this test; so do the great "wins the battle, loses the war" reversals.)

> CRAFT NOTE — **Without therapy-speak.** Character craft borrows psychology's vocabulary constantly, and beginner pages fill with characters who are "in denial," "processing trauma," "avoidant-attachment," "finally healing." Two cautions. First, clinical labels *summarize*; fiction must *dramatize*: we don't need Ray diagnosed as avoidant, we need him not making the phone call while the phone sits on the table. Second, labels invite symmetry — every flaw tidied into a category — and tidy categories are where originality goes to die. Let your characters be *specifically* odd: not "controlling" but the woman who alphabetizes the spice rack and then lies about small things to avoid arguments. Write the behavior; let the reader do the diagnosing. If your draft's character notes read like a intake form, delete three adjectives and add two actions.

**Wants and needs across types.** In genre engines, the want often *is* the plot (find the killer, win the tournament) while the need supplies the theme (learn to trust a partner again; decide what winning costs). In literary work, the inversion is common: the want is modest and plausible (sell the house; deliver the meal) while the need carries the weight (forgive, stay, confess). Neither arrangement is superior; what fails in both is *absence* — a protagonist with only a want is a machine for errands, and one with only a need is a mood waiting for a plot to happen to.

### Three splits, taken apart

Abstract tests stick better when run on live material, so here are three complete want/need splits — one genre, one literary, one from Example B's science-fiction register — each annotated for what makes the friction productive.

**Split 1 — Ray Ferrara (detective, negative-arc potential):** *Want:* close one clean case before forced retirement. *Need:* admit the "Rook" years were chosen, not imposed — ask for help without managing the ask. Friction analysis: the want's methods (solo legwork, controlled information, no debts) are precisely the need's violations; every scene that advances the case can therefore *simultaneously* deepen the wound — the plot and the character problem share one engine, which is why the structure will feel inevitable rather than bolted. Climax test preview: solvable only by the public admission the flaw forbids.

**Split 2 — Mara Solano (Example A, literary):** *Want:* sell the house in two weeks. *Need:* stay long enough to grieve alongside Theo. Friction analysis: her want's successful execution *is* the need's failure — every box packed is an absence made physical — so the story cannot merely stall her; it must make the logistics themselves suspect. The November schedules, the contract deadlines, the survey posting: her instruments become temptations. Note the inversion quality — the two pulls point opposite *in time* (leave versus remain), which is why the storm (a machine for trapping people in place) functions as theme, not just weather.

**Split 3 — Dr. Leila Haddad (Example B, science-fiction station story):** *Want:* keep the botany bay's failing oxygen loop online until the relief shuttle docks. *Need:* accept command responsibility — stop delegating the hard calls to protocol so her crew stops dying by the book's fine print. Friction analysis: the genre want is a countdown (excellent external clock); the need is an identity shift from *scientist who follows procedures* to *commander who amends them*. Each life-extension hack she approves cuts against her old self-image, so engineering decisions double as self-revisions — worldbuilding and character advancing on the same wire, which is the trick good SF does and padded SF forgets.

Common to all three: the need cannot be *declared* into satisfaction ("I've learned to trust!" over the credits); it gets paid at the climax in currency the want understood all along (the signature, the keys, the counter-protocol order). When you draft your own split, write these three columns — want, need, *what the want's success costs the need* — and if the third column comes out empty, the split is fake; go back to field 3 of your sheet.

> TRY IT — Pick three characters from stories you know cold (a film, a novel, a series). One line each: external want, internal need, and one scene where the two pull opposite. Fifteen minutes. The third column is the one that trains you — it is where you learn to *see* the split in finished work, which is the only way to reliably build it in your own.

## Flaw and wound

Now the chapter's load-bearing idea: the flaw chain. A **flaw** (also called a fault or weakness — the terms interconvert) is a persistent pattern of self-defeating behavior. A **wound** is the history that made the pattern adaptive once. The chain connects both to your climax:

**flaw → choices → consequences → climax**

Work it in order.

**1. Flaw as pattern, not label.** "Stubborn" is a label. The pattern is: *when contradicted, Mara doubles down on logistics — she responds to emotional appeals by producing schedules.* Patterns are writable because they predict behavior: you now know what she does at the notary's office, at the dinner table, at 11 p.m. when Theo won't answer. If your flaw cannot predict a specific behavior under pressure, it is still a label; keep drilling until it generates *doings*.

**2. Choices: give the flaw jobs.** The flaw must cause decisions a wiser character would not make — at least three across the story, escalating. Mara refuses Theo's vague counteroffer and hires the surveyor anyway (choice 1); she lets Casi accelerate the paperwork timeline instead of asking Theo what the house *means* (choice 2); she schedules her departure for the storm's peak because the ship won't wait (choice 3). Each choice is *defensible in the moment* — the flaw works by making the wrong move feel like the responsible one. That disguise is what separates character-driven from merely stupid.

**3. Consequences: the world answers back.** Every choice must cost something real: relationship equity, information, options, time. If consequences come only from *other* characters' flaws, your protagonist is a pinball. Mara's refusals cost: Theo stops telling her things (information loss); the buyer's timeline tightens (option loss); the storm finds her on the road (time made literal). By midpoint, the reader should be able to trace a clean causal line from who she is to how trapped she is — that line *is* the plot, which is why we promised at the chapter's start that good characters generate structure.

**4. Climax: the flaw faces its bill.** The climax puts the character where the flaw's methods definitively fail — where schedules cannot save her, where "manage it alone" leads to the worst outcome — and forces the choice between the old pattern and its alternative. Ray's climax demands the admission his flaw forbids. If your climax can be won *by* the flaw (patience solved by impatience, apparently), your chain has a short in it: go back to step 3 and sharpen the costs.

**Wound, carefully.** The wound explains the pattern's origin: Mara's years of managing their mother's dementia alone, then the last phone calls she stopped returning — the logistics shell *worked*, once; it got her through. Wounds supply sympathy and causality, and they should be **dosed, not dumped**: revealed under pressure, late enough to recontextualize behavior we've already seen, early enough to matter before the climax. Backload them emotionally, but schedule them structurally — put the wound beats in your outline (Chapter 9). Two dosing warnings: wounds do not *excuse* (a wound explains why the flaw exists; it does not void the consequences that make the story a story), and wounds do not *require* tragedy Olympics (a quieter wound — the promotion she took instead of the apprenticeship with her father — can run a whole novel if the pattern is sharp).

### The chain clinic: four drafts, diagnosed

Because the chain test is this chapter's pass/fail skill, run it on four specimen chains — two that fail in instructive ways, two that pass — before you build your own.

**Chain A (fails — label never became pattern):** *Flaw: jealous. Choices: she glares at the rival; she checks the rival's messages; she sulks at the party. Consequences: people notice. Climax: she wins anyway.* Diagnosis: the "choices" are symptoms, not decisions with arguments behind them; none costs *her* anything the plot requires; the climax doesn't need her to outgrow or surrender anything — the flaw could be present or absent with the same ending. Repair: make each choice *defensible* (checking the messages recovers evidence the plot needs — she is proven right once, which is how the pattern stays seductive), then design a climax where the jealous method destroys the very proof it sought.

**Chain B (fails — wound doing the flaw's job):** *Flaw: closed-off. Wound: father's abandonment. Choices: none listed — "he pushes people away" appears as narration between plot events.* Diagnosis: the flaw is reported in summaries and never *issues orders*; the wound is doing double duty as explanation *and* as excuse, so the character never actively chooses the wrong move. Repair: convert the narration into three staged refusals (he doesn't call the ally; he pays the ferry alone and misses the deadline; he lies about the package's contents "to keep it clean"), each with a bill. The wound then returns to its proper job: seasoning the refusals, not replacing them.

**Chain C (passes — Example A, compressed):** *Pattern: emotion → logistics. Choices: surveyor hired before telling Theo (cost: trust) → contract timeline accelerated (cost: honesty + leverage) → departure scheduled mid-storm (cost: safety, and the fight arrives early). Climax: forced to sign or refuse *with Theo watching*, where the pen-stroke — her signature instrument — can either betray him or finally serve them.* Passes every gate: pattern predicts behavior; choices disguise the flaw as responsibility; costs compound into the midpoint's trap; the climax weaponizes her own instrument. Note also the reparative symmetry — the same pen that logged her absences is what must now choose — chains earn their endings by *rhyming* with their beginnings.

**Chain D (passes — negative arc):** *Pattern: outsources moral calls to "the job." Choices: signs the raid warrant knowing the informant is unreliable (cost: a friendship) → withholds the exculpatory memo (cost: his own sleep, managed with whiskey) → testifies to the gapless version (cost: the last colleague's trust). Climax: the gapless version holds — he keeps the position — and the closing image shows him checking the door twice.* Diagnosis: each choice *upgrades* the surrender (that's the negative arc's signature: escalation by precedent, not by pressure alone); the climax's "win" is the fall's invoice. The twice-checked door is the arc's residue — change that isn't a speech.

Diagnose your own chain against C's rubric: defensible choices? compounding costs? climax that fails the *flaw's* methods? rhyming ending? Two failing links is a rewrite of the sheet — which is a rewrite of pages — which is exactly the cheap-now, expensive-later law from Chapter 2, now installed on the character side.

**Flat vs. round (working definitions).** From the long critical tradition (E. M. Forster's distinction is the famous ancestor, endlessly re-taught): a **flat** character is built around one or two consistent ideas and can be stated in a sentence ("the notary who will not deviate from procedure"); a **round** character contradicts themselves, contains conflict between traits, and resists one-sentence summary. The useful correction to the usual hierarchy: **flat characters are not failures.** Stories need both — flat characters are the load-bearing walls of clarity (Casi Duarte's single-mindedness makes Mara's complexity legible by contrast); round characters are where theme gets debated. Protagonists and their mirrors should be round; the cast *around* them can profitably stay flat. Beginners' common error is making every minor character "surprisingly deep," which is actually flatness by other means: the *same* depth, everywhere, always kind, always witty — a failure we will meet again, with AI's help, in Chapter 11.

## Arcs: positive, negative, flat

An **arc** is the story's answer to: *does the character change, and in which direction?* The taxonomy in most modern craft teaching is a triad, and knowing which one you are writing prevents the most expensive late-stage disappointment in fiction — finishing a draft that secretly wanted to be a different book.

**Positive arc (mastery first).** The character starts with a false belief or dysfunctional pattern (the flaw's rationalization: *handling everything alone is how I protect people*), suffers escalating costs from acting on it, and at the climax *chooses* the truth instead (*I stay, and staying is not losing*). The want usually gets renegotiated in the process: Mara's sale becomes a shared decision or a delayed one, and the survey posting — the symbol of escape — becomes something she can take *without* fleeing. The satisfying shape is not "flaw erased" but "flaw outvoted at the decisive moment, under pressure, at a price." Beliefs change slower than decisions; one climactic choice is the beginning of the change, not its completion — sequels start from there.

**Negative arc (the fall).** The character adopts or surrenders to the lie: Michael Corleone's consolidation of the family business; every tragedy's protagonist who trades the last ethical card for the want. The negative arc delivers coherence, not comfort — the ending *makes sense*, and the sense it makes is a warning. Beginner guidance: negative arcs need even tighter plotting than positive ones, because the reader must *want* the character to stop while watching them not stop; if your beta readers think the fall is glamorous, the costs are underpaid.

**Flat arc (the world changes).** The protagonist's core holds; the *situation or surrounding cast* transforms around them. Many parables, detective stories, and hard-SF stories run flat protagonists against evolving worlds; the drama comes from what the steadfast stance *extracts* from the environment. Flat is not static: a flat arc story still needs escalation, revelation, and a climax where the held value is tested at maximum — the test confirms rather than revises, and readers who came for that confirmation get paid.

**Choosing deliberately.** Match arc to engine: want/need friction with a costly climax → positive; theme of corruption or temptation → negative; theme of principle under erosion → flat. The beginner default — and this book's recommendation — is **master the positive arc first**: its machinery (false belief → costs → choice) is the triad's spine, and the other two are variations once the spine works. Whatever you choose, write it in the skeleton's field 3 area now: "Arc: positive; false belief: ___; climactic choice: ___." When Chapter 12 asks whether your ending delivered, that line is half the invoice (the stakes sentence from Chapter 2 is the other half).

> REALITY CHECK — Arc is not automatically *therapy*. Stories change people in stories faster than life changes people in life, and a novel's arc can compress years of inner movement into a weekend — that compression is legitimate dramatization, not a claim about psychology. What arc is *not* negotiable on is causality: no arc without the chain. A character who "learns a lesson" the plot never forced them to is reading a fortune cookie, not completing an arc.

### Antagonists, foils, and the cut-down cast sheet

Before the sheet, one cast-shaping rule that saves beginners whole drafts of symmetric over-building: **depth is budgeted, not moral.** Your protagonist gets the full twelve fields; the character who *opposes* or *mirrors* them meaningfully (Theo, Casi, the rival detective) gets eight; everyone else — the notary, the ferry attendant, the mother's neighbor — gets the cut-down four (identity, want, flaw-as-pattern or one governing trait, contrast role). Flatness in the supporting cast is not laziness; it is *contrast architecture*, the principle noted above now applied as staffing: roundness everywhere flattens the effect by spending it everywhere.

The **foil** deserves its own sentence because it does half of theme's work for free: a foil is a character who made a neighboring choice under similar pressures — Theo stayed, Mara left; Casi buys the houses his father lost; the partner who didn't take the bribe Ray took. Place foils so their *want/need splits invert* across from your protagonist's, and your scenes acquire argument structure: the same question ("what do you owe the living?") tried in two laboratories. The cut-down sheet's field 4 ("contrast role") is where you state that inversion in one line — if you cannot, the character is currently a stranger to the theme, and either give them a function or let them go nameless.

One casting warning that Part III will revisit with the machine in the room: **do not let the cast's job descriptions do the wanting.** "The notary wants the paperwork filed" is an institutional function, not a desire with a cost — institutions want things by *hiring people who want them*. Give your flat characters one private want visible only in a single beat (the notary who files everything correctly *and* keeps a snapshot of someone else's holiday in his drawer), and they will hum like a struck bell for one line of prose without demanding Chapter 11's full treatment.

## The character sheet

Time to fix everything above in a reusable document. The sheet is the character-side twin of Chapter 2's skeleton: premise-level, one to two pages, living in your project folder (Chapter 7's story bible absorbs it), and required for your protagonist before Part III. Supporting cast gets a cut-down version (four fields max).

```text
CHARACTER SHEET — [name]                                   v[__]

1. IDENTITY: name, age, occupation, one physical tell that shows
   under stress.
2. DEFINING SITUATION: what ordinary life looks like the week
   before the story.
3. WANT (external, checkable): one line.
4. NEED (internal): one line — must differ in verb from the want.
5. FLAW AS PATTERN: the self-defeating behavior *as it acts*
   ("responds to emotion with schedules"), not a label.
6. WOUND (dosed, not dumped): the history that made the pattern
   adaptive. One sentence now; reveal beats go in the outline.
7. FALSE BELIEF (rationalization the flaw tells itself): one line.
8. ARC TYPE: positive | negative | flat — plus the climactic
   choice in one line.
9. CHAIN: three choices the flaw will cause (beginning / middle /
   late), each with its cost.
10. VOICE: diction level (formal ↔ crude), typical sentence
    length, one verbal habit (interruptions? full stops? weather
    metaphors?), one thing they never say.
11. RELATIONSHIPS: the load-bearing relationship and what both
    sides want from it.
12. CONTRAST ROLE: which cast member they mirror or foil, one line.
```

**Voice, in brief** — field 10 deserves a paragraph because dialogue differentiation is where sheets pay off fastest (Chapter 11 runs the full program). Start with three levers anyone can control: **diction** (does this character say "insufficient" or "not enough"?), **syntax** (long缠 clauses or clipped stops?), and **rhythm under stress** (talk faster in short sentences, or go quiet and long?). Add one *habit* — interrupts, qualifies ("sort of"), answers questions with questions — and one *never* (no apologies, no first names, no swearing). That is enough to make two characters distinguishable in a blind test, which is exactly the test Chapter 11 will run.

## Worked example: Example A's protagonist sheet

Here is Mara Solano's sheet, filled — the document Chapters 8 through 13 will carry like a passport, and the reference every AI-assisted character prompt in Part III must respect.

```text
CHARACTER SHEET — Mara Solano                              v1

1. IDENTITY: 36; hydrographic surveyor (compiles tide tables for a
   maritime authority); tell: straightens whatever's in reach —
   cutlery, charts, other people's schedules.
2. DEFINING SITUATION: successful city posting; promotion shortlist;
   tidily emptied apartment; nine months since her mother died,
   eleven since she last spoke to Theo.
3. WANT: sell the cliff house in Porto Alvaro in two weeks and make
   the survey posting that starts in six.
4. NEED: stay long enough to grieve WITH Theo — presence, not
   logistics.
5. FLAW AS PATTERN: answers emotional emergencies with operational
   plans; converts feelings into tasks that can be completed.
6. WOUND: she managed their mother's dementia from afar, then
   stopped answering calls; the last voicemail she didn't return
   came four days before the stroke. (Dose: journal beat, ch. ~;
   fight beat, midpoint.)
7. FALSE BELIEF: "Handling it alone is how I keep people from
   drowning — including me."
8. ARC: positive. Climactic choice: hands Theo the keys to KEEP —
   takes the posting WITH a return date — instead of forcing the
   sale and leaving the past administratively closed.
9. CHAIN: (a) hires the surveyor before telling Theo — cost:
   trust; (b) lets Casi accelerate the contract — cost: leverage
   and honesty; (c) schedules departure mid-storm — cost: safety
   + the fight she's been dodging arrives early.
10. VOICE: precise diction, meteorological metaphors under stress,
    medium sentences going clause-heavy when anxious; habit:
    converts questions into checklists; never: says "I'm scared."
11. RELATIONSHIPS: Theo — she wants his signature, he wants his
    sister; the house is both sides' proxy.
12. CONTRAST: Theo (feels first, acts slow) mirrors her (acts
    first, feels later); Casi foils her escape plan with a
    better-organized version of the same impulse.
```

**Annotated: which choices drive plot.** Field 9 *is* a three-act structure in disguise — the choices give Act I its trust fracture, Act II its acceleration, and the storm-noon deadline its Act III clock; from this sheet alone, Chapter 9 can hang beats without inventing anything. Field 5's pattern makes field 11's central conflict automatic: every scene with Theo is a scheduling-versus-feeling collision, so the *relationship* generates friction on demand — the payoff promised at the chapter's open. Field 6's dose plan (two beats, placed in the outline, not improvised in the draft) implements the wounding-as-schedule rule. And note what is absent: no horoscope, no childhood best-friend subplot, no inventory of favorites. The sheet is causal machinery, not biography — every field either causes choices or explains them. When you fill yours, cut any field you cannot connect to the chain with a one-line arrow. Archaeology for its own sake belongs in a different notebook.

> CRAFT NOTE — Fill sheets *before* Part III but expect them to change. A sheet that survives the draft untouched probably under-described the character; a sheet rewritten weekly probably isn't being read before scenes. The discipline is bidirectional: outline from the sheet, and feed discoveries back into it — one session per draft stage, changelog noted (Chapter 7's format).

## Exercises

- **Try (15 min):** Want/need for three characters you know well — one from a story, two from real life (yours included, handled gently). One line each for want and need, different verbs mandatory. Then circle any pair where you had to stretch to invent the need: in real people, needs are often invisible to everyone but the person — your instinct that it's *harder to see* is why fiction must build need into structure rather than trust it to leak.

- **Stretch (1–2 h):** Build the flaw-to-climax chain for your protagonist: write the flaw as a pattern sentence, then three choices it will cause (early / middle / late), each with a concrete cost, then the climax line where the flaw's methods fail and the alternative is chosen. Pressure-test each link with "and therefore?" — if two links connect with "and then" instead, the chain is an event list wearing a chain's clothes. Finish by filling fields 1–10 of your full character sheet (skip 11–12 if your cast doesn't exist yet; Chapter 11 supplies them).

- **Project (ongoing):** Complete the full sheet for your protagonist *and* the cut-down four-field version (identity, want, flaw-as-pattern, contrast role) for your antagonist or central opposition. Revisit both after your first draft chapter exists — the draft will have opinions. Track changes in the sheet with version numbers, the same discipline as your manuscript; the sheet is a living document, not a monument.

## Pitfalls

> PITFALL — **Decorative flaw.** *Symptom:* your notes describe a flaw impressively — proud, secretive, reckless — but no scene changes because of it; the climax could be won by anyone; beta readers shrug at the character's "complexity." *Why it happens:* flaws get written as profile features (they make people *interesting* in the abstract) instead of engines (they make people *wrong* in the specific). Also sneaks in via the wound: a tragic backstory that never issues orders in the present. *Fix:* run the chain test. List the flaw's three costed choices and the climax failure; if any link is missing, wire it now — a decorative flaw repaired is an outline rewritten, which is cheaper than a draft rewritten. *Prevention:* the character sheet's fields 5–9 are all chain fields; never mark the sheet complete with an empty climax line.

> PITFALL — **Therapy-speak substitution.** *Symptom:* characters are labels with dialogue ("my avoidant tendencies make me push people away" — spoken aloud, in the voice, by a fisherman); notes read like intake forms; change arrives by insight-declaration rather than event. *Fix:* delete the label, add an action and a cost — the same beat re-staged as behavior: Theo stops answering because every call becomes a logistics call. *Prevention:* the Craft Note's rule — write the *specifically odd* doing, let the reader diagnose; ban the eleven diagnostic words from your draft search (Chapter 12's line pass runs the search for you).

Related failures for Appendix C: flat-ensemble syndrome, every character kind and reasonable (feeds F-02); arc-plot mismatch discovered at revision (F-03's character-side face).

## Checklist

> CHECKLIST — You're ready to move on when:
> - [ ] Want and need are stated in one line each, with different verbs.
> - [ ] The flaw is written as a pattern (predictable behavior), not a label.
> - [ ] The chain test passes: three costed choices reach a climax where the flaw's methods fail.
> - [ ] Arc type is chosen on purpose, with the climactic choice named.
> - [ ] A completed character sheet exists for your protagonist — and its field 9 looks suspiciously like a plot.

## Key takeaways

- **Want versus need is friction, not trivia:** the want drives scenes; the need is violated by how the character pursues it; the climax forces the trade. Different verbs, or you have two wants.
- **The flaw chain is the chapter's spine:** flaw → costed choices → consequences → climax. A flaw that costs nothing is a condiment; decorate nothing — wire everything.
- **Dose the wound; don't dump it.** Backstory explains the pattern's origin; the present-tense pattern does the dramatizing. Schedule reveals in the outline, not by ambush in the draft.
- **Arcs are positive, negative, or flat — chosen deliberately.** Master the positive arc first; whatever you choose, write the false belief and climactic choice into the sheet now so Chapter 12 has something to invoice.
- **Sheets are causal machinery, not biography.** Every field must cause or explain choices — cut the rest. The payoff arrives immediately: field 9's chain gives Chapter 9 an outline and gives every scene its friction on demand.
