## Opening hook

Two prompts, one assistant, thirty seconds apart.

**Prompt A:** "Write me a story about a lighthouse."

**Prompt B:**

```text
Role: you are a literary-fiction drafting partner; I will revise everything.
Context: coastal town, present day. The lighthouse has been automated for
twenty years; the last keeper, Ana, 68, still lives in its shadow and
resells the lamps tourists steal as "authentic artifacts."
Task: one scene, 350-450 words: a tourist asks Ana to pose with a lamp;
she decides whether to lie about having been the keeper.
Constraints: close third, past, Ana-anchored; no flashbacks; the word
"lonely" may not appear; end on an action, not a realization.
Output: scene only — no title, no preamble, no commentary.
```

Prompt A returns the average of ten thousand lighthouse stories: storm, bell, metaphor about solitude, possibly a ghost. You have read it already; it was published the day the model's training data ended and every day since. Prompt B returns something narrower and stranger — a lying keeper, a lamp-economy, an ending that stops on a *decision made visible* — and the difference is not that you tricked the machine or found a magic incantation. The difference is that Prompt B **transfers decisions**: role, context, task, constraints, output shape — five slots, each a decision *you* made about the story before the machine was asked anything.

That transfer is this entire chapter. Prompt craft for fiction is not咒术; it is *pre-writing in a machine-readable format*. The five-part anatomy gives you a reusable frame; constraints do the heavy lifting where adjectives fail; iteration turns first drafts into usable material; few-shot examples teach the model *your* prose instead of the internet's; and six reusable prompt cards (PC-01 through PC-06) graduate into Appendix A. By the end of the chapter you will build and test your own scene-drafter prompt — the first card in *your* file, joining the book's library you'll collect through Part III.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- [ ] Write a fiction prompt with role, context, task, constraints, output shape.
- [ ] Use constraints (and negative constraints) instead of adjective fog.
- [ ] Run draft → critique → refine loops and few-shot prompting with your own prose.
- [ ] Debug a misbehaving prompt instead of re-rolling until luck arrives.
- [ ] Assemble and save a reusable scene-drafter prompt (PC-01, adapted to your voice).

## Anatomy of a fiction prompt

Every effective fiction prompt in this book — including all twenty-seven cards in Appendix A — is a assembly of the same five slots. Not every prompt must fill all five loudly (experienced users compress), but every prompt *should account* for all five somewhere:

1. **Role** — who the assistant is *for this task*: "you are a skeptical fiction editor," "a continuity checker using only my story bible," "a drafting partner whose output I will rewrite." Role is not cosplay theater; it selects which slice of the model's behavior space gets pressure. The same model answers differently as "copyeditor" and as "MFA workshop leader" because the completions those frames predict differ — you are steering the prior.
2. **Context** — what the model must know that it cannot see: story situation, POV, audience, prior decisions, the relevant bones of the bible. Context is where Chapter 5's shared-desk lesson applies: **fresh, tight, and near** beats long, old, and buried. Paste the *relevant* three paragraphs, not the last forty pages; summarize stable facts in two lines instead of pasting their fifth retelling.
3. **Task** — the operation, singular and named: *draft, critique, extract, compare, compress, stress-test*. One primary verb per prompt when possible. "Improve this and also tell me if the stakes are right and suggest a title" is three tasks wearing a trench coat; the model will do the most pattern-salient one (often the title) and gesture at the rest.
4. **Constraints** — the rules that shape the space: length, POV, tense, banned words, required beats, register, what the ending must do. Constraints are *the* differentiator between Prompt A and Prompt B — more on them directly below; this is where your Chapter 2–4 decisions become machine-checkable.
5. **Output shape** — what the deliverable looks like: "scene only, 350–450 words, no preamble"; "a table: beat | purpose | rung of stakes"; "five options, each ≤30 words, numbered." The shape is a contract that makes *your* job easier: unpromted prose arrives with an unwanted essay attached; stated shapes arrive ready to use — and make failure obvious (missing column = missed instruction = resurface, don't debug blind).

**Constraints beat adjectives.** This is the chapter's sharpest technical point, so let's feel it work. Adjective fog asks the model to *share your feeling*:

> "Write a really tense, atmospheric, beautifully written scene with great dialogue."

Every content word there — tense, atmospheric, beautiful, great — is *subjective and unverifiable*; the model resolves each toward its statistical center: candle adjectives, breath held, silence stretched. You asked for mood; you specified nothing; you received the center. Now the same intent as constraints:

```text
Constraints: the scene's conflict must be unresolved at the end; no
character raises their voice above a normal pitch; every line of
dialogue must contain a request or a refusal; maximum one sentence of
interiority; no weather indoors; the last paragraph contains a
physical action and no interior thought.
```

Each line is *checkable*: either the output does the thing or it doesn't. And notice what happened to "tense": **tension stopped being a mood request and became a structural arrangement** — unresolved conflict, requests and refusals only, action closing instead of reflection. The model is now much likelier to produce tension not because it felt your adjective but because the *shape* you demanded is the shape of tension. When a mood word matters, convert it: "tense" → structural consequences; "funny" → specific comic mechanism (understatement? escalation? category slip-ups?); "lyrical" → a rhythm rule you can state (clauses of a certain length, image source restricted to one domain — water, say). Constraints are adjectives with their homework done.

> TRY IT — Take your weakest existing prompt (everyone has one they keep reusing) and rewrite it with all five slots, converting at least two adjectives into checkable constraints. Run both versions in fresh sessions. Diff the outputs *against your constraints*, line by line, before judging which you prefer — you are grading compliance first and quality second, because compliance is what you can make repeatable.

### The version line

Every prompt card in this book carries a header comment:

```text

```

Three fields, all load-bearing: the **ID** (stable identity across the book and your file), the **version** (v1, v2… — bump when you edit the prompt's behavior), and the **tested** date against a mainstream assistant (freshness evidence for the Chapter 5 discipline — capability-dependent cards age). "Generic, no model-specific syntax" is a promise: no secret slash-commands, no brand dialect, nothing that dies with a product update. When *your* cards graduate to your file (the Project exercise), keep the same three fields; six months later they are the difference between a prompt library and a prompt graveyard.

## Iteration: draft → critique → refine

One-shot prompting — the prayer model — assumes a perfect first result, which Chapter 5 already told you to stop expecting. The working loop has three legs, each a distinct *task* with a distinct *role*:

1. **Draft leg:** generate material under constraints (the anatomy above).
2. **Critique leg:** in a *fresh* framing (or fresh session), evaluate the output against your stated goals — "as a skeptical editor: where does this scene fail its constraints? Miss its goal? Cliché? Be specific; no praise." (PC-02 is this leg in card form.)
3. **Refine leg:** apply *your* adjudicated fixes — sometimes by asking the model to execute *specific* changes ("tighten paragraphs 2 and 4; keep every constraint from the original prompt; do not touch the dialogue"), often by editing yourself and returning the result as new context.

Why legs instead of one mega-instruction ("draft it perfectly"): instruction stacks conflict (Chapter 5's field guide, force 2), critiques of a model's own fresh draft are shallow unless *grounded in your criteria*, and — decisively — **the loop's adjudication step is where your taste does its reps.** A loop you adjudicate is practice; a loop that self-feeds is a circle (failure F-07's little sister, previewing Chapter 12's non-converging revision loops). Two loop-hygiene rules: (a) **max two refine passes per leg before you intervene by hand** — if the output isn't converging toward your constraint list after two, the prompt is wrong, not the output (see the debugger below); (b) **log the loop's outcome in one decisions-file line** when anything substantive was accepted — the loop is only as honest as its ledger.

**Prompt chains** extend the loop across operations: extract → transform → verify, each step's output the next step's context. Example chain for description work: (1) *extract* every concrete detail from my scene as a list; (2) *flag* which details are owned-by-this-story versus generic (Chapter 4's ladder); (3) *rewrite flagged lines* to rung 3+, keeping count fixed. Chains beat mega-prompts for the same reason recipes beat prayers: each step is checkable, failures localize (you can see *which* step lied), and you can re-run just the broken link. The cost is bookkeeping — hence Chapter 7's prompt file, where proven chains get card-ified.

**Negative constraints** are the "must not" half of the space, and fiction needs them more than most domains because the model's positive priors are so strong (it *knows* what stories usually do — Chapter 5's prediction frame — so "surprise me" underspecifies against a gale). Practical set: banned clichés ("no *heart pounded*, no *silence stretched*"), banned summary ("no backstory paragraphs"), banned resolutions ("do not reconcile the sisters in this scene"), banned closings ("no final-sentence realization"). Pair every negative with a positive substitute where possible ("no weather indoors; use interior objects instead") — pure prohibition leaves a vacuum the prior rushes to fill; prohibition-plus-replacement gives the vacuum a shape.

**The prompt debugger (PC-06 habit).** When output misbehaves: *don't re-roll and hope* — re-rolling samples the same distribution. Instead run Chapter 5's four-force card in order: (1) buried or vague instruction → move and sharpen; (2) contradictions inside the prompt → hunt them (examples beat instructions — check your few-shot); (3) desk pressure → shrink or re-enclose context, restate locks *after* the paste; (4) task stack → decompose into a chain. Still failing? Run the prompt **three times in fresh sessions** to see whether you have a *systematic* miss (prompt problem) or a *draw* (randomness — now fix by locks, not panic). Then change **one slot only** and retest — single-variable debugging, or you'll never know which edit worked. This habit, institutionalized as PC-06, is the difference between owning prompting and renting it.

## Few-shot with your own prose

**Few-shot prompting** = including examples of the behavior you want ("few" = anywhere from one to a handful). The examples do more teaching than your instructions (Chapter 5, force 2: *examples always outrank instructions*), which cuts both ways: a bad example poisons faster than a good rule can save, and — the opportunity — **your own prose as example gives the model something no instruction can: your voice's actual statistics.**

Technique, in order:

1. **Select the anchor carefully.** One to three paragraphs *you* stand by — voice-consistent, scene-typical, no experimental failures. This is the voice-anchor idea at prompt level (the paragraph-first discipline of Chapter 10 grows from here): the example must be *yours* or the model will faithfully imitate someone else's you.
2. **Frame the example as data, not as answer key.** "Below is my prose; match voice, sentence rhythm, and image density — do not continue or improve it" — otherwise the model starts *writing toward* the example's ending, and you get fan fiction of your own paragraph.
3. **State what to abstract** — name the dimensions: contraction habits, clause length, how you handle interiority (free indirect? quoted thought?), image source range. Naming dimensions prevents shallow mimicry (swapping in your vocabulary while keeping the internet's rhythm).
4. **Keep the example adjacent to the task**, after the instructions, right before generation — the desk's recency bias is now your ally (Chapter 5's "near" rule).
5. **Test the pair:** if the model's output would pass as yours when you're not looking, the few-shot worked; if it passes only because you're *tired*, tighten the frame with one negative constraint (PC-04 is this recipe in card form).

Few-shot's limits, honestly: it shifts *surface statistics* impressively and shifts *judgment* not at all — the model can sound like you while choosing things you never would (Chapter 14's whole diagnostics exist for this residue). And there's a privacy rhyme with Chapter 5: examples are *your* text going onto the shared desk — paste from files you own, never third-party prose ("match this famous author's voice" is both an IP tar pit and a worse teacher: published famous voices are overrepresented in the model's center already).

## Prompt cards PC-01 … PC-06

The chapter's six graduates, introduced here in teaching order, collected in full (with knobs) in Appendix A. Read them now as *patterns of anatomy made flesh* — each card's five slots marked in miniature:

> PROMPT CARD PC-01 — Scene drafter (voice-anchor)
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-01 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic, no model-specific syntax -->
> Role: you draft fiction scenes for me to revise; your text is raw
> material, not final prose.
> Context: [story situation in 2–3 lines: who wants what here, what
> opposes]. Anchor prose (my voice — match rhythm and diction, do not
> continue it):
> [1–3 paragraphs of my prose]
> Task: draft the next scene from these beats: [beats as short lines].
> Constraints: POV [X], tense [past/present], 400–550 words; conflict
> unresolved at the end; no backstory paragraphs; no words from my
> banned list: [list].
> Output: scene only after a line "DRAFT:" — no preamble, no titles.
> ```

> PROMPT CARD PC-02 — Critique my draft (structured)
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-02 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic -->
> Role: skeptical fiction editor; specific, unsentimental, no praise
> unless a line earns it with a reason.
> Task: critique the passage below against these five questions only:
> 1) What does the POV character want in this scene, and is it visible
> by the midpoint? 2) Where does telling occur that should be staged?
> 3) Which sentences are generic (could appear in anyone's story)?
> 4) Where does POV/distance slip? 5) Does the ending turn or fade?
> Format: numbered findings, each as [location quote] → [issue] →
> [concrete fix]. Max 12 findings, ranked by damage.
> Passage: [text]
> ```

> PROMPT CARD PC-03 — Explain-my-options brainstormer
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-03 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic -->
> Role: options-broker, not co-writer; you generate choices; I choose.
> Context: [story situation / decision point, 3–5 lines].
> Task: give me 10 options for [decision], each labeled with:
> (a) what it costs the protagonist, (b) which story shape it makes
> most likely (choice A’s kind of ending), (c) one risk it creates
> for pacing.
> Rules: no option that requires new characters unless I mark
> "cast open"; at least 3 options must be uncomfortable; no ranking,
> no recommendation — I decide.
> ```

> PROMPT CARD PC-04 — Few-shot style transfer (my prose)
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-04 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic -->
> Role: stylistic apprentice to the anchor prose below.
> Task: rewrite the TARGET passage to match the ANCHOR's voice.
> Abstract and carry: sentence-length rhythm, contraction habits,
> interiority method, image-source range, punctuation tics.
> Do not carry: the anchor's content, names, or plot.
> Do not improve ideas — only manner.
> ANCHOR (my prose): [1–3 paragraphs]
> TARGET: [passage to re-voice]
> Output: rewritten target only.
> ```

> PROMPT CARD PC-05 — Constraint imposer ("write with…")
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-05 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic -->
> Role: constraint executor for a drafting exercise.
> Task: rewrite the passage under ALL of these simultaneously:
> - no sentences longer than 18 words
> - no abstract nouns from this list: [list]
> - every paragraph must contain one concrete object handled by a hand
> - dialogue lines ≤ 8 words
> - keep: POV, tense, proper nouns, and the sequence of events
> If a constraint makes the passage impossible, keep the closest
> lawful version and list which lines you strained at, after the text.
> Passage: [text]
> ```

> PROMPT CARD PC-06 — Prompt debugger
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-06 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic -->
> Role: prompt engineer for fiction workflows.
> Below: MY PROMPT and THE OUTPUT I got, plus WHAT I WANTED.
> Diagnose in this order, stopping at the first confirmed cause:
> 1) vague/buried instruction  2) internal contradiction (check my
> examples vs. my rules)  3) context pressure (what in my pasted
> material pulls against my instruction)  4) stacked tasks.
> For each confirmed cause: quote the offending line, then give ONE
> revised prompt (full text) fixing only that cause. No praise, no
> general advice. End with: what to test first and what result
> would prove the fix worked.
> MY PROMPT: [...] OUTPUT: [...] WANTED: [...]
> ```

Read PC-02's five questions against Chapter 4's anatomy — the card *is* the chapter's craft made executable. PC-06 is Chapter 5's four forces, prompt-level. Nothing in the six cards is brand-specific or clever; their power is entirely anatomical — and watching *which* slot produced which output effect, across your own tests, is the real curriculum of the card library.

### Five failures, mapped to slots

A compact pathology table — each row a symptom you will actually see, the slot that caused it, and the repair:

1. **Symptom:** output is a wall of purple description before anything happens. **Slot fault:** *task* was "set the scene" shaped (or implied by a bare "write a scene about…"), and no *constraint* marked what counts as progress. **Repair:** name the operation's spine — "the scene must move from her decision to open the letter to her decision not to answer it" — and add "no description longer than two sentences before the first line of dialogue or decision."
2. **Symptom:** characters talk like the same well-educated stranger. **Slot fault:** missing *context* voice data (no anchor, no voice notes from Chapter 3's field 10) plus *constraints* silent on diction. **Repair:** paste one speech per major participant as voice data with "each speaker keeps their diction/syntax/habit"; add "if two consecutive lines could swap speakers without meaning changing, rewrite line two."
3. **Symptom:** the ending ties off what you wanted left open. **Slot fault:** *constraint* left the ending's shape unspecified, so the model's default (literary default: resolution; genre default: climax-payoff) filled the slot. **Repair:** state the ending as a required shape — "end on the unanswered question; no reconciliation, no summary of feelings" — in the *last* constraint line, near generation.
4. **Symptom:** output answers a *different, easier question* than yours (gives definitions when you wanted options; gives one polished option when you wanted ten). **Slot fault:** *task* verb mismatched your actual need, often buried mid-prompt. **Repair:** lead with the verb and quantify — "Give me TEN options, then STOP" — and move any secondary asks ("and briefly note risks") behind an explicit "after the ten, in a separate section."
5. **Symptom:** format keeps collapsing (preamble essays, markdown tables where you wanted prose, bullet fragments of a scene). **Slot fault:** *output shape* absent or specified once, early, away from generation. **Repair:** restate the shape adjacent to the task AND at the end ("first line of your reply: DRAFT: — nothing before it"), and give a miniature shape example (three lines of the format you want, placeholder content) — shape examples teach faster than shape rules.

Rows 4 and 5 are the everyday annoyances; rows 1–3 are where stories quietly go wrong, which is why your compliance grading (pen on constraints, output beside them) checks *ending shape* and *progress* with the same seriousness as word count. A card that has never been graded on its story-critical constraints has only ever been graded on its formatting.

### Parameterized cards: prompt templates with fills

As your file grows, you will notice yourself maintaining *families*: the same skeleton with swappable parts. Treat serious cards like tiny programs — **fixed logic, marked fills**:

```text
[fixed]  Role + loop rules + output shape + banned list logic
[FILL-A] story situation (2–3 lines, from the bible)
[FILL-B] beats (3–7 short lines, hand-written)
[FILL-C] anchor prose (1–3 paragraphs, my best voice)
[FILL-D] constraint variant (comedy / dread / intimacy pack)
```

Two disciplines make parameterization pay: **fills come from your documents** (FILL-A from the bible, FILL-B from the outline — never improvised under the chat's influence, or the desk starts drafting your beats too); and **constraint packs are named and versioned separately** (the "dread pack" — no interior comfort, weather permitted only as pressure, endings forbid relief — can be tested once and reused across stories, since it encodes *your* theory of dread, not this plot's). Promoted cards in Appendix A are shown *de-parameterized* for teaching; your working copies should look like the marked template above. When a fill type keeps growing past its slot (context balloons), that's Chapter 5's desk pressure — summarize to the bones, keep the bible's relevant three rules verbatim, and restate locks after the paste.

### Register, audience, and task-mode controls

Three smaller slot-refinements that beginners reach for constantly:

**Register** ("how the prose is dressed"): state it as *readers and restraint*, not vibes — "register: literary-contemporary workshop prose; restraint over ornament; contractions in dialogue; zero rhetorical questions in narration." If register matters, ban one opposite failure explicitly ("no inspirational-poster cadence, no closing aphorisms").

**Audience** ("who is imagined to read this"): "draft for a discerning adult literary audience; assume intelligence; no YA sensibilities; no explaining the metaphor." Audience lines quietly fix tone where mood adjectives fail — they are constraint-conversions of taste.

**Task-mode** ("which hat you wear now"): critique mode vs. drafting mode vs. extraction mode must never share a prompt (stacked tasks force one). Signs you mixed modes: a "draft" that keeps editorializing ("here you might consider…"), or a "critique" that silently rewrites everything. Split it: critique in session one with PC-02; drafts in session two with PC-01 and your adjudicated notes as FILL material. The mode boundary *is* the loop leg boundary — the anatomy and the iteration are the same system seen twice.

## Worked example: building your scene drafter live

Let's assemble PC-01 from scratch — the chapter's Project exercise, run once in full so you can imitate the *process*, not just copy the card.

**Step 1 — Decide before you type (all five slots, in your words).** Suppose your project is a family comedy of manners at a wedding: POV close third on the bride's sister; present tense (the genre's jittery heartbeat); the scene you need: the seating-chart confrontation. Decisions to transfer: *role* (drafter-for-revision — you'll rewrite everything anyway), *context* (three lines: the fight's stake is the mother's public verdict; the sister knows the chart is wrong on purpose), *task* (draft from your five hand-written beats), *constraints* (800–1,000 words; comedy register via understatement, *no* farce escalation; no one cries; unresolved; present, close third, no head-hopping), *output shape* (DRAFT: block only).

**Step 2 — Add your anchor.** Two paragraphs of your prose from Chapter 1's baseline or the milestone story — the ones that still sound like you when you read them cold. (Anchor placement: *after* instructions, *before* beats — near generation.)

**Step 3 — First run, grade compliance first.** Paste, run fresh, and check against constraints with a pen: present tense? (…) close third? (…) no tears? (…) unresolved? (…) Assume anything ungraded failed until shown otherwise. Typical first-run misses: the comedy slides to farce (your "no farce" line was vague — convert to "no pratfalls, no one locked in rooms, misdirection only through dialogue"), or the ending resolves with a hug (restate "unresolved" *as the last line of constraints* — nearness wins).

**Step 4 — Single-variable edits, version to v2.** Change the farce constraint to its concrete form; rerun; grade again. One variable at a time (debugger discipline). Log: `PC-01-yourname v1 → v2 · farce→concrete-ban · 2026-09-XX`.

**Step 5 — Critique leg (PC-02) on the best run.** Fresh session: the five questions against your output; adjudicate — typically you'll keep structure, rewrite 40% of sentences (voice-anchor doing its job — Chapter 14 will name and systematize that 40% instinct).

**Step 6 — Freeze the card, keep knobs visible.** Save `scene-drafter-[yours]` with version line, blanks marked `[...]`, and a two-line "when this fails" note (e.g., "fails on action scenes — beats need physical causality, not dialogue beats"). That failure note is what makes a prompt file a *tool library* instead of a scrapbook: every card carries its own edge-wear.

Commentary on the whole build: nothing clever happened — decisions → constraints → anchor → compliance grading → single-variable versioning → critique. The machine's contribution was *throughput* (a draft to grade in seconds); your contribution was *every line that made grading possible*. Run this build once properly and you've learned the chapter; the six cards and every card you write for the rest of the book are variations.

## Exercises

- **Try (15 min):** Fix five broken prompts (provided in Appendix B's mirror — attempt first): (1) "Write something sad about the sea."; (2) "Make this paragraph better."; (3) "Continue my story in the same style." [no story attached]; (4) "Write a cool dialogue between two spies, very realistic and very witty."; (5) "Critique my chapter." [no chapter, no criteria]. For each: name the missing slot(s), rewrite with all five, convert at least one adjective to a checkable constraint. Five rewrites in fifteen minutes is the right pace — anatomy becomes reflex through reps, not re-reading.

- **Stretch (1–2 h):** Build and test your scene-drafter, following the worked example's six steps on a real scene from your milestone story or current project: two runs minimum, compliance graded both times, one single-variable fix logged as v2, one PC-02 critique pass, your adjudication written in five lines. Then *deliberately break it once*: feed it a beat list with an internal contradiction (e.g., "quiet scene, no raised voices" + a beat "she screams") and watch which constraint wins — that experiment teaches more about constraint priority than any theory paragraph.

- **Project (ongoing):** Start your personal prompt file (`prompts/` folder — Chapter 7's anatomy), seeded with your tested scene-drafter and copies of PC-01…PC-06 you've *actually run at least once* (cards you've run are tools; cards you've收藏ed are shopping). Habit: every prompt you reuse twice gets an ID, a version line, and a one-line failure note. By Chapter 16 this file is your private prompt library — the book's Appendix A was built exactly this way, and yours should eventually embarrass the book's with your own cases.

## Pitfalls

> PITFALL — **One-shot hoping.** *Symptom:* you re-roll the same prompt five, eight, twelve times waiting for magic; drafts accumulate in the chat like lottery tickets, none graded, nothing learned; the vocabulary of luck enters your notes ("this one *feels* right"). *Why it happens:* re-rolling is cheaper-feeling than diagnosing (no self-confrontation required), and the first-run lottery genuinely pays off often enough in brainstorming to wire the habit where it doesn't belong — by drafting time, you're sampling distribution shape without knowing it (Chapter 5's randomness), so the failures you hit are *predictable properties of the prompt*, and no number of draws fixes a broken slot. *Fix:* the debugger's order — three fresh runs only to classify *systematic vs. draw*; then four forces; then one-variable edits with version bumps; compliance grading with a pen before taste. Two refine passes cap, then hands-on intervention (loop hygiene). *Prevention:* version lines and decisions-log entries make hoping *visible* — a prompt with v1 and no tests is allowed to be lucky once; anything you reach for twice must be a card with compliance history.

Related failures for Appendix C: F-10 (prompt works once, never again — randomness + unversioned prompts), F-05 (agreeable brainstorming — previewed: your critique legs need adversarial roles or they'll flatter; full treatment Chapter 8).

## Key takeaways

- **Prompt craft is pre-writing in machine-readable form:** role, context, task, constraints, output shape — five slots, each a story decision transferred before generation.
- **Constraints beat adjectives:** convert every mood word into a checkable structural rule; prohibition pairs with replacement; examples outrank instructions, so police your few-shots.
- **Iterate in legs (draft → critique → refine), chain for multi-step work, cap refine passes, adjudicate everything** — the loop's value is your taste's reps, not the model's throughput.
- **Few-shot with your *own* prose is the voice lever** — anchor carefully, frame as data, name what to abstract; it transfers surface statistics, not judgment (Chapter 14 finishes the job).
- **Debug, don't gamble:** four forces, three-run classification, one variable per edit, version lines on everything — a prompt you can't debug is a prompt you don't own yet.
