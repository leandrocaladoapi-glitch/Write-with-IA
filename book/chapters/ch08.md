## Opening hook

Ask a general assistant for story ideas and you will receive, with enthusiasm, fifty ideas. They will be: a time traveler who regrets one meeting; a detective who discovers the killer is himself; a child who befriends the monster under the bed; a woman who inherits a mysterious house; a robot who learns to love. Ask again tomorrow and you will receive them again — slightly recombined, equally polite, equally dead. Not because the machine is stupid, but because *unconstrained ideation is a vote for the statistical center*, and the center of the distribution of "story ideas ever proposed" is where stories go to be nobody's.

Now the other experiment. Five prompts, each carrying *your* constraints — a place you actually know, a job you actually understand, a fear you actually have, a formal restriction you chose (one location; told in receipts; no villain), a combination of two domains that don't belong together (tide tables + custody law). Five attempts at the same act of narrowing. You will get some clunkers; you will also get one idea that makes your hands want to type — not because it's "better written" than the fifty, but because it couldn't have existed without *you* in the prompt.

This chapter is the art and discipline of that difference. **Constrained ideation** (generating premises as a narrowing game, not a wishing well), **stress-testing** (killing darlings with adversarial prompts before they cost you a year), and **loglines** (compressing the survivor into a sentence you can steer by). These are the first cards of your prompt library — PC-07 through PC-10 — and by the end of the chapter you will have selected *one* premise, with a stress-tested logline, and parked the rest. That selection is the seed every later chapter grows: outline (9), draft (10), characters (11), revision (12). Skip it and the rest of the book becomes an elaborate way to avoid choosing.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- [ ] Generate premises with constrained ideation (not "give me ideas").
- [ ] Stress-test a premise for stakes, novelty, and fit.
- [ ] Write and refine a logline.
- [ ] Select one premise worth months of work — and park the rest.

## Constrained ideation

The governing insight from Chapter 6: the model's default output *is* the average of what's been asked before; your constraints are the only lever that pulls it away from that average — and simultaneously the only thing that makes an idea *yours*. Three techniques, escalating in force.

### What-if ladders (PC-07)

A **what-if ladder** takes one seed and forces it through successive rewrites, each rung adding a constraint that *costs* something. The anti-pattern it replaces is "brainstorm 20 premises" (quantity of neighbors); the ladder produces *lineages* (quality of descent). Run it:

> PROMPT CARD PC-07 — What-if ladder
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-07 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic, no model-specific syntax -->
> Role: ideation partner executing a ladder, not a list.
> Seed: [one sentence situation].
> Produce 10 successive WHAT-IF rewrites. Rules for the ladder:
> - Rungs 1–3: change ONE external condition each (place, era,
>   profession, constraint of the situation) — keep characters intact.
> - Rungs 4–6: each rung must give the protagonist a harder WANT
>   (something they can fail at visibly).
> - Rungs 7–8: each rung must add a cost that lands on a specific
>   other person.
> - Rungs 9–10: each rung must make the obvious genre version
>   impossible (state which genre you just banned and why).
> Format: numbered rungs, one line each, ending with: which rung you
> would develop and one sentence of why — do not choose for me; flag
> the choice as a suggestion only.
> ```

Read the rules *as constraints doing Chapter 6's work*: "one external condition," "harder want," "cost lands on a specific person," "ban the obvious genre" — each checkable, each converting vague ideation into structural pressure. The ladder's real product is often **rung 9–10**: banning the obvious version forces the model (and you) off the road you'd both default to, which is where the original rung either dies usefully or reveals it had a second, stranger life.

> TRY IT — Ladder your worst stored idea — the one you're embarrassed still interests you. Ten rungs, ten minutes if you type fast. Most writers discover the embarrassment was the *generic* wrapper around a decent core; the ladder's job is to strip the wrapper. If rungs 7–10 feel impossible ("this seed can't carry costs"), that's the seed telling you its weight class — file it and ladder something with more mass.

### Combination and constraint stacking (PC-10)

**Idea combiners** take two parked premises (or a premise + a formal restriction) and fuse them under a rule that prevents mush:

> PROMPT CARD PC-10 — Idea combiner
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-10 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic -->
> Role: synthesis engineer; I provide elements; you fuse, you don't
> average.
> Elements: A) [parked premise one-liner]  B) [parked premise
> one-liner]  C) [optional formal constraint: one location / told in
> documents / no direct dialogue / etc.]
> Task: 5 fusion attempts. For each, state: (1) the NEW conflict that
> exists only in the fusion (not A + B side by side); (2) which
> element you had to distort to make room, and how; (3) the first
> scene's goal in one line.
> Anti-rules: no title-generation; no "best of both worlds" language;
> if an element refuses to fuse, say so and give the nearest honest
> version.
> Output: 5 attempts, numbered, then stop.
> ```

The load-bearing line is (1): *the new conflict that exists only in the fusion* — the test distinguishing synthesis from collage. (2) forces honesty about distortion — fusions always damage an element; knowing *which* one bent tells you what the story now privileges. Fusions that pass tend to produce premises with built-in thematic pressure (two worldviews colliding in one plot); fusions that fail teach you your parked ideas were parked for reasons.

**Constraint stacking** is the freest form: gather your personal constraint bank — *places you know cold, jobs you've done or watched up close, one fear, one formal toy, one "not this genre"* — and require each ideation run to spend a minimum: "every premise must include one real place + one non-negotiable cost + one formal restriction from my list below." The bank is a *you*-artifact, accumulated in the margins of your reading (Chapter 14's style sheet doubles as a bank source); the model contributes recombination labor only. Ideation help you can't get anywhere else looks like this — *not* "write me five premises," but "recombine *these* mine under *these* rules."

### The ideation loop, honestly

The full working loop for this chapter's stage: ladder or combine (PC-07/10) → *you* select 2–3 rungs/attempts that produce hand-itch → stress-test survivors (PC-08, below) → logline the winner (PC-09) → **file everything else** in the parked-ideas doc with one-line loglines (the Parked file is Chapter 16's sequel-writing mine and your anti-sunk-cost insurance). Two loop truths before we move to testing: *generate in bulk, select in silence* — don't adjudicate mid-ladder; selection during generation contaminates both (you'll get safer rungs, dumber judgments). And *one seed per session* — ideation sessions that mix five seeds produce five averages; sessions that stay on one seed drill to the strange strata where the good stuff hides.

## Stress-testing premises

Selection without testing is vibes; testing is where the machine's adversarial value (Chapter 1's improvement #2, systematized) earns its keep. Three tests, each a prompt, each answered *in writing by you* before anything gets "developed."

> PROMPT CARD PC-08 — Premise stress-test
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-08 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic -->
> Role: ruthless development editor with no stake in your feelings;
> you are testing a premise for a multi-month commitment.
> Premise: [four-part premise from Ch 2 — protagonist, goal,
> obstacle, stakes].
> Run these checks, IN THIS ORDER, in this format — for each: FINDING
> (one line), DAMAGE (high/med/low), REPAIR (one concrete change, or
> "none available — flag for parking"):
> 1. STAKES CHECK: is the cost-of-failure concrete, climbable on the
>    Ch 2 ladder, and *worse* than inconvenience at every rung?
> 2. NOVELTY AUDIT: state the three most-predictable versions of this
>    premise; then state what makes MINE not those — if you cannot
>    tell them apart, say "center-of-distribution" loudly.
> 3. WHY-ME / WHY-NOW: what in ME (the author-proxy below) makes this
>    mine to write — and what in the story's present moment makes it
>    urgent? If either is empty, mark the premise.
> 4. CAPACITY CHECK: against my declared constraints (length target,
>    POV exclusivity, single-timeline), name the structural cost this
>    premise will charge.
> 5. KILLER QUESTION: the one objection a skeptical first reader will
>    raise by page 10 — phrase it as the reader's exact words.
> No praise. End with: verdict — DEVELOP / PARK / PARK-AND-STEAL-
> [element] (name the element worth cannibalizing later).
> Author-proxy (why-me fuel): [3–5 lines: your obsessions, the
> specific memories/knowledge you'd spend, what you'd refuse to
> sentimentalize]
> ```

**Why the author-proxy is inside the prompt:** check 3 would otherwise be answered with horoscope-grade flattery ("this premise suits a writer with your evident depth!") — Chapter 5's sycophancy, fully deployed. Give the model *raw material about you* to work against ("I obsess over inheritance disputes; I refuse to sentimentalize small towns; I know tide tables from a former job") and the check becomes answerable with specifics: *the premise spends exactly your listed obsessions and respects your refusal* — a claim you can verify. **Why verdicts include PARK-AND-STEAL:** premises die easier when they leave a bequest; naming the organ to harvest (a character, a setting, one turn) converts killing darlings from funeral into inventory — the parked file's real wealth-building habit.

**Novelty-check honesty.** Check 2 is the one the machine is *worst* at judging for you and *best* at scaffolding: its "three predictable versions" are genuinely its center-of-mass samples (reliable — they're literally what the distribution suggests), and the differentiation sentence ("MINE is not those because…") must be *yours to assert* — if you can't complete it without the machine's help, you are standing in the center, however flattering the view. Expect this to happen; it's the test working. Park, ladder again, return.

**Why-me, why-now — the two halves aren't equal.** Why-now (urgency) is nice-to-have; why-me (authority + obsession + refusal) is load-bearing — stories survive outdated urgency; they rarely survive an author with nothing at stake. Taste note: "why-me" answers that are only *competence* ("I know that world") produce competent books; answers containing *compulsion* ("I can't let the inheritance question go") produce the ones people finish at 2 a.m. Mine for the compulsion line; competence is the floor, not the fuel.

### Killing darlings early

The stage's hardest discipline, and the one AI assistance makes *cheaper* — which is precisely why to use it now. Every parked premise gets: one-line logline, verdict date, and the bequest (if any) noted; the file never gets deleted from, only grown (Chapter 16 mines it). The economics that justify ruthlessness: a premise killed today cost a logline; the same premise discovered broken at outline cost a week; at draft, a month; at "why isn't this working," a year of sunk-cost bargaining. Your decisions file gets one row per kill — *premise X parked: predictable-version problem; stole the notary for Y* — because future-you's favorite proof of taste will be the graveyard's size. Writers who only ever *add* are not generous; they're afraid of the ledger.

## Loglines that work

The survivor gets compressed. A **logline** is one sentence (professionally, one to three — we'll practice the one-sentence discipline first) that carries: protagonist + want + obstacle/antagonism + stakes-flavored cost, in language whose *word choice* signals the tone. Anatomy, using a famous-shape example rebuilt generically: *[A specific protagonist] must [goal] or else [cost], despite [obstacle] — all in [world/tone signal]*. The tonal-signal clause is the underrated half: "…or the town's last well goes dry" says rural fable; "…before the merger closes" says office thriller; the logline *recruits* the right expectations before page one.

> PROMPT CARD PC-09 — Logline refiner
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-09 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic -->
> Role: logline editor; compression specialist; no new plot elements.
> Input premise: [four-part premise]. Input draft logline (mine):
> [your sentence].
> Task: (1) DIAGNOSE my draft against the anatomy — name which slot
> is missing, vague, or overloaded (protagonist / goal / obstacle /
> cost / tonal signal). (2) Offer 6 REFINEMENTS of MY draft only —
> each must keep my plot's events and characters; vary which slot
> gets emphasis. (3) For each, note what it HIDES (every logline
> lies by omission — name the omission).
> Anti-rules: no words like "must confront"; no "battling
> [abstraction]"; if my premise can't support a concrete cost, say
> so instead of decorating it.
> Output: diagnosis, then 6 sentences with their hidden cards.
> ```

Part (3) — *name the omission* — turns refinement into training: every logline necessarily hides something (the subplots, the POV trick, the false lead), and knowing *what yours hides* is how you keep the document honest when agents or friends ask "what's it about?" — and how you notice when your logline's hidden thing turns out to be the *actual* story (surprise: the tide journal was the book; logline gets revised at Chapter 12's structure pass, not carved in stone now).

**Your draft first, always.** PC-09 refines *your* sentence; arriving with the machine's sentence as input trains you into a reviewer of its compression rather than a compressor — the reps are the point (same reason Chapter 1's improvement #3 was hand-written). Aim: twenty minutes of your own attempts before the card runs; six refinements adjudicated down to one line you'd say out loud in an elevator without checking your notes.

### Anatomy of an ideation session (the ninety-minute lab)

Sessions that produce selections follow a fixed arc; running it deliberately separates *lab time* (structured, clocked, mechanical) from *itch time* (the unstructured noticing that feeds the bank). The lab, in order:

1. **Frame (5 min).** One seed, one session (loop truth #2). State the session's output contract aloud in your notes: "By 90 minutes: ≥10 ladder rungs filed, 2–3 shortlisted, 1 stress-tested if a shortlist survives." Output contracts prevent the common drift where ideation *feels* productive and ends without a verdict.
2. **Bank check (5 min).** Open the constraint bank; star today's ingredients — a place, a job, a fear, one formal toy, one genre refusal. If the bank's starved (first sessions), seed it *now* from memory: five places you know cold, three jobs you've watched or lived, one fear you'd spend. The bank is the session's *you*-reservoir; the ladder without it is Chapter 5's distribution tour.
3. **Generation sprints (30–40 min).** PC-07 (or PC-10) runs, one seed per sprint, *selection silent* (loop truth #1). Between runs: file non-selected output immediately (parked file grows *during* the session so re-reading old rungs can't anchor the next sprint). Two sprints on one seed max — the third sprint's rungs are almost always recolored rungs 1 and 6; notice and switch seeds rather than push.
4. **Cool-down + shortlist (10 min).** Reread cold; circle two or three without justifying yet — the circle is itch, not argument. Justifications come *after* the circle, written as one line each ("rung 6: the maintained-house-as-argument detail is the story's engine").
5. **Stress-test leg (25–30 min).** PC-08 on each shortlisted candidate, separate fresh sessions, findings adjudicated row by row — this is where ninety minutes gets spent properly: testing is the lab's actual product. Verdicts filed (DEVELOP / PARK / PARK-AND-STEAL).
6. **Close (5 min).** Session-log block (Mode: ideation lab; Adjudications: N filed), parked file's new entries skimmed once, next lab's seed question written if today's wells ran dry.

Two calibration notes. Ninety minutes is a ceiling, not a quota — a session that reaches a DEVELOP verdict at fifty minutes *ends* (the temptation to keep laddering past a verdict is how strong premises get talked out of themselves by fatigue); a session that hits ninety with no circle should log "seed dry," bank three new seed questions for next time, and end without manufactured enthusiasm — *dry sessions are data about seeds, not about you*. And lab cadence: weekly for an active project (the parked file compounds fastest at weekly rhythm); monthly otherwise — more frequent than monthly and ideation starts cannibalizing development time (a premise chosen is a premise that should be outlining, Chapter 9's job), less frequent and the bank goes stale.

### When the well is dry: seeds that refuse to ladder

First sessions and winter sessions meet the same failure: rungs that restate the seed in costume ("woman sells house" → "woman considers selling shop" — a list of synonyms pretending to be a descent). Three wrenches, in order of force:

- **Wrench 1 — Change the axis, not the content.** If rungs keep rotating the same situation, ladder *in a different dimension*: era (the same conflict in 1987), scale (the house is one room of a condemned block), ontology (the "brother" is the executor who never met her) — sometimes the seed's *subject* is fine and its *coordinates* are boring. One axis per rerun; note which axis unlocked descent in the session log (that's bank metadata about *you*: your axis preferences are part of your ideation signature).
- **Wrench 2 — Import a stranger.** PC-10 with an element from an unrelated parked premise or a bank item you haven't spent ("fuse with the formal toy: told in receipts"). Fusions break dead wells because the *conflict* must be new before the components settle — the collision does the laddering for you.
- **Wrench 3 — Invert the want.** The universal solvent: keep the situation, flip who wants what ("*Theo* wants to sell; Mara wants to keep" — five seconds of inversion, and watch rung 6's pressure reappear mirrored). Most dry wells are wells where the *first* want you imagined got silently elected permanent; inversion re-opens the ballot.

What does *not* fix a dry well: more runs of the same prompt (you're sampling a distribution Chapter 5 already told you about), lowering constraints (removing friction *increases* center-drift — the opposite of what's wrong), or "one more sprint" past fatigue (rung quality collapses and files junk you'll re-read as anchoring junk). If all three wrenches fail twice on a seed: file it as `dry-2026-09` in the parked file and pick a seed from the starred bank instead — persistence at ideation is a different virtue than persistence at drafting, and confusing them burns lab time that testing would have spent better.

### The parked file as a long game

`parked-ideas.md` deserves the chapter's closing attention because it is the stage's only *compounding* artifact — everything else (selected premise, logline) is a point; the file is a curve. Three long-game habits:

**Ledger format:** `- [date] · one-line logline · verdict · bequest? · itches? (yes/no)`. The `itches` flag is the file's secret engine: monthly top-to-bottom reads (Ch 7's habit) re-rate itches — an idea that still itches after cooling has *survived its own novelty decay*, which is the cheapest quality signal available to a solo writer. Two or three itches on file at any time = your next projects pre-vetted; the itch that survives a *year* is the novel you owe yourself.

**Bequests make kills relational.** Changelog-style linking ("premise A parked; its notary became protagonist of premise C") turns the file into a graph where dead ideas quietly vote on living ones — and gives PC-10 its best raw material (fusions of *cooled* elements beat fusions of fresh ones: fresh ideas resist distortion because they're still defended by recency; cooled ideas bend). When Chapter 16's 90-day plan asks what to write *next*, the graph answers with evidence instead of vibes.

**What the file is *not*:** a second manuscript (one line per entry — the moment entries grow paragraphs, you've started developing parked premises and are avoiding the selected one), a place for *other people's* ideas (borrowed premises carry obligations you haven't examined — log the source if you must, prefer your bank), or a graveyard you visit *instead* of testing (itches must enter labs; an un-tested itch at month eleven is procrastination wearing curiosity's clothes). The success metric stands: entries added, verdicts filed, itches re-rated — growth only, judgment always, development reserved for exactly one survivor at a time.

### Constraint stacking with a personal bank — a transcript

To make the bank concrete before you build yours: here is one ideation run as it actually looks when the "you" material is loaded. Bank excerpt (author's, kept in `prompts/bank.md`):

```text
PLACES (cold knowledge): Atlantic fishing towns (winter); a municipal
  tide-gauge shed; the ferry maintenance depot my father managed.
JOBS WATCHED UP CLOSE: hydrographic survey crews; small-claims
  clerks; wedding planners (one season, as a temp).
FEARS TO SPEND: administrative permanent mistakes (a filing that
  can't be undone); being recognized in a place I've outgrown.
FORMAL TOYS: told in receipts / one location / no direct dialogue
  for 3 pages / countdown without clocks.
REFUSALS: haunted house; evil corporation; prodigy child; any
  villain whose motive is only money.
```

**Run (PC-07 seeded with constraint statement rather than plot seed — the bank *is* the seed):**

> Task: from the bank above, produce 5 premise seeds. Rules: each seed spends ≥1 place + ≥1 fear + ≥1 formal toy; no seed may use a refusal item; every seed's protagonist must *work* in the place (not just visit). Format: four-part premises, ≤60 words, then: which bank items you spent (list).

**Output (abridged, two of five):**

> 1. A small-clerks-office clerk (place: tide-gauge shed as de facto office) discovers a decade of misfiled storm-surge claims that all trace to her own early handwriting; she has one filing cycle before the archive locks (fear: permanent administrative mistake) — told in receipts, each one a claim she must decide whether to re-file honestly and cost herself the job. [spends: gauge shed, filing fear, receipts toy, clerk job] [...]
> 3. [...] the ferry-depot manager's daughter returns for the sale of the depot's last tool crib, recognizes her father's inventory codes in a stranger's ledger (place: depot; fear: recognition-in-place), no direct dialogue for 3 pages — every family negotiation conducted through parts-order slips. [...]

*Commentary:* seeds 1 and 3 are already four-part (the bank's specificity did the heavy lifting — the model's job was *assembly under rules*, which is work it does reliably when the parts list is yours). Note what's absent: no seed needed a "twist," because constraint-collision *is* the novelty engine — receipts-as-family-communication existed the moment "formal toy" met "depot," and the machine's contribution was noticing the collision faster than a blank page would. Seed 1's fictionality check is the author's to run (small-claims clerks: real jobs, invented office — verify any detail that reaches the page per Ch 5's type-split). Selection went to seed 1 with the survey-crew job swapped in (the bank's *first* place item was stronger) — that swap, made by hand in thirty seconds, is why the bank stays under your authorship even when generation is delegated.

### The why-me file: compulsion over competence

Check 3's author-proxy deserves a permanent home: `research/why-me.md`, a running document — not per-premise, per *writer*. Three sections, maintained quarterly: **Obsessions** (the questions you keep re-entering — inheritance and escape; whether maintenance is love; institutions that outlive their purpose — write yours plainly, without justifying them); **Authority** (what you've *done or watched at close range* — jobs, families, subcultures, griefs — competence inventory, no resume padding); **Refusals** (what you won't sentimentalize, won't fake, won't write for the market — the negative space of your voice: "no villains whose only motive is money," "no small towns as therapy," etc.). When PC-08's proxy line runs, you paste three bullets from this file, not an improvised paragraph — improvised proxies drift toward flattering self-summary under session pressure; filed obsessions survive it.

The file's second job is *surprise-keeping*: quarterly re-reads show you which obsessions are decaying (fine — cross them out; tastes live) and which are *urgent in a way you can't yet place* (the keepers). Two practical guards: it is **research-folder material, not bible material** — it informs premise selection and why-me answers, never becomes story canon about *characters*; and it stays **private** — paste *excerpts* into prompts (the proxy field's three bullets), never the file itself (Chapter 5's paste-discipline: the whole document is more personal than any single premise needs). Writers who maintain one find Chapters 8–16 repeatedly handing it back to them: premise tests, logline tone checks, Chapter 16's "what's next after this book" — all answer faster when *why-me* was already written down somewhere other than the mood of a Tuesday.

### Five questions the stage always raises

Collected from a decade of workshop versions of this chapter — answered in the book's voice, briefly, because premature answers are how premises die:

**1. "My seed is tiny — too small for a premise?"** Size at the seed stage is *uninformative*; what matters is *pressure-capacity* (can the four parts be forced without breaking your constraints?). A tiny seed — a woman who won't use elevators — ladders into a premise the moment a *want with a deadline* attaches (she must reach the 14th floor to sign something before the building's sale closes — the phobia now *costs* something on a clock). The ladder *manufactures* size from pressure; the banks' constraint material supplies the specifics. What tiny seeds genuinely *can't* survive: capacity checks for *novel-scale* ambitions with no relational cast to staff them — then the honest verdict is "long short story" or "park with a size-note," not a bloated premise apologetically inflated into a form it won't fill. Scale is chosen at the skeleton stage (Ch 2's length-honesty) — here, seeds get *pressure*, and pressure decides.

**2. "How is this different from just… having ideas?"** It isn't, at the bottom — the techniques are *organization for an ordinary faculty*, not a substitute for one. The difference the stage adds is **judgment infrastructure**: unconstrained ideation produces and *stops*; the lab produces, *selects against written criteria, tests adversarially, verdicts, and files* — the verdict-and-filing part is what "just having ideas" never does, and it's where the months get protected. An ideas-person without the lab forgets; the lab's parked file, why-me file, and decisions rows are the *institutional memory of taste* — which is exactly why Chapter 16's "what next" questions get answered from documents instead of mood.

**3. "The stress-test keeps saying PARK — am I broken?"** No: PARK is *the test working* — most premises should park (your future shelf is *supposed* to outnumber your active project; one survivor at a time is the design from the chapter's first paragraph). Distinguish two failure modes though: **healthy parking** (specific findings: predictable-version problem, capacity break — the verdict *teaches*; ladder-lessons accumulate into better seeds) versus **vacuous parking** ("just not feeling it" with no PC-08 rows attached — the test wasn't run, a mood signed its name; run the test *before* believing the mood — sometimes the findings actually *clear* a premise the mood had buried, and the verdict flips to DEVELOP-with-repairs in ways vibes never would). If healthy parking repeats five times *on structurally different premises*, the smell is upstream: your *author-proxy* may be too thin for the checker to defend anything (check 3's mark) — go maintain the why-me file before the next lab, because a premise-stage curse is usually a *fuel*-stage quiet.

**4. "Should the logline come before the stress-test?"** Order matters and the chapter's sequence is load-bearing: premise → stress-test → logline, because the *logline's compression inherits the test's repairs* (name the posting *first*, then compress — compress first and you'll polish a sentence encoding the premise's *pre-repair* defects, then feel oddly loyal to it afterward: **premature loglines become sunk-cost poetry**, defended for aesthetic reasons long after their underlying premises were flagged for exactly what the pretty sentence hides). Exception that proves it: when a premise's *natural form* arrives as a phrase ("the tide she never learned to read" class of image) — capture it in the parked file or the skeleton's field-10 margin as *raw material*, but the *working* logline (the elevator sentence you steer by) still gets built post-verdict from the repaired four-part. Capture fragments freely; *steer* only by post-test sentences.

**5. "Can I develop two premises at once?"** For *full labs* (stress-tests, skeletons, beta-bound projects): no — one DEVELOP verdict at a time is the whole selection architecture (two active labs *will* contaminate each other's constraint banks and, worse, *split your adjudication attention* until both get vibes instead of verdicts). For *light ideation* (bank-maintenance, laddering seeds that stay untested, parked-file mining): yes, indefinitely — the parked file *runs on parallel curiosity by design*. The distinguishing line: **verdicts are serial; wandering is parallel.** The moment a second premise asks for a *stress-test appointment*, it has *also* asked to park its rival — schedule the test for *next month* (cooled premises test better anyway — recency-fandom distorts check 3's why-me answers), and meanwhile keep only one skeleton at a time in `manuscript/`'s hands. Chapter 16's 90-day plan will re-derive this rule from the *calendar's* side: projects consume *finishing-capacity*, and finishing-capacity, for every writer not yet finished once, is the scarcest resource the book exists to multiply.

### The parking ritual (killing darlings, step by step)

The stage's coldest procedure, specified so it stays a *habit* rather than a mood-dependent mercy: **(1)** verdict recorded in the decisions file with its top finding quoted (*why* parked — in the test's own words, not yours — future rereads need the *checker's* evidence, not your summary of it); **(2)** the one-liner logline written *from the four-part as-is* (not as-is-plus-hope: the parked line describes *what was tested*, so later PC-10 fusions don't inherit unstressed material); **(3)** bequest named if the test's verdict included PARK-AND-STEAL-*X* (the stolen element logged *in the receiving file's header* — "notary came from parked premise #7" — chains of custody for ideas, the graph Ch 16 will mine); **(4)** itch-flag set to *no* (monthly rereads re-rate it consciously — an auto-yes flags would just be today's mood pre-writing future ones); **(5)** folder discipline: `parked-ideas.md` grows-only, entries never edited *after* the fact (if a parked premise gets *re-tested* later, the new verdict is a *new row with a new date* — the file is append-only like the claim register, because a parking record edited in retrospect is a parking record that can't be trusted to have existed); **(6)** the *ritual's* closing act, psychologically load-bearing: **delete the draft fragments** (any half-opened scenes, note-dump files, or chat-transcripts developing the parked premise move to `cuts/` with the date and one line — parked premises keep their *ledger rows* and their *bequests*, not their *manuscript debris*): fragments exert a gravity that loglines don't, and half-written openings are precisely how "parked" quietly becomes "two projects at once with one starving" — the cut file's address makes the murder *reversible if truly needed* (and the append-only row means the *idea* was never at risk anyway — you're filing the body, not destroying it). Run the six steps in one twenty-minute sitting per verdict — the ceremony matters *more* than the duration: writers who ritualize parking park *more*, park *better*, and carry visibly smaller grave-shoulders into the next lab — while writers who "get around to it" accumulate a draft-folder of unkillable darlings that will, with certainty, arrive at Chapter 16's planning stage demanding to be finished first. Serial verdicts, parallel wandering, twenty minutes per murder, append-only mercy: the stage's whole emotional economy, stated once.

### Logline variants: the family of forms

The single logline has relatives — writers get asked for the wrong shape all the time and lose minutes improvising under pressure; here's the family, each defined by *what it hides less of*, all derived from *your* PC-09-refined sentence (the master version never leaves the folder):

**The elevator (25–35 words, one breath):** protagonist + goal + obstacle + cost, tonal signal implied — the Ch 8 anatomy at speaking speed; use: parties, casual introductions, the *first* answer to "what's it about?" (its test: say it aloud without tripping — written-smooth and spoken-smooth are different crafts; if the breath fails, a clause is carrying two slots, split it). **The query logline (~40–50 words):** elevator + *stake-amplifier* (the stakes sentence's existential rung, compressed) + *comps-adjacent framing* where genre-standard allows ("*X* meets *Y*" patterns — used sparingly, honestly, from books you've actually read — the Ch 16 pre-flight's packet citizen); use: query letter's opening, cover emails, submission forms asking "describe in one line" with a word budget that actually means *two-and-a-half lines*. **The back-cover (60–90 words, *present tense, hook-final*):** NOT a logline stretched — a different species: *setup beat → inciting disruption → ticking condition → final-line question*, characters named as the cover's tradition demands (Mara-first for literary, situation-first for thriller — genre's cover-conventions are real and Ch 16's category-research tells you *which* yours expects); the discipline carrying over: **every sentence earns its clause** (back-covers die of adjectives, not plot — the style sheet's banned-list applies *verbatim* to your own blurb, the self-application test being its own reward: writers whose back-covers pass PC-26's four-minute rung *understand* the de-AI lesson as readers now, not just writers); use: production-side, Ch 16's metadata line's parent. **The synopsis first line (~20 words):** goal + stakes + *the story's engine* (what kind of trouble, structurally — "must decide whether to sell her dead father's house before the coast takes it" — engine = inheritance-versus-place on a clock); use: synopsis headers, fellowship/grant forms, the *first sentence of every professional packet* (readers triaging slush read line one *before* line forty — this variant's stakes-per-word is the packet's highest-leverage twenty). **The internal compass (~15 words, *no character names*):** the *shape* only — "inheritance-versus-place on a storm-clock, ending with a signature not a rescue"; use: *private* — outline headers, beat-list margins, the done-criteria document's header — its no-names rule the point: compass-lines survive *character renames and structural surgery* (the name-free version stays *true* when the named version's gone stale — the instrument's resilience trick: **what you check daily should be robust to what you change weekly**). Maintenance rule for the whole family: **regenerate variants from the master *after every mint that changes the stakes-sentence*** (the changelog line's small print — variants are *derived artifacts*, stale variants being how back-covers end up describing *draft-07's* story while draft-11 ships: derived-artifact staleness is a *sync* problem, and sync problems this book solves with a line-item, not a virtue: "variants regenerated: [date]" — one row, the family kept honest at cadence).

## Pitfalls

> PITFALL — **Agreeable brainstorming.** *Symptom:* the assistant loves every direction ("excellent premise!"), suggests improvements that are really compliments, and the session ends with mood-boost but no decisions; your decisions file gains zero rows while the chat gains forty exchanges; alternative: the opposite overcorrection — you ask only "what's wrong with this?" and get a model that (without adversarial framing) softens the defects into "areas for development." *Why it happens:* Chapter 5's sycophancy in both configurations: unframed requests for opinions drift toward approval; unframed requests for critique drift toward *gentle* critique; and ideation sessions are the easiest place to forget that the machine's enthusiasm is *predicted text*, not assessment — it has read ten million "excellent premise" openers. *Fix:* adversarial frames as default: PC-08's ordered checks *require* findings with damage ratings; explicit bans ("no praise"; "phrase objections as the reader's exact words"); the author-proxy gives the model material to *check against* instead of you to flatter; and the standing loop rule — sessions end with verdicts (DEVELOP / PARK / PARK-AND-STEAL) written to the file, or the session didn't happen. *Prevention:* the adjudication-before-next-leg discipline — selection happens in silence, verdicts in writing, one seed per session (the three loop truths) — plus the weekly adjudication audit (Ch 7) which flags "forty exchanges, zero rows" as the sycophancy signature it is.

Related failure for Appendix C: **F-05, agreeable brainstorming with zero decisions** — this chapter's home entry; the fix stack (adversarial prompts, damage ratings, forced verdicts, one-seed sessions) is reused by Ch 9's outline critic and Ch 13's feedback triage.

## Key takeaways

- **Unconstrained ideation votes for the center; constrained ideation is authored.** Ladders (PC-07), fusions (PC-10), and a personal constraint bank turn "give me ideas" into narrowing work the model can labor at and only you can judge.
- **Stress-testing is cheap insurance with ordered checks:** stakes, novelty (against the three predictable versions), why-me/why-now (armed with your author-proxy so flattery has nothing to hold), capacity, and the killer question — each finding adjudicated by you, in writing, with damage ratings.
- **Kill at premise stage, not draft stage** — and make killing *constructive*: every park gets a logline, a date, and a named bequest; your graveyard's size is a taste metric, not a body count.
- **Loglines compress your own sentence, never the machine's:** anatomy diagnosis, six refinements, and *name what each hides* — you keep the compression reps and the honesty card.
- **The stage's outputs are the workflow's seed:** selected premise + tested logline + grown parked-file + bible syncs. Chapter 9 expands exactly this premise — nothing else — into an outline; if it's not selected yet, Chapter 9 has nothing to serve.
