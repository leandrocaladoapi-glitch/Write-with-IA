## Opening hook

"A story about a detective who discovers the killer is himself."

You have just read an idea. It is not a story. It has a genre, a twist, and exactly zero people in it — no name, no case, no reason the discovery should cost anyone anything. You cannot write the next scene from it because there is no next scene; there is only a fog with a twist-shaped shape at the center.

Now watch five minutes of honest work turn it into something writable. *Who* is the detective? A burnout named Ray Ferrara, three weeks from forced retirement. *What* does he want? To close one last case clean so his daughter will attend his retirement dinner. *What stands in the way*? Every线索 he follows buries evidence that implicates the version of himself he has spent a career denying — his old undercover identity, "Ferrara" to his captains and "Rook" to the crews. *What happens if he fails*? He retires into the same lie that hollowed out his marriage, and his daughter doesn't come.

Two sentences later we have a protagonist with a job and a wound, a goal with a deadline, an obstacle with irony built into it, and a cost that lives in a specific chair at a specific dinner table left empty. That transformation — idea to premise — is the entire first half of this chapter, and it is a skill you can run in twenty minutes on any number of premises until it becomes reflex. The second half builds the other two load-bearing walls: stakes (why the failure *matters*) and structure (how the failure *unfolds*). By the end of the chapter you will hold a one-page skeleton of your own story — the document every later stage of this book, AI-assisted or not, will lean on.

## Idea vs. premise

Craft writers have used several names for this distinction — situation versus story, premise versus idea — and the formulations differ in their corners, but the working consensus is stable enough to rely on: **an idea names a possibility; a premise states a conflict.** You can build a career's worth of ideas on a napkin; you cannot build a scene from one until it has the four parts below.

**The four parts.**

1. **Protagonist.** A specific person (or, rarely, a group treated as a person), defined enough that we know how *this* one would act differently from a generic stand-in. "A detective" is a job description. "A burnout three weeks from forced retirement who measures every case against the daughter he failed" is a protagonist: we can already predict the mistakes he will make.
2. **Goal.** Something the protagonist is trying to *get or do*, stated so a stranger could check a box when it happens. "Be happy" fails the check; "close one last case before his retirement dinner" passes it. Goals that can be verified on the page give your draft a direction of travel and your ending a target.
3. **Obstacle.** The force — a person, a system, a storm, the protagonist's own nature — that makes the goal hard *specifically for this protagonist*. Obstacles that the protagonist could sidestep with one honest conversation do not count; the obstacle must be welded to the goal.
4. **Stakes.** What is lost if the goal fails, made concrete enough to feel. Stakes get their own section below; for now, note they are the fourth required part, not an optional garnish.

**The cost-of-failure test.** The quick diagnostic: *can you state, in one plain sentence, what failure costs this person?* If you can, you have a premise; if you cannot, you still have an idea, no matter how original the fog around it feels. A second test, slightly harder: *could the story's ending be wrong?* Not vague — wrong. A premise worth 60,000 words must be capable of ending badly for your protagonist in a way you can imagine and might even prefer, because stories where failure is inconceivable have no weather.

**Premises are also portable.** The same conflict can be told in a noir novella, a stage play, or a family dinner conversation, and the four parts survive the trip. That portability is why every stage of this book's workflow keeps returning to your premise: the outline serves it, the draft tests it, and when a chapter of your draft drifts, the premise is the plumb line you drop.

> TRY IT — Take three ideas you already have (everyone has three) and run each through the four parts in one sentence each. Time yourself: five minutes total, ugly sentences allowed. Which idea produced the sharpest cost-of-failure sentence without you stretching? That instinct — reaching for the one that hurts clearest — is editorial taste operating preverbally. Learn to hear it.

## Stakes: the ladder

Stakes are not intensity; stakes are *consequence*. A writer can scream on every page and have no stakes at all, because volume is not loss. The reliable way to build real stakes is a ladder, rung by rung, from the concrete to the existential.

**The three rungs.**

- **Personal** — what happens to *the protagonist's own life, body, livelihood, or self-concept*. He loses the job, the hand, the retirement dinner, the last story he tells himself about who he is.
- **Relational** — what happens to the *people he is bound to*. The daughter doesn't come. The sister stops calling. The crew he vouched for goes down with him.
- **Existential** — what happens to the *ground the story stands on*: the town's water, the family's name, the self-story that organizes a life ("I am a man who protects people").

The ladder's use is procedural: for any scene or premise, climb *down* from the personal (easiest to write) toward the existential (hardest to earn), and deliberately ask "and then who else pays?" The first answer stays on the protagonist; the second rung is usually where beginners' stakes actually live and don't know it. In the Ray premise above, the personal rung is "case stays dirty"; the relational rung — which we put in the premise itself — is the empty chair; the existential rung, if we wanted to climb there, is that the whole undercover mythology his identity rests on was always the wrong story.

Let's run the ladder on Example A, since you will be living inside that story for the rest of this book. Mara wants the house sold — **personal rung:** if she fails to sell in time, she loses the survey posting she has arranged as her exit, and with it the city life she built *instead of* a family; if she forces the sale too hard, she walks away carrying what she always carries — the logistics triumph, the feeling of having managed grief rather than had it. **Relational rung:** Theo stops being her brother in any sense beyond shared documents; the last family structure their mother left (the house, the town, the habit of returning) is dissolved by the person who already left once. **Existential rung:** the story's ground-note — that some things are scheduled and some things must be stayed for — is answered wrong; Mara's organizing self-story ("I handle what others can't") wins again, and wins unchallenged for the rest of her life. Note what the ladder produced: the strongest material is not the money and not the storm; it's that *her way of surviving is the thing that fails*. When personal and existential rungs rhyme like that, you have found the story's real wager.

Two governing cautions:

> CRAFT NOTE — **Melodrama vs. stakes.** Melodrama is *big things happening to flat people*; stakes are *meaningful things happening to specific people with something to lose*. Escalation is not the ladder — a car bomb is not higher stakes than a dinner if nobody at the dinner matters to us yet. The ladder works by specificity of loss, not by pyrotechnics. When a reader shrugs at your disaster, the fire was never the problem; the relationship was.

> CRAFT NOTE — **Stakes must be active, not backstory.** "She has trauma" is a wound, not stakes. Stakes are what happens *next* if she fails *in this story's present*: she loses the house, the daughter, the vote, the chance. Backstory explains why a rung hurts; it is not the rung. Keep wounds on file (Chapter 3 builds the filing cabinet) and keep the scene's stakes on the page.

**The stakes sentence.** Practice condensing the ladder into one line with two clauses: "If he fails, [personal] — and worse, [relational or existential]." Ray: *If he fails, he retires into the same lie that hollowed out his marriage — and his daughter doesn't come.* Keep this sentence in your skeleton; when Chapter 12 asks whether your climax delivers, this line is the invoice.

## Structure that survives beginners

Structure is the order in which the reader learns what happens — not the order events technically occur, but the order *you* deliver them, and the pressure curve that delivery creates. Three shapes appear often enough in the craft canon to be worth your working knowledge. None is a law; all are proven designs you can draft inside before you learn to bend them.

**1. Three-act shape (practical, not dogmatic).** Set-up → confrontation → resolution, with a turn into the middle and a turn into the end. In beginner-safe terms:

- **Act I — orientation (roughly the first quarter).** We meet the protagonist in a *defining situation* (not their average Tuesday — their particular Tuesday), a disturbance arrives, and by the act's end the protagonist has made a choice that cannot be unmade, committing them to the fight. This closing commitment is the act's real job; a first act that ends in mere *incident* ("he noticed something") leaves Act II floating.
- **Act II — pressure (the middle half).** Attempts to reach the goal meet escalating resistance; each attempt changes the protagonist's situation and understanding. The middle's classic failure — the "Act II hole" you will hunt in Chapter 9 — happens when events accrue without changing anything: activity without consequence. The fix is always the ladder: every scene should move a rung or move the map.
- **Act III — decision under pressure (the last quarter).** The forces gathered through Act II collide; the protagonist acts from who they have *become* rather than who they were; the stakes sentence is paid in one direction or the other.

Attribution matters: the three-act shorthand popularized by modern screenwriting handbooks is a *teaching compression* of older structural observations, not a natural law — experienced writers routinely violate its ratios, and some celebrated stories ignore it entirely. Use it the way you would training wheels: as a shape you can trust while balance develops.

**2. Fichtean curve (crisis-first).** Named for a German dramaturgical tradition and popularized in modern craft teaching, this shape replaces gentle set-up with a rising sequence of crises: story opens *in media res* or near it, difficulty escalates through compounding confrontations, a **crisis** point forces the protagonist to their worst choice, a brief **climax** resolves it, and **falling action** shows the new equilibrium. It suits writers who find Act I slow and stories whose engine is escalating jeopardy — mysteries, thrillers, situational farces. Its trap is the inverse: no orientation means readers who don't yet care about whom to fear for. If you draft Fichtean, you owe your first pages extra work making the protagonist legible fast.

**3. Kishōtenketsu (orientation, development, twist, reconciliation).** A four-part narrative structure with roots in Chinese and Japanese literary tradition (*ki-shō-ten-ketsu*: introduction, elaboration, turn, conclusion), widely used in East Asian storytelling and increasingly taught to Western writers as an alternative that does **not** require conflict as its central engine: the *ten* (twist) reframes what the first two parts meant, and harmony of the whole matters more than escalation. For a beginner it is worth knowing for two practical reasons. First, it offers a legitimate exit when your story is about *reframing* rather than *winning* — many literary shorts and slice-of-life sequences are kishōtenketsu in bones. Second, borrowing its twist mechanic ("the middle section means something different than you assumed") is a reliable rescue for sagging Act II material in any structure. Contemporary Western "four-act" beat sheets are related in spirit but not identical in lineage; don't confuse a marketing template with the tradition.

**Beginnings that orient.** Whatever the shape, your opening has three jobs in miniature: *who* (which person we are anchored to), *where* (the defining situation's texture), and *what's wrong* (the disturbance or lack that gives the story somewhere to push). That's it — no prologues required, no world's history, no weather report that isn't pressure. If a stranger can't answer those three questions by the end of page one, orient harder.

**Endings that resonate.** A resonant ending pays the stakes sentence and answers the story's central dramatic question — then, ideally, steps *one inch* past it, showing the new equilibrium in a concrete image rather than explaining the lesson. Resonance is earned by setup: Chapter 9 will show you how to plant the ending's requirements early enough that the close feels inevitable *and* surprising. Beware the two classic counterfeits: the **abrupt stop** (we simply run out of story) and the **tacked-on epilogue** (the story proper ended 30 pages ago; the epilogue is apology).

> REALITY CHECK — No structure guarantees engagement. These shapes are compression devices for decisions you still have to make scene by scene. If your skeleton looks perfect and your pages are dull, the problem is upstream: stakes specificity or character desire. Structure charts cannot fix a protagonist who doesn't want badly enough.

### Choosing a shape: a working decision table

Rather than abstract allegiance, choose by what your story's engine actually is:

| If your story's engine is… | Start with | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| A pressure chamber closing in (limited time/place, escalating conflict) | Three-act | The Act II hole — scenes that add events but change nothing |
| Immediate jeopardy, trouble from line one | Fichtean curve | Under-orientation: readers who don't yet know whom to fear for |
| Reframing — what you saw in parts 1–2 means something new | Kishōtenketsu | Forcing conflict where the tradition invites reflection |
| A character study of inner change | Three-act or kishōtenketsu | External plot bolted on as decoration |

Change columns later if the draft argues — outlines serve drafts, as Chapter 9 will hammer. What you must not do is stay *unconscious* of your choice, because then the story will fight itself: a Fichtean engine in a three-act template feels rushed at the start and padded in the middle, which is the most common structural sickness this chapter exists to prevent.

A concrete orientation pair, before we move on. Weak opening work: *"It was a rainy Tuesday in Bellhaven, a town with a complicated history, when Margaret received a letter she didn't expect."* — no protagonist yet (Margaret is a name, not a person in a defining situation), no texture of *her* life, no disturbance that costs anything (an unexpected letter is a genre sticker). Same information, oriented: *"Margaret had opened the shop alone for eleven months, and she had just locked up when the letter slid under the door — her sister's handwriting, which meant their mother was dead or dying, and the shop's only delivery was due at six."* — who (a woman who runs a shop alone and counts on it), where (her shop, her solitary routine, specified by 'eleven months' and 'six'), what's wrong (a sister's handwriting that only carries two meanings). Three jobs, one sentence-cluster, no history of Bellhaven required. That ratio — specificity over coverage — is the whole orientation craft.

## The one-page story skeleton

Everything above now condenses into one page — a document you will fill by hand for every story, because the act of fitting your idea into it *is* the testing. Use this template verbatim (a printable copy lives with the companion materials; retype it if you prefer — retype it once and you'll never forget the fields):

```text
STORY SKELETON — [working title]                          v[__]

1. PROTAGONIST: name, age, situation, defining trait (one sentence).
2. WANT (external, checkable): what they are trying to GET or DO.
3. NEED (internal, usually opposite to the want): what would actually
   heal or teach them.  [Ch 3 develops this field.]
4. OBSTACLE: the force welded to the want — who or what says no,
   and why it must be THIS obstacle for THIS person.
5. STAKES SENTENCE: If they fail, [personal] — and worse, [relational/
   existential].
6. STRUCTURE CHOICE: three-act | Fichtean | kishōtenketsu (one line
   of why).
7. DISTURBANCE: the event that breaks the defining situation.
8. POINT OF NO RETURN: the choice (not accident) that locks them in.
9. CRISIS/MIDPOINT: the worst-pressure moment; what changes after it.
10. CLIMAX PREVIEW: the collision the ending must contain — one
    sentence, no choreography.
11. ENDING PREVIEW: the closing image or action, one sentence.
12. POV & TENSE: close third past | first present | … (Ch 4 locks this.)
```

Twelve fields, one page, no scene numbering — deliberately. The skeleton is a *premise-level* document: it proves the story can stand up before you spend months leaning on it. Beat-level outlining is Chapter 9's job, with the machine's help; doing it now would be like drawing floor plans for a house with no foundation.

**How long should drafting the page take?** Fifteen to forty minutes for a first pass, then a day of background simmering, then one revision pass. If a field refuses to fill — if you cannot write the stakes sentence or preview a climax — that refusal is diagnostic: the story is telling you where it is soft, before it costs you 40,000 words.

### The stakes sentence: a drill and its sub-drills

The stakes sentence gets its own practice block because it carries the outline's weight and *fails* in ways naming helps. **Drill 1 — the cost-of-failure ladder run, aloud:** take any draft stakes sentence and climb it three rungs by asking the *specific* follow-up each time (the pattern from the chapter's stakes section, compressed to a script): "The sale fails → she loses *what exactly?* — the house (rung 1: personal/property); → and *who else* is affected? — Theo gains a sibling who resents him for gaining (rung 2: relational — note it cuts *toward* the survivor too, the ladder's usual direction); → and what story about herself stops being tellable? — 'the one who kept things' dies with the keys (rung 3: existential — the *self-story as infrastructure* line from the ladder table)." **The drill's checkpoint:** if any rung arrives in *abstractions* ("her identity," "the family legacy") rather than *pictures* (the keys, the ledger, the notice on the door), the rung is fake — redo it until you could *photograph* the loss. **Drill 2 — the swap test:** paste your stakes sentence over a *different* story's premise-summary (any thriller's, any romance's) — if it still "works" (no edits needed), it's **template stakes**: the tell is generic loss-language ("everything she's worked for," "more than just a job") that any protagonist could wear; fix by re-running Drill 1 on *your* character's actual rungs until the sentence *can't* leave its own story (swap test fails = specific). **Drill 3 — the skeptic's one question** (the stake-sentence's PC-08 preview): read it to someone (or play skeptic yourself) and take *only this* question: "why doesn't she just…?" — every "just" is a **missing obstacle-justification** (she can't walk away because *what*, specifically? — the loan terms, the notary's letter, Theo's silence — whatever *binds* her is the stakes sentence's hidden fourth element surfacing under pressure); if the honest answer is "nothing, she could just," **you don't have stakes yet — you have a situation**, and the chapter's earlier reframe ("bad premise = situation without checkable goal AND/OR stakes without binding cost") just told you *which* half broke. **Drill 4 — the compression round** (runs only after Drills 1–3 pass): three sentences → two → one, *each cut tested for rung-loss* ("did that clause carry a rung? — if yes, restore it: the sentence is already as short as its *load-bearing* parts allow"). The four drills in sequence = the sentence's full quality-assurance: ladder (substance), swap (specificity), skeptic (binding), compression (craft) — and the whole sequence takes eleven minutes on a first draft of the sentence, which is eleven minutes against the outline's *most-quoted, least-inspected* line item: the line beta readers will later quote *back* to you as "the reason I cared" — worth the drill, every time.

### Where premises actually go to die (five funnels, five fixes)

A field guide to the chapter's promise ("premises die in specific ways") drawn from years of workshop outcomes — each funnel with its *signature* and the stage's built-in fix, so you recognize which cliff you're approaching *before* the view changes:

**Funnel 1 — the enthusiasm cliff.** Signature: premise written on a high (late-night energy, one great image), skeleton started next week, first gap between *vision* and *draft-reality* discovered — and the project quietly ends not with a rejection but with *no sessions booked*. Root cause: **the emotional high got recorded as a *decision* without a *capacity check*** (the four-part's "can this survive my actual life?" question skipped — high-energy sessions are *terrible* capacity judges; they over-promise on behalf of your tired self). Fix already in the machinery: **PC-08's capacity-check row + the no-exceptions entry conditions from Part III's opening** (a premise that survives the stress-test has *signed* for the tired version of you — the check-4-constraints work double duty: it protects the *outline* and protects *enthusiasm from its own amnesia*). Prevention habit: never let a night's excitement mint anything above `parked` — *parking is the enthusiasm-capacity handshake* (the file's itch-flag + re-rate rule is exactly this funnel's speed bump, timed for the month when memory has softened the high).

**Funnel 2 — the research swamp.** Signature: skeleton exists, but sessions convert *one-by-one* into "just looking things up" — the project becomes a reading list with a title page; deliverable count approaches zero while `research/` grows fat. Root cause: **research-as-avoidance wearing research-as-craft's clothes** (the writing-session's resistance gets *licensed* by genuinely-useful information-gathering — the swamp's cruelty: the swamp-feel good, *look* responsible, and the *draft file stays cold*; also the generator-raw-material trap in costume — the tool's eager research-assist makes the swamp *faster to enter and harder to notice*). Fix already in the machinery: **Ch 5's type-split at scheduling level** (research questions get *slots* — a ninety-minute weekly block, not *every* session's first tab) + **the why-me/why-now-research protocol's bounded form** (perishables get dated, *closed* questions — "the harbor authority's 2024 staffing rules: two primary sources, done Friday" — research ends when *the question* ends, not when curiosity retires — one *bounded* question per swamp-day, then the draft's FILL slots get fed from what arrived). Prevention signature: if `research/` added three files this week and `manuscript/` added zero paragraphs, the audit's *ratio* line fires — the same instrument logic as word-counts-without-adjudications: **motion without output in the *target* channel is the swamp's humidity reading.**

**Funnel 3 — the shiny-new-premise diffusion.** Signature: three projects' *ideation-stage* tabs open in rotation; each session's energy split three ways; *all three* progress at the pace of "almost started" — the parked-file's discipline violated *upward* (premises not parked but *kept warm* — the diffusion's vanity: "I'm working on three things" usually means "I'm *maintaining* three almost-things"); root cause: **the one-DEVELOP-verdict-at-a-time rule from Ch 8 flouted because *ideation sessions don't feel like work*** (the lab's serial-verdicts rule exists precisely because *wandering is parallel but finishing is serial* — two active skeletons in `manuscript/` = two voices in the sessions, each contaminating the other's constraint banks until both drafts sound like *neither*); fix already in the machinery: **Ch 8's five-questions answer #5** ("verdicts are serial; wandering is parallel" — the second premise asking for a stress-test *has asked its rival to park*) + **the itch re-rating ritual** (unwanted warmth for parked items dies at monthly review pace; kept-warm premises *never* get that review — they just *stay warm in the calendar's shadow*, consuming Sunday-audit attention that belongs to the one ACTIVE); prevention signature: **the folder test** — `manuscript/` contains ONE project's `draft-current` *or* the project in most-recent-mint position, full stop; a second skeleton in the same folder is the diffusion's *first symptom* — move it to `parked-ideas.md`'s care before the next session, not after the quarter's self-reproach.

**Funnel 4 — the never-quite-ready polish-loop.** Signature: *v8 outline*, *v14 character sheets*, beat-lists redrafted for weeks — **zero words of prose committed** — the project's revision-axis achievements mounting while its *time-to-first-page* metric sits at infinity; root cause: **perfectionism colonizing the pre-draft stages** (outline-worship's cousin — the *document* gets polished because documents can be polished *without risking the draft*, and the draft risks everything: the moment "discovery through drafting" dies, so does the fun; also the feedback-loop-absence problem: sheets have no *reader* to disappoint, and the ego's risk-aversion *smells* the safety); fix already in the machinery: **Part III's no-exceptions entry conditions + Ch 12's lesson previewed early** (the *entry* conditions' design: outline's stake-sentence checkable, sheets' fields 1/3/5/6 filled, level-3 beats for *session one* — three boxes, *then the gate closes*: entry conditions exist to be *passed*, not *polished* — a v8 outline with zero mints is a machine whose *only* output is self-assurance) + **the deadlines-hidden-in-reality reframe** (two-week sale clock in the *story* — build yourself one: a beta-reader's date, a class's submission window, a contest's — *external dates are the polish-loop's fire alarm* — schedule it the day the outline hits v2, before comfort negotiates it later). Prevention signature: **the no-prose-week alert** (session logs showing two consecutive weeks of bible-work only → the audit auto-flags it — instrumentation again: the *metric* isn't "outline quality," it's "words-at-draft-stage per week > 0" — a number your logs already track, waiting for you to *treat it as the health reading it always was*).

**Funnel 5 — the mid-draft collapse of belief.** Signature (the cruelest — it strikes *after* the entry conditions): Act II's sag arrives *experientially* — the writer stops believing *their own stakes sentence* mid-draft; sessions slow to bargaining ("I'll finish if it's good"); the outline's promises start reading like *lies you told yourself*; root cause: **the stakes' *initial* substance was real but *un-rehearsed under draft-pressure*** (the sentence's rungs were checked in *outline-mood*, not *draft-mood* — and Part III's middle is where every writer discovers which of their rungs were *authored* versus *assembled* — the gap the Ch 12 "belief-check: does the promise still feel true" line item exists to catch at pass-1, but *some writers meet it first* in week six of drafting, unbuffered); fix already in the machinery: **re-run the chapter's Drill 1 on the *draft's* current state** (the ladder works mid-draft — "the sale fails → what, now that I've written ch1–6? — what *specifically* have I promised the reader by page 140 that the failure would destroy? — THAT's the stakes sentence's current rung-set — usually *sharper* than the outline's original, because the draft made promises the pre-draft sentence couldn't know") + **the F-07 diagnosis's sister question** ("am I doubting the *premise* or doubting my *draft-rewards*? — if the former: the outline needs a v-next *repair* (legitimate, logged); if the latter: the doubt belongs to *altitude-confusion* — structure-pass now, line-pass later, one job at a time — belief in the *project* survives the *pass* schedule when the schedule's kept, and dies *inside* the schedule when each pass' jobs get *stacked onto every session*"). The chapter's closing line, earned twice now: **the premise that survives drafting is the one whose costs were *sized to your own capacity* — every funnel above is some variant of that sizing missing; the drills, checks, and entry conditions aren't bureaucracy — they're the sizing tools.**

## Worked example: Example A's skeleton, v1

Let's build the skeleton for the running example that will carry Part III. **Example A** is a literary-contemporary short story, "Low Tide," which you will watch move from premise (Ch 8) to outline (Ch 9) to drafted scenes (Ch 10–11) to revision (Ch 12) to beta feedback (Ch 13). Filling the skeleton now — unaided, before any AI enters the room — is the book practicing what it preaches: the aims exist first, so that later the machine proposes against them rather than instead of them.

```text
STORY SKELETON — "Low Tide"                               v1

1. PROTAGONIST: Mara Solano, 36, hydrographic surveyor who compiles
   tide tables for a maritime authority; treats every problem —
   grief included — as logistics to be managed.
2. WANT: sell the family house on the cliff in Porto Alvaro and
   return to her city job within two weeks.
3. NEED: stay long enough to grieve her mother WITH her brother —
   and admit why she stopped coming home.
4. OBSTACLE: her brother Theo refuses to sell and won't explain why;
   the buyer (Casi Duarte) needs the deal before the season;
   a February storm keeps everyone inside the same house.
5. STAKES SENTENCE: If Mara forces the sale, she gets her clean
   exit — and loses the last chance to be a sister to Theo; if she
   stalls too long, she loses the survey posting that is the life
   she chose instead.
6. STRUCTURE: three-act — the house and the storm give me a natural
   pressure chamber; Fichtean would rush the grief.
7. DISTURBANCE: the notary's letter — probate requires both signatures
   by month's end — arrives the same day Mara does.
8. POINT OF NO RETURN: Mara finds their mother's tide journal and
   realizes Theo has been reading it for months — she can't leave
   without knowing what's in it.
9. CRISIS/MIDPOINT: power dies in the storm; by flashlight the
   brothers' fight surfaces the real story: their mother set a third
   place at the table for years, waiting; Mara's unanswered calls.
10. CLIMAX PREVIEW: alone in the kitchen with the journal and Casi's
    contract, Mara must sign or not sign — with Theo watching from
    the doorway.
11. ENDING PREVIEW: Mara hands Theo the keys to keep — she's taking
    the survey posting, but she's coming back for the equinox; the
    tide table for March hangs unfinished on the wall.
12. POV/TENSE: close third past, Mara only.
```

**Annotated: where each element came from.**

- **Fields 1–3 arrived together** as one image: a woman whose *job* is predicting the sea's schedule, unable to schedule her own grief. The profession was chosen *because* it rhymes with the flaw (control over chaos) — when your protagonist's job and wound echo, half your symbolism is pre-installed. Field 3's NEED is already in tension with field 2's WANT; that tension is the story's engine (Chapter 3 builds it formally).
- **Field 4 stacks three obstacles** — human (Theo), clock (Casi's deadline), weather (the storm) — so pressure has redundancy. Note none is a mustache-twirler: Theo's refusal is love without vocabulary; Casi is business. Literary stories die when the obstacle is cosmically evil and survive when everyone is understandable and wrong.
- **Field 5 names both directions of failure.** The stakes sentence has a *both-ways-lose* shape, which is what makes the climax a decision rather than a tally. "The life she chose instead" quietly plants field 3: her city posting isn't neutral background, it's the counter-bet.
- **Field 8 is a discovery, not an accident** — Mara *chooses* to open the journal (curiosity she can't log away), so the point of no return preserves agency, as the pattern requires.
- **Fields 10–11 preview without choreography.** "Sign or not sign, with Theo watching" defines the collision; the unfinished tide table as closing image ties the profession-symbol to the resolution — she leaves the schedule open, the anti-logistics ending. Whether the short story earns that image is now a *testable question*, which is exactly what a skeleton is for.
- **What's deliberately missing:** scene order, subplots, dialogue, the mother's journal contents. Premise-level fields only. The machine never sees this document until Chapter 8, where it will stress-test it; if it survives *that*, Chapter 9 turns it into beats.

### Skeletons at short lengths

Everything above assumes room to move — a novel or a full short story. What about flash fiction, or the 1,200-word story a magazine will actually consider from a beginner? The fields do not disappear; they *compress into single decisions*. The four-part premise becomes one sentence that already contains its own stakes ("Mara has two weeks to sell the house her brother refuses to leave"). The structure choice collapses to a single turn: many flash stories are, in effect, a kishōtenketsu *ten* in miniature — a reframing so fast it feels like a cut — or a three-act shape with each act reduced to a paragraph. Fields 10 and 11 (climax preview, ending preview) become nearly the same sentence, because at short lengths the last line *is* the collision's residue.

The discipline that never compresses: **the cost-of-failure sentence** and **the choice at the climax**. If your 800-word story cannot name both, it is a vignette — a moment with a mood, which is a legitimate thing to write, but not yet a story with an ending you designed. Label it honestly in your notes ("vignette on purpose") rather than discovering the distinction from a rejection letter. And do fill *something*: even flash gets a five-field skeleton — protagonist, want, obstacle, turn, residue. The fifteen-minute habit scales down to four minutes; only the page count shrinks.

One more short-form caution: compressed structures rely on the reader *supplying* connective tissue — implied wants, assumed stakes, relationships entered mid-argument. At novel length you control that supply; at flash length you are betting on the reader's generosity. Choose generic situations carefully (a fight in a kitchen reads as "siblings" faster than a fight in a customs hall), because every field you cannot state explicitly must be instantly guessable from the situation itself. When the Stretch asks you to skeleton your story, write the length you are actually aiming at — the skeleton for a 2,000-word story and the skeleton for a 90,000-word novel share fields, not sizes.

> CRAFT NOTE — Skeletons are cheap; drafts are expensive. Writers resist fifteen-minute documents because the story feels more magical unfilled. The magic argument has it backwards: every experienced writer you admire is, underneath the mystique, someone who tested cheaply and often. Fill the page. If the page is boring, you have lost half an hour. If you skip it and the novel stalls at 30,000 words, you have lost a year.

### When a field refuses to fill

Every stalled skeleton has a diagnosis. The refusals cluster in three fields, and each one means something specific — read the failure as a message rather than a mood.

**Field 2 (want) stays vague.** "She wants a better life" will not fill because it is not a want; it is a *result* of wants. Force the checkable version: what would a camera record as success — the signed contract, the verdict, the ticket bought? If no camera-checkable want exists, your story may be a mood rather than a plot, and that is a legitimate literary choice *only on purpose*. Say so in field 6 ("character study; want is non-external by design") and stop fighting the field.

**Field 5 (stakes sentence) stays flat.** You are probably stuck on one rung. Writers in love with their plot often cannot name a relational loss because no relationship is loaded yet — fix by adding one (the daughter, the sister, the crew) *before* the outline stage, because retrofitting relationships in Chapter 12 is surgery with the patient awake. Writers in love with their themes often cannot name a personal loss because the protagonist is a symbol wearing a name — fix by giving them a body, a livelihood, and one embarrassing daily routine that failure would take away.

**Field 10 (climax preview) stays vague.** "They confront the truth" is a theme, not a collision. A climax preview needs parties and a choice: *who* is in the room, *what* binary decision stands before them, and *what* each choice costs. If you can fill field 10 only after inventing a new character or event, your middle was missing its engine all along — good to know now; Chapter 9 will rebuild Act II around the collision the climax actually requires.

There is no shame in a skeleton that takes three passes; there is only expensive shame in a skeleton never attempted. The right response to a refusal is ten more minutes, not twelve more weeks of hopeful drifting.

## Exercises

- **Try (15 min):** Ten premises in twenty minutes (start your timer; ugly is fine). Pick one seed word — a place, a job, a fear — and generate ten one-sentence premises that each contain all four parts. Force different genres across the ten. Do not stop to judge; judging happens after the timer. Then circle the two that produce the sharpest cost-of-failure sentences. Quantity first works because it demotes your first idea (usually the generic one) and forces the search past it — the same principle behind every ideation prompt in Chapter 8.

- **Stretch (1–2 h):** Take your best premise from the Try and build its full stakes ladder: write the personal rung, the relational rung, and the existential rung as three separate "If they fail…" sentences; choose the strongest pairing for your stakes sentence. Then fill all twelve skeleton fields for your story. Walk away for thirty minutes; return and revise only fields 4, 5, and 10 — the obstacle, the stakes, the climax preview. If any of the three still feels soft after the second pass, swap the premise now, while it costs a page instead of a book.

- **Project (ongoing):** Skeleton an old favorite film — from memory, no pausing, no looking up. One page, all twelve fields, ten minutes. Compare your filled fields against the actual film: where did your memory *invent* structure? This drill installs the shape in reflexes faster than any diagram; repeat it with a short story you love after Chapter 4, and with a friend's anecdote at a coffee shop. Within a month, you will hear premises differently at parties — the fields fill in automatically, and weak ones stop surviving your attention.

## Pitfalls

> PITFALL — **Mistaking plot for story.** *Symptom:* you can list what happens — the letters, the trial, the heist — but the summary bores you when said aloud; scenes accrete without the reader caring which way it goes; your stakes sentence comes out as events ("things fall apart") rather than losses ("he loses X, and worse, Y"). *Why it happens:* events are visible and therefore feel like progress; costs are invisible and therefore get deferred — often forever, into a sequel, into backstory, into "the reader will sense it." *Fix:* run the ladder on your existing events. For each major event, write who pays, in what currency (personal, relational, existential), and by how much; delete or merge events that pay nothing. Then rewrite the stakes sentence from the payments, not the happenings. *Prevention:* the skeleton's field 5 is required, never blank; in Chapter 9 every beat outline must state which rung each beat moves. A story is not what happens; it is what happens *to whom it matters*.

Related failure for Appendix C: scenes that feel busy but weightless — symptom feeds F-03 (draft-structure mismatches) when left unaddressed.

## Checklist

> CHECKLIST — You're ready to move on when:
> - [ ] You can state a premise in two sentences containing all four parts — protagonist, goal, obstacle, stakes.
> - [ ] Your stakes sentence has both clauses (failure costs *this*, and worse, *that*).
> - [ ] A one-page skeleton exists — for Example A as modeled, and for your own story after the Stretch.
> - [ ] You can name your protagonist's cost of failure without rereading your notes.
> - [ ] You chose a structure on purpose (three-act / Fichtean / kishōtenketsu), and can say in one line why.

## Key takeaways

- **Idea versus premise:** an idea names a possibility; a premise states a conflict with a specific protagonist, a checkable goal, a welded obstacle, and concrete stakes. The cost-of-failure sentence is the test.
- **Stakes are consequences, not volume.** Climb the ladder — personal → relational → existential — by asking "and then who else pays?" Specificity of loss beats pyrotechnics every time.
- **Three structures are proven designs, not laws:** three-act for pressure-chamber stories, Fichtean when crises must escalate from page one, kishōtenketsu when the engine is reframing rather than conflict. Train inside one; bend it later.
- **The one-page skeleton is the chapter's deliverable** and the workflow's foundation: twelve fields, premise-level only, with the obstacle, stakes, and climax preview carrying the most weight.
- **Test cheaply, test early.** A soft field discovered now costs minutes; the same soft field discovered in Chapter 12 costs months. Fill the page.
