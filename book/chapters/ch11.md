## Opening hook

Meet three AI-generated characters. **Kai**, a 300-year-old starship engineer with combat scars: kind, dry-witted, fiercely loyal, haunted by a loss he discusses with wry understatement. **Rosa**, a village healer who has seen too much: kind, gently sarcastic, fiercely devoted to her people, haunted by a loss she mentions with wry understatement. **Inspector Nkechi**, who has solved every case: kind, sharp-tongued, fiercely loyal to her unit, haunted by a case she discusses with —

You see it. Three biographies, one person — and not even an interesting person: agreeable, competent, faintly wounded, *available at every party*. This is the machine's character default, and it has a name in this book: **the helpful intern** — the model's statistical average of "character who would be pleasant to work with," surfaced whenever character-generation prompts lean on adjectives ("interesting," "complex," "strong backstory") instead of on *pressure*. The intern shows up in first drafts even from good writers, because the same forces that make models agreeable conversational partners (Chapter 5's sycophancy; Chapter 8's F-05) make their *characters* agreeable too: likability has mass, and mass is where generation lands.

This chapter is the counter-programming. You already own the structural side (Chapter 3: sheets, flaw chains, arcs — the machine never designs those without you). Here the craft is *developmental*: interviews that extract usable behavior instead of horoscopes (PC-17), arc tracking across a draft that spans weeks of your life (PC-19), dialogue voice tests that catch the intern *before* beta readers do (PC-18) — plus the flattening problem stated openly, with countermeasures, and Example B's genre scenes as the demonstration ground where three *genuinely* different voices get built on the page. By the end: each major character in your project passes the blind voice test, your arc tracker covers every drafted chapter, and nobody in your cast sounds like they'd be nice about your fixes.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- [ ] Run productive AI character interviews — and know their limits.
- [ ] Track arcs across chapters with an arc tracker (PC-19).
- [ ] Differentiate dialogue voices across your cast (PC-18) with diction/syntax/rhythm levers.
- [ ] Catch stereotype drift and agreeable-average flattening — and name the countermeasures.

## Interviews and backstory

The **character interview** is the format beginners reach for first: ask the character questions, receive a person. Done naively, it produces the intern with a questionnaire (Q: "What are you afraid of?" A: "Losing the people I love" — said every kind character ever). Done *with pressure*, it produces scenes' worth of usable behavior. The difference is entirely in the prompt's design:

> PROMPT CARD PC-17 — Character interviewer
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-17 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic, no model-specific syntax -->
> Role: interviewer for a FICTION character IN SCENE — you ask the
> questions; the CHARACTER answers in first person; you also note
> what the answer refuses.
> Context: character sheet below (fields 1,5,6,10 minimum) + the
> specific upcoming scene's pressure (what's about to happen to
> them).
> Rules for answers: (1) every answer must include ONE concrete
> detail a camera could film (an object, a habit, a location);
> (2) no abstract self-diagnosis words (afraid of "loss" is
> forbidden — say what they'd actually do at the graveside); (3) if
> the question could be answered kindly by anyone, the character
> must answer it *specifically unkindly or specifically wrongly* —
> their flaw speaks first; (4) 60-word cap per answer.
> Ask me 8 questions, ONE AT A TIME — wait for my reply roleplaying
> the answer, then judge: "in-character / drifting to intern /
> contradicts sheet field __" and correct before the next question.
> After Q8: output the THREE answers most usable for the scene and
> ONE refusal the character wouldn't touch (and why they wouldn't).
> ```

Three design decisions carry the card. **Interactive, one question at a time:** batching eight questions invites eight generic answers (each prompt-line gets thin attention as stacks grow — Chapter 5's task-stacking); the turn-by-turn format forces *sustained* persona under pressure and lets you correct drift **at Q2** instead of discovering it at Q8 ("drifting to intern" is the card's own verdict vocabulary — make the model audit its own conformity to your sheet; it's fallible at this, and even fallible self-audits raise your attention, which is the real sensor). **Concrete-or-nothing:** the camera test (rule 1) converts personality-abstractions into *behavior inventory* — answers you can paste into beats, not wiki entries; the 60-word cap keeps answers scene-sized (unbounded answers drift into theme statements — the intern's habitat). **The flaw speaks first (rule 3):** the single most valuable rule — generic questions retrieve generic *kindness*; requiring the flaw to answer first ("What do you do the night before the hearing?" → *she re-times the ferry in her calendar six times* not *she hopes for the best*) mines exactly the pattern-charged behavior Chapter 3's chain needs.

**Interview limits — stated plainly (REALITY CHECK below has the full version):** the character on the other side is *the model completing a persona built from your sheet plus its priors* — answers are **ideation material, not canon** until adjudicated: every Q&A run passes through the sheet (does this contradict fields 5/6/10? then it's discarded or the sheet changes *deliberately* via changelog) and through the bible. What interviews are genuinely good for: **stress-testing your sheet's behavioral implications** (you wrote "flaw: converts emotion to logistics" — now watch it *answer a graveside question with calendar talk* — if that answer feels dead, your pattern sentence was too abstract, fix the sheet *now*), **mining concrete details** (rule 1's camera objects become set dressing with fingerprints — the soup-pot's cousin), and **pre-writing scene-specific responses** (run the interview *with the upcoming scene's pressure as context* — you're not interviewing "Theo" in general; you're interviewing him the night before the December conversation, which is a different person than Tuesday-Theo — character interviews should almost always be *situational*, one scene deep, not biographical, life deep).

**Backstory on demand vs. on need.** The twin failure modes: *on demand* = interviewing for a complete life (childhood, schooling, three loves) before a single scene — archaeology that outruns the story, most of it never load-bearing (Ch 3's "every field must cause or explain choices" — interview answers inherit that law: **each backstory answer must name the present beat it will touch or it goes in `cuts/`**); *on need* = waiting until a scene *requires* history and then improvising it cold — cheaper, but the improvisation happens inside drafting, where the desk's priors shape it (the machine's "tender orphan backstory" defaults reach you through your own typing hand). The working synthesis: **need-scheduled, demand-limited** — the outline knows *where* wounds get dosed (Ch 3's schedule: journal beat, fight beat), so *those* points trigger short situational interviews (2–4 questions each, scene-pressured, camera-tested), and the answers cap at what the beat consumes. Unconsumed answers don't rot in the bible — they file in `research/backstory-raw.md`, non-canonical until a future beat earns them (the Ch 7 lore-promotion rule, character edition).

> TRY IT — Run PC-17 on your protagonist with *one* upcoming scene's pressure in the context line. Eight turns, roleplaying their answers yourself (you know them best — you're the world's foremost expert on your character, which is *why* the interview's rules are yours to enforce). Time-box: fifteen minutes. Harvest: three scene-usable details + one refusal. If all three details could appear in anyone's story, your sheet's field 5 was an adjective — go strengthen it *before* the next interview.

## Arcs across chapters

Chapters 3 chose the arc; this chapter *sustains it across a draft that takes months* — the tracking problem is logistical, not theoretical: by chapter 14 you will not remember what Mara's false belief sounded like in chapter 3, whether Theo's "silent labor" pattern escalated or plateaued, or which of your outlines' flaw-choices actually made it to prose. **The arc tracker** is the instrument — one table, updated at every draft mint (Ch 7's bible-sync ritual, extended):

> PROMPT CARD PC-19 — Arc tracker
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-19 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic -->
> Role: continuity auditor for CHARACTER CHANGE (not plot).
> Context: character sheet (arc type, false belief, flaw-pattern,
> climax choice) + my chapter-by-chapter notes below + any drafted
> chapters I paste (bible-first: paste the sheet FIRST, always).
> Task: for each chapter-note/passage, extract in a table:
> chapter | pressure applied (what forced the pattern or its
> alternative) | pattern response (flaw-behavior or growth-behavior —
> quote the triggering line if pasted) | false-belief status
> (reinforced / challenged / unaddressed) | cost logged? (Y/N —
> which cost) | rung moved (P/R/E/—).
> Flag: (a) 3+ consecutive chapters with pattern UNADDRESSED (arc
> stalling — the character is coasting); (b) growth-behavior
> appearing BEFORE any cost paid for the flaw's alternative (arc
> *pre-paid* — change unearned, the classic false-dawn); (c) any
> row contradicting the sheet's arc type (if negative arc: flag
> unexplained kindness-spike; if positive: flag unexplained
> surrender-spike). NO recommendations — flags are my inventory to
> adjudicate.
> ```

The table's column design *is* the craft: **pressure → response → belief-status → cost** mirrors Chapter 3's chain running in *time* — the tracker's job is to prove the chain has chapters to stand on, and its three flags catch the two classic arc failures plus one: **stalling** (pattern never pressured — the draft's equivalent of the Act II hole, but for character: plot moves, person doesn't; readers feel this as "I liked her better at the start"); **pre-paid change** (growth appearing *before* the climax's price — the false dawn, usually from writing the *intended* end-state into early scenes because you've already *felt* the arc complete in your head — the sheet's payoff-aspiration leaking into act one); and **arc-type contradictions** (your chosen arc fighting the prose's drift — a negative-arc protagonist acquiring unearned warmth the prose finds easier than corrosion — *smiling characters are cheap*; the flag's job is to make the cheapness visible as rows).

**Updating discipline:** the tracker fills *from the hub* — chapter notes from the session log's one-liners, passages from draft-current — never from memory of memory (months-old recollection of "when did Theo last do the silent-labor thing" is exactly the amnesia the instrument exists for); one table-row pass per draft mint takes ten minutes and *is* the character half of Ch 12's continuity pass already queued (PC-22 there reads this table as its character input — the passes share documents, not vibes); flags adjudicate like any critic output (decisions rows: fix now / fix at character pass / *flag is wrong, prose is right* — the last one possible because the sheet can be amended *deliberately*; every arc-tracker flag that ends in a sheet-v-next is the system working *backwards* from pages, which is how real characters outgrow plans honestly rather than through silent drift).

**Supporting-cast tracking (cut-down form):** cast members with functional roles (Ch 3's field 12) get a *presence/contrast* row instead of arcs: chapter | appeared Y/N | contrast beat shown (the inversion still legible?) | one new detail (or — : stale since Ch N). A foil who vanishes for eight chapters or stops *contrasting* (their choice no longer legible against the protagonist's) is a structural dropout — catchable in seconds from the cut-down table, expensive if first noticed by a beta reader in Ch 13 ("I forgot Casi existed" is a triage row nobody wants).

## Distinct dialogue voices

The blind test, formalized — the chapter's falsification instrument:

> PROMPT CARD PC-18 — Voice differentiation test
>
> ```text
> <!-- PC-18 v1 · tested 2026-09 · generic -->
> Role: blind attribution examiner.
> Input: 6–10 dialogue EXCHANGES from my draft, speakers labeled
> only A/B (strip tags first — or paste tags and instruct me to
> cover them while you reason — blind either way).
> Task 1: for each exchange, guess the speaker from character sheets
> (paste the cast's voice notes: diction / syntax / habit /
> never-say). Show confidence 1–5 per guess.
> Task 2: list every line that could have been said by EITHER
> speaker without meaning changing (swap-violations), quote them.
> Task 3: for each cast voice, output a ONE-LINE "fingerprint":
> the pattern that identifies them (e.g., "clipped negatives +
> tool metaphors + never asks questions").
> Output: guess table, swap list, fingerprints. NO rewrites —
> fixing is my pass.
> ```

Task 1's confidence scores are the nuance that makes the test real: **guesses at confidence 5 are fine even if technically ambiguous** (strong fingerprints make attribution *obvious* — that's success), while **guesses at 1–2 on *major* characters are failures to fix** (the machine couldn't tell your leads apart using their *own* notes — the sheets and the prose have diverged, or both are soft); Task 2's swap-list gives you the surgical worklist (each quoted line gets rewritten *in that speaker's fingerprint* — your hands, per the attribution-pass rule of Ch 10); Task 3's fingerprints graduate into the bible §2 voice-notes (one line each — the most-pasted character data after the names list: PC-15's context pack and PC-14's dialogue locks *both* draw from them).

**The three levers, worked —** because "make them sound different" needs mechanics beneath the test. Build from Example B's cast (detective **Inspector Nkechi Bello**; xenobotanist **Dr. Leila Haddad** — the station-story protagonist from Ch 3's split examples) and see one situation run through each:

*Situation: told the plan will fail, response starting "That's not—":*

- **Diction (the word shelf):** Nkechi takes the shelf of *procedure and record* — "That's not in the report, and the report is what we have." Leila takes the shelf of *measurement* — "That's not within tolerance; the loop's been bleeding two percent since shift change." Vocabulary *domains* separate characters harder than vocabulary *levels* (both are precise professionals — level-matching is why the intern slips past naive checks; **domain** is the lever).
- **Syntax (the sentence skeleton):** Nkechi: fragments welded by conjunctions ("That's not in the report, and that's the end of it — file it or don't."). Leila: conditional ladders, clauses stacking like rack hardware ("If the plan assumes the loop holds, then the plan assumes something the sensors don't say."). Same intelligence, opposite architectures — *syntax under stress is the fastest tell in the business*, which is why Ch 3's field 10 puts sentence-length-under-pressure on the sheet *before* any dialogue exists.
- **Rhythm + habit + never-say (the metronome and the bans):** Nkechi's habit: answers questions with the *record's* version of events; never: says "I think" (record-voice bans speculation-markers). Leila's habit: trails into unit-mutter ("—two percent, two percent…"); never: says "trust me" (trust is a *measurement* to her, not a plea). Habits are *visible under dialogue-tags-covered reading* — the blind test's natural allies; never-says are checkable by search (Ch 12's line pass runs the list — bans enforced mechanically, not by memory).

Run those three levers *down your own cast* now: two characters with different *domains* + different *skeletons* + one *habit* and one *never* each are distinguishable at confidence 5 while speaking about unrelated topics — which is exactly what the swap-test will verify. Voice differentiation is not a talent; it's *four specified variables per character, maintained across every line* — and the maintained-part is where the machine both helps (searching for swap-violations across 90,000 words faster than any human) and hurts (its own generation *wants* to converge every voice toward the house register — Ch 10's locks and your attribution passes are the wall).

## The flattening problem

Now the chapter's honesty section — the pitfall named as a *systematic tendency*, not a scare story, with the mechanism and the countermeasures, because everything above is *detection*; this is *prevention*.

**Why the machine flattens characters toward the agreeable average.** Four forces, stacked: (1) **training mass** — likable, competent, quip-capable characters have more *text* written about them (the helpful-intern prior isn't a bug from nowhere: it's what a model's "character" most often *is* across its diet); (2) **sycophancy's cousin** — generation that might displease a user's *implied* sympathies (your beloved protagonist's assistant-generated scenes getting *cruel* to him) completes toward warm-safe; (3) **constraint poverty** — Ch 6's core lesson applied to people: adjectives on a character sheet ("complex," "tragic past") resolve to center; only *behavioral* constraints (flaw-first interview rules, fingerprint bans) push off it; and (4) **averaging across your own pasted context** — if the desk holds your *kind* scenes, kindness is the style-condition next scene inherits; the desk has no memory that your book's *point* is the characters' unkindnesses unless you *say so* (bible §5 story-rules, restated).

**Countermeasures, one per force — the chapter's stack:**

1. **Flaw-first everything (against training mass):** PC-17's rule 3 extended to every generation: character-touching prompts include the *flaw-behavior* line from the sheet in their context pack, phrased as *behavior* ("answers grief with calendar logistics"), so the completion's prior includes *your pattern* as a probable continuation. The intern doesn't fight your sheet; your sheet has to *be* in the room, in pattern-language, every time.
2. **Adversarial casting audits (against warm-safe):** monthly, one pass: "list every interaction in these chapters where a character was *kinder/wiser/funnier than their sheet's flaw permits* — quote each" — the mirror-image of PC-11's critique (here: hunting *unearned warmth* the way structure-hunting went after "and then"). Findings adjudicate normally; the typical result is a cluster of early-chapter smiles to strip (your drafting-speed days lean on the intern most — the audit's clusters reveal your *calendar* too).
3. **Behavioral, never dispositional, specification (against constraint poverty):** sheets specify *what they do under X pressure*; if a field could be satisfied by a personality-quiz answer, rewrite it until it *wouldn't* — this is Ch 3's pattern-not-label rule again, and it is the single highest-leverage anti-flattening edit that exists (a good flaw-pattern is a *generator* of un-average behavior; an adjective is a permission slip for it).
4. **Desk hygiene for character work (against context-averaging):** character-sensitive generation opens with the cast *extract relevant to this scene only* — not your whole pleasant chapter folder; anti-relevant context (scenes where your antagonist *won*) gets pasted *when you need the edge*, not left to accumulate warmth; and the story-rules §5 bans ("no character is reassured in this scene" / "cruelty must go unanswered once per act") are the standing constraint-pack version.

**Stereotype drift — the adjacent hazard, handled bluntly.** When "distinctiveness" gets sought through *group* markers instead of *individual* pressure — dialect-as-costume, culture-as-flavor-packet, trauma-as-backstory-accessory — characters stop being people and become *types with authenticating detail*, a failure that is both craft-dead (type = flat by definition, Ch 3) and *ethical* (Ch 15's territory: caricature of real groups is harm, not risk-free style — the ETHICS NOTE this chapter has been owed, spent here): **fictional individuals may be awful; groups they *represent* must be as particular and as granted interiority as your protagonists' group.** Operational guards, since principle needs mechanism: (a) the *specificity audit* — for any character carrying group-markers, name three *individual* traits that have nothing to do with the group-marking (if you can't, the group *is* the characterization — a stereotype in evidence's clothing); (b) *self-check the source* — if your character's group-knowledge comes from media rather than from contact, memory, or research, the sheet gets a `research/` entry (first-person accounts, history — sourced, dated, Ch 5's type-split applied to *people's* histories) or the marker comes *off*; (c) *the machine's role is bounded here* — generation is fine for *plot behavior* of characters whose group-context you've grounded, and is **not consulted as authority on the lived texture of groups you don't know** (it completes *representations* of groups — the same distribution-flattening that produces clichés, now aimed at humans who exist); your sources for that texture are human and primary, full stop. One line for the style guide's inclusivity promise, now load-bearing: *vary your cast across cultures, ages, genders, bodies — and give every member of every group the flaw-chain depth you give your leads, or don't write them in.*

> REALITY CHECK — PC-18's blind test, PC-17's flaw-first rules, and the audits above raise the floor substantially; they cannot *guarantee* un-flattened characters, because the generator's center-of-mass pull never turns off — it is subtracted from, never absent. What this means for your workflow: machine-*drafted* character dialogue always passes through your attribution+voice pass (Ch 10's standing rule — *the seam where flattening enters is sentence-level, so the seam where it exits is too*); interviews and trackers shape *material and continuity*, not final lines; and your cast's ultimate un-average-ness has exactly one source — Ch 3's sheets written from *your* specific humans-and-observations (the model cannot supply the one ingredient it doesn't have: your particular life's evidence about particular people. No prompt fixes a sheet built from vibes; every prompt above *amplifies* a sheet built from observation).

### A situational interview, run live (Theo, before the fight beat)

To show the interactive format's texture before you run your own — abbreviated, but the *shape* of turns and corrections intact. Context pack: Theo's cut-down sheet (flaw-pattern: substitutes silent labor for conversation; want: keep the house as-is) + scene pressure: *"the kitchen fight — Mara is inventorying; the December fact is about to land."*

> **[Interviewer]:** Q1 — It's the night before you'll say the thing about December. You're fixing something in the kitchen that doesn't need fixing. What exactly is broken, and what's actually wrong?
>
> **[Theo]:** The drawer runner on the far side. It sticks. I could do it in my sleep — I have. [flaw speaking first: the *wrong* repair — camera detail present: the runner.]
>
> **[Interviewer — verdict line]:** In-character ✓ (labor-substitution, concrete). One probe deeper —
>
> **[Interviewer]:** Q2 — Mara offers to hold the flashlight. What do you say, and what does your hand do while you say it?
>
> **[Theo]:** "Don't need it — there's the window light." And my hand keeps the screwdriver working, doesn't stop when I answer. Stops *after*, when she's gone. [habit captured as *timing* — refusal expressed in logistics, the pattern's exact grammar; the pause-after-her-leaving is a beat-ready detail.]
>
> **[Interviewer — verdict]:** In-character ✓✓ — the stopped-hands detail serves scene 5's physical business; filing to beats, not bible (consumed = scheduled).
>
> **[…Q3–Q6 compressed: the December answer arrives as logistics again — "She asked. I said you were on the survey. Two things in one sentence — that's how I told her." — flagged and kept: flaw-pattern under *memory* pressure, worst-case framing passed…]**
>
> **[Q7 — the probe the sheet warned about]:** What won't you talk about tonight?
>
> **[Theo]:** [drift caught live] ~~Whether I'm angry.~~ → **[Interviewer correction]:** DRIFT: "angry" is abstract self-diagnosis (rule 2). Restate as camera.
>
> **[Theo]:** …The two weeks after the funeral. Nothing specific — *nothing*. [the refusal itself becomes the Q8-output: "one refusal the character wouldn't touch — and why": the emptiness *is* the content; pushing would break the 60-word cap's spirit and the sheet's dosing schedule — wounds arrive on outline, not on interviewer curiosity.]
>
> **[Q8 + harvest]:** Three usable: the runner; the hand-keeps-working beat; the two-things-in-one-sentence self-report. One refusal: the funeral fortnight (reason: dosed at fight beat, not interview). **Sheet verdict:** no field changes — Q2's "window light" line *may* join setting-facts §4 as a checkable (kitchen has north light — consistency: verify against Ch 4's "thin harbor light" description before bible-minting).

*Annotation of what just happened:* two of eight turns needed correction (drift and abstraction — the intern's two doors), corrections *strengthened the sheet's operational vocabulary* (the verdict line's "flaw speaking first" phrasing now matches how the pattern *answers* under memory pressure — field 5 got one word richer via a decisions row); the refusal honored the dosing schedule (archaeology rule held under temptation); and the last consistency-check note — *verify north light against drafted prose* — shows interviews **serving** continuity rather than outrunning it (an interview-only "fact" contradicting chapters 4's light would be canon drift's birth; the check ran *before* filing, which is the whole discipline in one gesture). Total session cost: fourteen minutes. Scene-usable yield: three beats + one calibrated refusal. Your runs will look messier — mess is fine; *verdict lines and harvest sections are what make the mess legible six weeks later*.

### The unearned-warmth audit, run live

Countermeasure 2 deserves one concrete pass, since "monthly" arrives faster than expected. Input: three recent chapters, one prompt:

```text
Role: adversarial warmth-auditor for this cast.
For each chapter passage below: quote every moment a character is
kinder, wiser, funnier, or more reassured than their SHEET (pasted)
would license under the scene's pressure. Table: quote | character |
sheet-tension (which field it strains) | severity (1–3).
Also flag: reassurances that END scenes (comfort-resolutions —
banned per bible §5 "no character is reassured in this scene"
for scenes 5–9's pressure band) | humor that undercuts a beat
marked [cost] in the outline.
NO rewrites. End with the three highest-severity clusters by
chapter-range — clusters reveal drafting-calendar patterns.
```

Real findings from the Example A demo (abridged): *ch5: "She squeezed his shoulder — he almost smiled" → Theo, strains flaw-pattern (silent-labor doesn't do comfort-touches on demand), severity 2 — cluster: drafted Sunday evening; ch6: Aurelia "explained gently, as if to a child" → Aurelia strains her own field (never-pities-Mara), severity 1, but the *tag* is narrator-warmth about Mara — flag as narrator-slip; ch9: "they shared the look of two people who had finally understood" → BOTH strain no-reassurance rule, severity 3, comfort-resolution ending a [cost]-marked beat — outline violation smuggled in as prose.* Two patterns the calendar line exposes: **Sunday-evening smiles** (fatigue drafts — you reach for the intern when tired, exactly as predicted) and **narrator-as-comfort** (when characters can't be warm per sheets, the *narration* warms them instead — subtler, caught by the "narrator-warmth" clause). Adjudication: ch5 shoulder-touch → KEEEP-with-repair (touch *stays*, "almost smiled" goes — a refused smile is flaw-compatible); Aurelia tag → cut narrator clause; ch9 look-sharing → REWRITTEN at line pass (the cost-beat ends on Mara *mis*-reading Theo — truer to arc, and feeds the fight's escalation). The audit's third month is when the pattern flips: findings drop *below five* — your drafting-time detection has internalized the floor the audits set. (If they never drop: your sheets are licensing warmth the *story* needs — maybe the sheets are wrong — adjudicate *upward*, deliberately, via versioned sheet edits. The audit polices drift in **both** directions: unlicensed warmth *and*, by its absence of findings, a cast grown uniformly cold.)

## Worked example: Example B — same scene, three voices

The genre register, for technique demonstration: **"The Clockmaker's Alibi"** (mystery) and a beat from the Europa station story (SF), run through the full stack — interviews' fingerprints, PC-18 structure, verdict annotated. The scene: *someone explains why the locked workshop's bell rang after the theft.*

**Pass 1 — before the stack (the intern's first draft, as shipped):**

> "That's strange," Nkechi said, frowning at the door. "The bell shouldn't have rung. It's a very secure room, and no one could have gotten in. 'Unless,' she thought, 'someone wanted us to think they did.' She had seen this before — in her experience, the truth was always hiding behind an apparent impossibility. 'Tell me about the night watch,' she said kindly."

*Diagnosis, line by line:* "frowning" — camera-feelings tag; "very secure" — dispositional nothing (no *how*: bolt count? bell mechanism? — rule 1's camera failed); "kinds of strange" and "said kindly" — **intern markers** (adverb-comfort, unearned warmth — flag set (b) family: kindness before any pressure earned it); the interior summary — **disposable depth** (the "she had seen this before" gesture at experience *substitutes* for the experience); "apparent impossibility" — **house-register abstraction** where Nkechi's fingerprint (record-voice) demands the *record's* impossibility. Nobody is *distinguishable* in this passage — not because Nkechi's absent, but because **no fingerprint constraints were in the desk's context** — the pass is the countermeasure list *failing by absence*: sheet not pasted, flaw not phrased as behavior, story-rules silent, warm-safe default fully in charge.

**Pass 2 — fingerprints loaded (context pack: Nkechi's §2 line + flaw "substitutes procedure for risk" as behavior + §5 "no reassurance in this scene"; same beats, PC-15's unattributed format first):**

> A: The bell's on the inner frame. Inner frame's bolted from the workshop side. Report says bolted at 21:10, unbroken since.
> B: Then the report's wrong or the bell's not what the report means.
> A: Reports don't ring. [...] "Tell me about the night watch" beats "Tell me about the night watch," kind.

*Annotated:* A's shelf = the record ("Report says"); skeleton = fragments welded by conjunction; habit = *answers the impossible with the document's version* (flaw-as-behavior performing under pressure — procedure substituting for risk exactly as the sheet billed); never-say held (zero "I think"s — searchable at line pass). B's conditional-ladder architecture ("or the bell's not what…") could not swap into A without rewiring meaning — **swap-test passes at confidence 5** *before anyone was named*, because attribution now travels in the *speech* (the tags-free format made the fingerprints the only carriers — which is what "voices distinct" actually means mechanically). The kind-tag deletion at the end: anti-flattening countermeasure 2 catching unearned warmth *mid-pass* (the intern had smuggled "kindly" back in between rewrites — note it: the pull doesn't turn off; the passes subtract).

**Pass 3 — the SF register, same technique, different domain (Leila, abridged):**

> A: Loop's bleeding two percent. Plan's got it at zero. One of those is a wish.
> B: We can compensate at the manifold — reroute through —
> A: Through the secondary line rated fourteen percent below the surge you're planning. That's not compensation. That's arithmetic with hope in it.

## Pitfalls

> PITFALL — **Agreeable average (F-02's root).** *Symptom:* your cast converges on kind-competent-quip-wounded; dialogue exchanges pass the plot but fail the blind test ("who's speaking? does it matter?"); interviews return personality-quiz answers; beta readers later say "everyone was nice" (Ch 13's most-received note worldwide); your antagonist's letters home are unexpectedly *thoughtful*. *Why it happens:* the four stacked forces (training mass / warm-safe completion / constraint poverty / desk-averaging) — plus *your* speed-days drafting over the intern because warm prose is *fast* prose to generate and to accept. *Fix (stack, in order — detection runs on prevention):* (1) **specification:** rewrite sheet fields as flaw-*behavior* patterns until they'd fail a personality quiz (the highest-leverage edit — behavior generators can't complete to average); (2) **context:** flaw-line + fingerprints + §5 bans in every character-touching prompt's pack (flaw-first rules — the intern has to *fight* your pattern, not sail past it); (3) **detection:** PC-17's self-audit verdicts mid-interview, PC-18's swap-list + confidence table, the monthly *unearned-warmth* audit (hunt kinder-than-sheet moments — strip the cluster, usually early chapters); (4) **repair:** attribution pass by hand in fingerprints (Ch 10 rule — seams exit where seams enter: sentence level); (5) **grounding:** when sheets run dry of specificity, the fix isn't more prompting — it's *observation* (your life's evidence, Ch 3's specific-odd rule) and *sourced research* for textures you don't carry (never the model-as-group-authority — stereotype-drift's source). *Prevention:* the blind test becomes a *per-scene* habit for dialogue-heavy chapters (two minutes — tags off, read aloud, "obvious?" — confidence felt by *you*, the original blind examiner), fingerprints filed in the bible *before* drafting starts (PC-15's context depends on them existing — prevention is a *pre-drafting* act), and the cast's group-marking passing the specificity audit (three individual traits beyond the marker, or the marker comes off) — craft defense and ethical floor in one check.

Related failure for Appendix C: **F-02 (all characters sound the same)** — joint entry with Ch 10's locks and Ch 14's voice erosion (F-09): one pipeline (specify → generate under locks → mark → blind-test → audit → repair), three gates; this chapter is where the pipeline's *character* stage lives.

## Key takeaways

- **The helpful intern is the machine's character default** — likable, competent, quip-wounded, available in every biography — produced by training mass, warm-safe completion, constraint poverty, and desk-averaging. It never *fights* your sheet; the sheet has to be in the room, in pattern-language, every time.
- **Interviews extract behavior only under pressure-rules:** situational (scene-pressured), interactive (one question at a time), camera-tested, flaw-first, 60-capped — answers are ideation until adjudicated against the sheet; backstory is need-scheduled and demand-limited, never archaeology.
- **Arcs are tracked, not remembered:** PC-19's pressure → response → belief-status → cost table (flagging stalls, pre-paid change, and arc-type contradictions) updates at every mint and *becomes* Chapter 12's character-pass input — ten minutes of logistics against months of draft.
- **Voices are four specified variables:** diction *domain*, syntax skeleton, habit, never-say — tested blind (PC-18's confidence table + swap-list), fingerprints filed in the bible, enforcement mechanical (search the never-says; tags covered, read aloud).
- **Flattening and stereotype drift are prevented at the sheet and the desk,** detected by audits, repaired at the sentence seam — and *grounded by your life*: the one ingredient no prompt supplies is your particular evidence about particular people; the model amplifies sheets, it cannot manufacture them.
