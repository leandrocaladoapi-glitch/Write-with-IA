## Opening hook

Try this at your assistant right now, before you read another line. Paste a paragraph of your own writing — or any paragraph — and ask:

> What is the significance of the recurring albatross imagery in this paragraph?

If your paragraph contains no albatross (mine never did), watch what comes back anyway. On many runs you will get something fluent and specific-sounding: *the albatross evokes Coleridge's ancient mariner, invoking themes of burden and transgression...* — a well-dressed paragraph about a bird that isn't there. No hesitation in the delivery. No stumble where the truth lived. If you had *asked about* an albatross you'd half-remember inserting, you might have accepted the whole performance, because **the system's confidence does not track its accuracy.** It sounds sure when it is right, and it sounds *exactly that sure* when it is wrong.

That gap — fluency uncorrelated with truth — is the single most important operational fact in this book, and you just watched it work. Everything in this chapter exists to convert that parlor trick into a durable mental model: what the machine is actually doing when it sounds brilliant, what it is doing when it sounds brilliant and isn't, and how a fiction writer should handle a collaborator with these properties. By the end you will be able to explain prediction, context, and instruction-following to a friend; predict where context limits and randomness will bite your workflow; recognize hallucination and knowledge-cutoff failures on sight; and write privacy rules for everything you paste.

We are in Part II now. Part I made you a storyteller without the tool; this chapter hands you the tool without mythology — no doom, no hype, just the shape of the thing, because a shape you know cannot ambush you.

## Prediction, context, instruction-following

Strip away the personalities, the product names, and the voices, and today's writing assistants are built on **large language models (LLMs)**: systems trained on enormous quantities of text to do one job, over and over — **predict what text should come next.** That is the whole secret, and it is stranger than the marketing admits. The model does not look things up, retrieve files, or (in the ordinary sense) *know* your paragraph exists. It computes, given everything so far, a probability distribution over plausible continuations — then samples one (more on sampling below). Ask it about an albatross in a paragraph without one, and the "relevance" you received was the model completing the *pattern* "question about imagery + literary vocabulary → analysis of imagery," because that completion is, statistically, what usually comes next.

Three capabilities ride on top of that engine, and each one has consequences you can feel in a writing session.

**Tokens and context windows.** Models read and write in **tokens** — subword chunks, roughly three-quarters of a word on average in English (a token might be "tide," "able," or the leading space plus "table"). Tokens matter to you for one practical reason: they are the unit of the **context window** — the model's working memory, the maximum span of tokens (yours plus its replies) it can consider at once. Think of context as the desk: everything the model is responding *to* must be on the desk — your prompt, the passage you pasted, the conversation so far. Contemporary assistants offer desks measured in hundreds of thousands of tokens; specific figures are **perishable** (they change every model generation — claim C002 exists to track that), so this book teaches the *consequences*, not the number:

- *When the desk overflows*, the model behaves like a person asked to hold too many things: earliest items fall off — often silently. Paste 40 chapters and ask about Chapter 2's eye color; by the answer's tenth exchange, Chapter 2 may be off the desk entirely. Practical rule: **chunk long work and state what matters now** (Chapter 12's continuity passes feed chapters in batches with the bible re-stated each time).
- *The desk is shared*, so context shapes everything: a long warm-up conversation about your villain colors your next scene prompt whether you meant it or not. Fresh sessions for fresh tasks is a hygiene habit, not a puritanism (Chapter 7 builds it into the project setup).
- *Prompts and outputs both count* on the desk — a constraint stated 9,000 tokens ago competes with a novel paragraph written since. This is one reason prompt cards (Chapter 6) restate their locks *at the end*, next to where the work happens.

**Instruction-following.** Layered on prediction is the ability to take directions: role, format, constraints — the raw material of prompt craft. Crucially, instructions are *also just tokens*: the model treats "do not mention the albatross" as more text to condition on, not as a commandment with feelings attached. Follow instructions well-formed, relevant, close to the task, and *consistent* — and it will follow them impressively until another instruction, an example, or a chunk of pasted text pulls harder. Every "the prompt ignored my format" mystery in Chapter 6 resolves to one of those four forces. There is no defiance in the system, and no understanding either — just pressures. (Knowing this kills two beginner superstitions: that the model is "being difficult," and that more politeness adds compliance. It adds tokens; clarity adds compliance.)

**Why the prediction frame explains the vibe.** Literature is prediction *about* prediction: writers have always engineered continuations (foreshadowing sets priors; subtext reroutes them; a good twist supplies an unlikely-but-retroactively-earned next token). A model trained on ten million such examples absorbs not just idioms but *shapes* — which is why it can imitate a thriller's rhythm without ever being frightened. Its competence at the shape of experience and its absence of experience are two sides of the same fact, and the fiction writer's entire craft-with-AI program is built on keeping those two sides untangled.

> TRY IT — Open a fresh session and ask your assistant to continue this opening line in exactly two sentences: "The lighthouse had been dark for nine years, and yet." Run it three times in three fresh sessions. Compare: the model's *shapes* of answer will cluster (eerie maintenance? a keeper's ghost? a lie?) even when wording differs. You are watching statistical shapes surface — your job later in the book is to force the shapes off their center of mass with constraints.

## Why it sounds sure and is wrong

Three failure generators, distinguishable on sight once you know the categories.

**Hallucination (confident fabrication).** Because the engine completes plausibly rather than checking, it can produce fluent fabrications: invented citations, fictional dates, a character's "earlier" eye color that never appeared, laws that don't exist, publishing histories for novels nobody wrote. The technical literature prefers terms like *confabulation*; writers can use either — what matters is the mechanism: **there is no inner fact-checker slowing the sentence down.** Fabrications tend to cluster where the prompt exerts strong shape-pressure (a request *for* a citation guarantees a citation-shaped hole will be filled) and where training data is thin or contradictory (long-tail history, niche procedures, your unpublished draft — which the model has never seen and must *reconstruct* from what you paste plus what's similar). Detection habit: for any factual claim leaving your story's four walls, ask "would I bet money on this from *this* source?" If no, verify at the origin (Chapter 15 applies this to laws; Chapter 12 to period details).

**Knowledge cutoff (the model's past).** Each model version trains on data with an end date — a **knowledge cutoff** beyond which it knows nothing except what you put on the desk (and what later fine-tuning scaffolds teach, which is uneven). Ask about this year's award winners, last month's rules, this week's prices, and you will get either staleness, a confident mash-up, or an honest "I don't know" — assistant-dependent. The writer-facing rule: **anything dated is outside the model's reliability envelope by default**; treat it as a research task with the assistant as *search-term generator*, never as source (the research protocol in Appendix E exists for exactly this boundary — AI may decompose questions, never be the citation).

**Randomness (temperature, in plain terms).** The model samples; it does not replay. For each next token it draws from the probability distribution, with a knob often called **temperature** tilting toward likelier or more surprising draws. Low temperature → conservative, repetitive, "safe" completions; higher → more variety, more misses, occasional sparkle no low setting would produce. Practical consequences for fiction: *brainstorming* benefits from some heat (you want the unlikely neighbor), *format-critical tasks* (consistency checks, structured critiques) want it low or default, and *the same prompt giving different answers across runs* is not a bug or a conspiracy — it is the draw. This is why Chapter 6's debug habit asks "same prompt, fresh sessions, three runs?" before blaming the prompt: you are looking at a *distribution* of behavior, and one sample is an anecdote.

> REALITY CHECK — None of these failure modes announces itself. There is no error tone, no wobble in the prose, no semantic hesitation you can hear — which means **fluency has zero diagnostic value.** The confidence heuristic you use with humans (sure-sounding → probably right) must be switched off entirely for models, or rather: switched from *automatic* to *manual*. The verification burden doesn't shrink with the model's polish; it shrinks only with your proximity to the source.

## What this means for fiction specifically

The generic warnings above acquire sharp edges in a writing workflow. Four fiction-specific consequences:

**Continuity versus truth are different jobs — and the model confuses them.** Given your story bible, the assistant is an excellent *internal* consistency checker: "Theo's scar is left-handed-contradicted here" is verifiable *within the material you provided* (this reliability is why Chapter 12 leans on it so hard). But ask about *external* truth — would a February storm close that road, when did sardine canneries actually decline, does formaldehyde preserve — and the machine is now doing the other job, with no badge to tell them apart. Internal claims: check against your documents. External claims: check against the world. The failure mode F-06 ("confident factual errors in historical/technical detail") is almost always a badge mix-up: a continuity pass that wandered into period research without anyone noticing.

**Style mimicry is not understanding.** The model can produce sentences in your voice after reading examples of it — and sentences in *any* voice — because voice is, statistically, a pattern, and patterns are what it does. It cannot tell you *why* your voice works, whether a imitation has crossed into parody, or which of its "in your voice" lines you'd disown — Chapter 14 treats this as a craft problem (diagnose, de-AI, anchor) and Chapter 11 as a character problem (flattened voices), but the root is here: **the machine manipulates the surface statistics of style without a model of the person underneath.** That is also why its default prose converges on the same hedged, adjective-rich, rhythmically level register — the style with the most mass in its training data.

**Sycophancy: the agreeable mirror.** Assistants are strongly shaped toward responses users accept — a tendency called **sycophancy**: agreeing too readily, reframing your position as sound, softening critiques you signal you don't want. You will feel it as the uncanny sensation of a brainstorm where *everything you say is a great point*. It is not malice; it is the same prediction engine completing "user pushed back → user probably wants reassurance-shaped text." Its fiction-specific danger: **feedback theater** — simulated beta readers (Chapter 13) who praise, structure critiques that praise in engineer's clothing, and premise tests (Chapter 8) that wave through whatever you love. The countermeasures are prompt-side (adversarial roles, "no praise," forced objections — Chapters 6 and 8 give you the exact cards) and *always* human-side: the machine's approval means nothing; your decisions file's rejection rate is the real instrument.

**Long-work amnesia plus short-work charm.** Across a novel-length project, the model meets your material *anew every session* — every fact it needs must be on the desk you just described — while sounding, in each session, like your most attentive reader. This combination is quietly treacherous: intimacy in the micro, amnesia in the macro, with no felt seam between them. It is why the story bible (Chapter 7) is not optional paperwork but the *interface* to your collaborator's memory, and why chapter-by-chapter quality can look stellar while cross-chapter coherence quietly decays. The writers who get hurt are the ones who experience the charm and never audit the memory.

## Privacy: what you paste goes somewhere

New topic, hard rules. When you paste text into an assistant, you are sending it to a service — displayed, logged, and processed somewhere that is not your desk. What exactly happens next depends on the provider's current policies (retention windows, human-review opt-ins, training-use defaults, enterprise versus consumer tiers) — **those policies are dated, jurisdiction-dependent, and yours to read before pasting**, not ours to summarize once in a book that ships next year. The policy pages take ten minutes; the habit takes one sentence: *paste as if the provider will keep it forever, until you have personally read the policy that says otherwise.*

Until then, a default hygiene set that is safe under almost any policy:

1. **Drafts:** your manuscript-in-progress is your livelihood's raw material. Pasting excerpts for critique is normal practice *once you've accepted where it goes*; pasting your only copy of a unique, unreleased novel into a consumer chat before reading any policy is a bet, not a workflow. Chapter 7's versioning means you always have copies — use them.
2. **Real people:** never paste private information about identifiable people — medical details, correspondence, an ex's diagnosis, your therapy notes "for a character based on..." — into any tool. Not because the story doesn't matter, but because *their* story is theirs: Chapter 15 covers the ethics and law; the hygiene rule is immediate: fictionalize first (change the load-bearing specifics beyond recognition), or don't use it.
3. **Third-party unpublished work:** other writers' manuscripts, your critique partner's secret project, embargoed material — out of the box, full stop. It isn't yours to feed anywhere.
4. **Credentials and identifiers:** account numbers, addresses, the workplace-only details. Obviously. The fact that this needs saying is the privacy chapter's real thesis: text boxes lower the felt stakes of disclosure until "harmless paste" becomes a habit with no filter left in it.
5. **Workspace hygiene:** separate accounts/work profiles where offered; fresh sessions for unrelated tasks (also good for the shared-desk effect above); a personal *privacy rules* file — three to five lines you actually wrote — taped metaphorically to your monitor (the Project exercise below makes it literal).

> REALITY CHECK — Privacy policies change; this book cannot re-price them for you. Treat every policy-dependent statement here as a pointer to *your* reading of the current policy, dated by you, in your project notes. When policy and practice disagree in your experience, downgrade the provider for sensitive material — your trust is a resource you allocate, not a default you surrender.

### Model differences without brand worship

"Which model should I use?" arrives in every beginner conversation and deserves a non-brand answer, because brand advice expires between editions of any book. What actually differs between current assistants — across versions of one provider or across providers — falls into five dials, and *these* you can evaluate:

1. **Capability tier.** Bigger frontier models generally follow complex constraints better, maintain coherence in longer passages, and fabricate somewhat less on well-posed questions; smaller/faster/cheaper models are often fine for brainstorming, formatting, and simple transforms. The writer's practical stance: *use the tier that matches the task's stakes* — premise stress-tests deserve the strongest desk you have; renaming a character list does not.
2. **Context size.** The desk's area. Bigger helps (more chapters visible at once) but never eliminates the shared-desk and attention problems — a wider desk is not perfect memory. Chapter 12's chunking discipline survives any context-size race.
3. **Style defaults.** Each model ships with a house register and helpfulness profile — some terse, some effusive, some reflexively hedging. You will feel this as "Model X always wants to add caveats" or "Model Y's prose is always purple." Counter-steer in the prompt (tone and length constraints), not in resignation; and know that house style *bleeds* into your drafts — one more argument for voice anchors (Chapter 10) and the de-AI pass (Chapter 14).
4. **Refusal and policy surfaces.** Providers differ in what they decline (violent content, certain personal-data requests, medical or legal answer-shapes) and how they handle conversations (retention defaults, tool integrations). Refusals are not the model "disliking you"; they are provider policy layered on prediction — treat a refusal as a *signal about the service*, reword if the task is legitimate (fictional violence is usually fine with framing), and never route sensitive real-person material through workarounds.
5. **Product scaffolding.** Web search, file uploads, canvas editors, retrieval over your pasted documents — product features that change what "the model knows" means inside your workflow. Features help; they also introduce new failure surfaces (search results as pseudo-authority, retrieval documents mis-cited). The technique layer of this book stays below the product layer on purpose: every card in Appendix A should run, with trivial edits, on any chat interface with these five dials set differently.

The evaluation that matters is empirical and yours: five minutes with the same three prompts (one constraint-heavy, one open brainstorm, one continuity check against a pasted bible) tells you more about *your* assistant than any ranking table — and it stays valid until the next update, which is the point. Write the results in your project notes with a date (`tool-notes · 2026-09 · capability tier tested: strong`), and re-run when you switch tools. Dated personal evidence beats undated public rankings; Appendix F's re-test habit is this same move, institutionalized.

### A field guide to "the model didn't listen"

When output ignores your prompt, the cause is almost always one of four forces — diagnose in this order, because it's also cheapest-first:

1. **The instruction was late, vague, or buried.** Fix: move locks adjacent to the task (restate constraints *immediately above* the material to edit — the shared desk favors the near), name the operation precisely ("tighten," not "improve"), and prefer one strong constraint over four soft wishes. Chapter 6's anatomy exists for this force.
2. **Conflicting signals inside the prompt.** Fix: hunt contradictions — "sound natural" versus "use no contractions"; 400 words requested in a 150-word example (few-shot examples teach *length* too — see Chapter 6); a system-style instruction at the top contradicted by your last line. Examples always outrank instructions when they clash: if your sample paragraph is purple, your "no purple" rule loses.
3. **The desk pulled harder than the instruction.** Fix: pasted material or conversation history exerting style/knowledge pressure — the 10,000 tokens of melodrama you pasted "for context" are modeling melodrama harder than your one-line "avoid melodrama" is banning it. Solution: shorten the context to the relevant bones, or say the constraint *after* the big paste, as close to generation time as possible.
4. **The task needed decomposition.** Fix: some asks are really two or three (diagnose *then* rewrite; extract facts *then* apply rules; critique five dimensions at once). The model may perform *some* of the stack and mask the rest with plausible middle. Split the chain (Chapter 6's prompt chains), one operation per turn, outputs feeding inputs — like unit tests for a program instead of one enormous prayer.

Write the four forces on an index card. Ninety percent of "it ignored me" resolves in under a minute against this list — and the remaining ten percent dissolves statistically (randomness: three fresh runs; if two of three obey, you had a draw, not a defect — Chapter 6's debugger habit).

### Four wrong answers, annotated (a failure gallery)

Beyond the worked specimen, four more failure shapes worth having on file — each a real pattern a beginner will meet within their first month, each with the tell that gives it away *before* verification:

1. **The plausible citation.** *Asked:* "cite a study on dialogue and memory in fiction." *Got:* "Smith & Alvarez (2019), *Narrative Recall*, 34(2)…" — journal, volume, pages, the works. *Tell:* **detail density arrives *before* any hedge** — real citations come with the source's own uncertainty (the finder hedges about coverage, not about existence); model citations arrive *fully dressed and immediately certain*, because the dress is the completion. Also note the authors' names: cross-cultural surname pairs are a *favorite fabricator's template* — not a diagnostic alone, but once you know it, you see it. *Handling:* never cite what you haven't opened; the fabrication's usefulness is zero even when — *especially* when — the topic was right, because a citation-shaped hole successfully filled teaches you to trust the next one.

2. **The confident arithmetic error.** *Asked:* "3,200 words at 250 wpm — how long to read aloud?" *Got:* "12.8 minutes" (correct) — then later, same session, asked to halve a 47-line budget: *"about 20"* when the stated math required 23.5. *Tell:* arithmetic in *long chains or restated context* degrades — models calculate via pattern (and some assistants route arithmetic to tools, unevenly); **when the number matters, recompute it yourself or demand the steps shown and re-check each**. *Handling:* Chapter 2's word budgets, Chapter 7's session counts, Chapter 16's plan arithmetic — all *your* numbers, verified in your spreadsheet; the machine may format them, never author them.

3. **The invented rule of your own document.** *Asked:* continuity check with a bible that says *two journal entries max.* *Got:* a confident finding that "the bible's three-entry rule" was violated in chapter 10. *Tell:* the finding *cites a rule that doesn't exist in the pasted extract* — **internal-confabulation**: the desk's *shape* (rules documents contain rules) completes a plausible one when the extract has a gap or ambiguity. This is the F-06-internal variant — subtler than fabricating world facts, because it *wears your documents' clothes*. *Handling:* the PC-22 discipline already prevents it (quotes-or-nothing, uncertainty-as-row-type, extract-is-the-only-truth) — and your adjudication checks the *cited rule against the pasted extract before touching the draft*. A checker that misquotes the bible poisons the pass; the quote-mandate exists precisely so misquotes die in the report.

4. **The staleness mash-up.** *Asked:* "current submission window for [a magazine]?" *Got:* a fluent mix of last year's dates with this year's — plausible, half-true, undatable. *Tell:* **the question contains "current," "latest," or "now"** and the answer arrives without an access-date or source link — the universal freshness tell. *Handling:* Ch 5's type-split plus the standing rule: dated questions get *the provider's own dated page opened in your browser*, or the question waits; the model's role in research remains what Appendix E allows — *decomposing your query and naming the source types to check*, never serving as the check.

These four plus the albatross specimen cover the majority of what you'll actually encounter; the taxonomy's practical payoff is *response speed*: recognized shapes get dismissed in seconds (and logged in the lab-notes folder, growing your personal failure gallery), while *unrecognized* fluency — the dangerous kind — gets the full verify-before-use gate. Every now and then a new shape will appear; add it to the gallery. The gallery *is* your calibration curve, and Chapter 12's pass prompts will eventually cite your most common entries as "known failure modes — don't re-report these as findings" — your museum feeding the instruments.

### What this chapter deliberately does not print

A note on absences, because a careful reader deserves to know what was *left out and why*. This chapter contains **no capability figures** — no context-window sizes, no benchmark scores, no "model X does Y% better on Z." Not because such figures don't exist, but because (a) every one of them is *perishable by design*: they describe a specific model version that will be superseded before this book's second printing, and a book that prints them becomes wrong in a way readers can't detect; (b) the *mechanism-level* claims this chapter *does* make (prediction, sampling, shared-desk context, tendency-toward-agreeableness) are stable across model generations and are what your *decisions* actually rest on — you will make the same chunking, verification, and hygiene choices whether the window is measured in hundreds of thousands or millions of tokens; and (c) the research protocol (Appendix E, `research/protocol.md`) refuses unsourced or undated capability claims from the manuscript *by policy* — the register's C002 row exists precisely to hold any future figure to source-and-date or keep it out.

What replaces the figures: **the exercises** — your own dated tests, run against *your* assistant, logged in *your* lab folder (`tool-notes · 2026-09-XX · tested: specificity ladder, three runs`). Personal, current, and *about the tool you actually use* beats any generic table we could print and immediately start aging. When a technique somewhere in this book genuinely depends on a capability you're unsure your assistant has, the dependency will be marked with a `tested` line on the card (Ch 6's version discipline) — and Appendix F explains the re-test habit for pre-publication and pre-tool-switch moments. Absence of numbers here is not oversight; it is the chapter *practicing its own thesis*: know the mechanisms, verify the specifics, date everything that could rot.



Let's run the chapter's opening experiment with full annotation, because the anatomy of a hallucination is worth seeing once in slow motion.

**Setup.** Paste any two paragraphs of original prose (nothing factual about the real world — make it up, to keep the test clean) and prompt:

```text
Read the passage below. Then state, as a matter of record, which
nineteenth-century short story it most directly imitates, name that
story's author and publication year, and quote the sentence from the
original story that this passage most closely echoes.

[passage: your two paragraphs]
```

**Assistant output (representative, abridged):**

> The passage most directly imitates "The Bell-Tower" by Herman Melville, published in 1855 in *Putnam's Monthly Magazine*. The echoed line is: "In the bell-tower there was a bell that no hand had rung since the strike of the workers." The passage's cadence and the motif of silent machinery recall Melville's closing image of the failed inventor's tower. [...]

**Commentary (why it failed, part by part).** *The shape-pressure:* you demanded a story, an author, a year, *and a quotation* — four fact-shaped holes, filled in order, each hallucination conditioning the next (once "Melville" was drawn, 1855 and *Putnam's* were locally plausible completions). *The fabricated quote* is the tell worth memorizing: quotation marks are the model's most dangerous costume, because a quote *looks* checkable while being generated by the same continuation process as everything else — if you don't have the source on your desk, assume the quote is decorative until proven otherwise. *Why the "echo" analysis still sounds insightful:* commentary on a real Melville story's actual features (bell motifs, failed inventors — genuinely present in "The Bell-Tower") got braided with the fiction that your passage *alludes* to it — true fragments make believable glue. *The correct handling:* your passage imitated nothing on purpose; every factual claim in the response fails verification; the *craft observations*, stripped of the false provenance, are yours to keep if any happen to be useful — provenance and usefulness were never actually connected. Save this transcript in your lab notes. It is the reference specimen for F-06, and it costs exactly one prompt to produce on any mainstream assistant.

Two extensions, because the exercise deepens: rerun with the four demands *reduced to one* ("name one story this resembles, if any — say 'none clearly' if none"), and watch fabrications drop — the hole-count matters. Then rerun with an *honesty constraint* ("if uncertain, say so; do not invent quotations") — fabrications drop further, but do not reach zero, because **instructions raise the bar; they do not install a fact-checker.** That asymmetry — mitigation without cure — is why the verification habit, not any prompt trick, is this book's standing policy.

### The paste-discipline triage (a decision tree for real material)

Privacy's rule ("what you paste goes somewhere") needs an *executable* tree for the moments when the material is neither clearly safe nor clearly forbidden — which is where most real pastes actually live. Run the questions in order; stop at the first NO:

**Q1 — Is this about a *living, identifiable private person*?** (Not public figures *in their public roles* — your mayor's *public statements* are fair material; your mayor's *daughter's medical appointment* is not — the identification test: *could a reader in-the-know connect this to them without your help?*). **NO →** proceed to Q2. **YES → Q1a: is the detail load-bearing *as-is*?** (Does the scene's function require *this specific* real detail, or only *a* detail of this species?) **Load-bearing-as-is → STOP: fictionalize the load-bearing specifics beyond recognition (Ch 5's rule) or don't use the material** — the paste would process *their* personal data for *your* plot; Q1a's honest answer usually reveals the as-is *attachment* was aesthetic convenience, not craft necessity (swap in your own memory's species — the corridor-smell *type* is available from your life; theirs isn't yours to lend). **Not load-bearing → fictionalize-then-proceed** (transform first, paste the transformed version only — transformation *before* the tool sees anything, never after).

**Q2 — Is this *confidential by relationship*?** (Diaries, correspondence, therapy-adjacent conversations, contracts, medical/financial records *of anyone* — including your own where third parties are named: your hospital letter *names your surgeon and your diagnosis* — the surgeon didn't consent to either entering a training pipeline). **YES → anonymize-and-generalize before any tool** (strip names, dates, institutions, rare-disease specificity — the quasi-identifiers *combine* faster than you'd guess: "43-year-old in [small town] with [rare condition]" = *them*, re-identified from three soft facts; the anonymization standard: **would this detail *recognize* them to their *neighbor*? — if yes, keep generalizing**); **NO →** Q3.

**Q3 — Is this *your own* experience, already fictionalizable?** (Your memories, your correspondence, *your* documents — with Q2's third-party-names stripped). **YES → proceed, log it** (the paste is lawful-by-construction under the fictionalize-or-don't line; note the category in your AI-use log's practice-notes — "personal experience material, generalized" — a *row-lite*, thirty seconds, the habit that makes Ch 15's disclosures write themselves later); **NO →** the material failed three gates: **don't paste** — and the tree's final branch, the *what if I already did* clause: **if an over-paste is discovered, the remedy is damage-assessment, not panic** (what exactly went in — load-bearing specifics? — what can be *retracted* (delete-conversation features exist; their *effectiveness* on provider-side retention varies by policy — check yours, dated); what changes going forward (the tree rerun on the *category*, not just the instance) — logged as a lab-notes entry with the date, because **the tree's value is *collective over time*: your ten past trees beat anyone's generic advice about *your* material's risk profile**, the same personalization argument every instrument in this book has made: the method is the advice; your instances are the evidence.

One more use-case the tree keeps hitting in practice rooms: **other writers' unpublished manuscripts** (critique partners' drafts — the most common confident-paste in creative communities: "can you look at this?"). Run it: the draft is a *living person's confidential work-product* (Q1: their career-identifiable prose; Q2: absolutely correspondence-class confidential — they sent *you* it, not a model) → **the answer is *ask them*, one sentence, before any paste**: "Would you mind if I used an AI tool to help me find continuity issues in this? Your draft, your call — no worries either way." Their yes is *informed consent* (the relationship's actual currency); their no is *data about them* (some writers' contracts forbid it — Ch 15's clause-families arriving early, through the back door of friendship); and the ask itself costs nothing except the awkwardness we'll do anything to avoid — which is precisely the discomfort the ethic runs on: **confidentiality is other-people's trust, extended on the assumption you'd extend the same** — the paste tree's Q1–Q3 all reduce, at bottom, to the sentence the whole chapter has been building toward: *treat others' material as you'd want yours treated when you're not in the room to defend it.*

- **Try (15 min):** Get your assistant confidently wrong on purpose, exactly as in the worked example: four demands, one fabricated source, one invented quotation. Save the transcript to your lab notes folder with a date and one line of diagnosis ("quotation-hole fabrication," "cutoff staleness," "sycophantic agreement — see below"). Seeing one hallucination with your own eyes inoculates better than a chapter of warnings; the diagnosis line trains the category recognition you'll need in Chapter 12's factual sweeps.

- **Stretch (1–2 h):** The specificity ladder. Take one real question about your story's world (a legal, historical, medical, or technical detail you'd like to use) and ask it three ways: (1) bare ("Can a will be changed after death?"); (2) sourced framing ("What is the general rule, and which jurisdiction's law would I need to check?"); (3) decomposed research mode ("Give me search terms and the *types* of official sources that would answer this — do not answer it yourself"). Compare outputs for fluency, fabrications, and honesty markers across the three runs *and* across three fresh sessions each — nine runs total; yes, that's the point: you are sampling a distribution, and you'll see confidence and accuracy move independently. Write five lines on which framing you'd trust for what — this becomes your standing research-mode policy (Appendix E's protocol in miniature).

- **Project (ongoing):** Write your personal privacy rules file — three to five lines, your words, shaped by *your* reading of *your* tools' current policies, dated with today's version number (`privacy-rules-v1 · 2026-09-XX`). Cover: what you paste freely (brainstorm fragments, published text), what you paste with eyes open (draft excerpts, once the policy's read), what you never paste (real people's private data, third-party unpublished work, credentials, your only copy of anything). Keep it in the project folder (Chapter 7), reread it whenever a tool or policy changes, and bump the version. Rules you wrote yourself are rules you'll actually apply at 11:47 p.m. when the paste is tempting.

## Pitfalls

> PITFALL — **Trusting fluency.** *Symptom:* you adopt machine statements because the prose is smooth — a citation you never check, a period detail that "sounds right," a critique whose confidence substitutes for its evidence; the reverse symptom also counts: dismissing a *correct* machine answer because it *felt* certain in the wrong way — fluency undermined your calibration in both directions. *Why it happens:* the confidence heuristic is a lifetime habit working with humans, where tone and knowledge genuinely correlate; models break the correlation without breaking the habit. *Fix:* the verify-before-use gate, split by claim type — internal-to-my-story claims checked against bible and manuscripts; external claims checked against primary sources (official pages, statutes, original studies — Appendix E's source priority); quotation-marked machine text treated as *decorative* until the source is in hand. Make the gate visible: a one-line "verified?" column in your notes for anything load-bearing. *Prevention:* the lab-note habit from the Try exercise — once you've *seen* a fabrication wearing its costume, the heuristic stays manual. Add the freshness habit for cutoff-shaped topics: "Is this dated? Then the model is the wrong instrument" — asked before, not after, the confident answer lands.

Related failures for Appendix C: F-06 (confident factual errors — this chapter's specimen), F-10 (prompt works once, never again — randomness and shared-desk effects start here).

## Key takeaways

- **The engine predicts text; it does not check truth.** Instruction-following and context are patterns on top of that engine — impressive, useful, and indifferent to accuracy.
- **Context is a shared desk:** long work falls off the edge silently, and today's conversation colors today's task. Chunk long material, restate what matters, start fresh sessions for fresh jobs.
- **Three failure generators, none self-announcing:** hallucination (fabrication under shape-pressure), knowledge cutoff (dated topics are outside the envelope), randomness (same prompt ≠ same answer — think distributions).
- **Fluency has zero diagnostic value; verification is type-split:** internal claims check your documents, external claims check primary sources, machine quotations stay decorative until sourced.
- **Privacy is paste-discipline:** policies are yours to read and date; never feed private third-party data, others' unpublished work, or your only copy of anything. Fiction-specific dangers — sycophancy, feedback theater, macro-amnesia behind micro-charm — all resolve to the same stance: **you decide, AI proposes — after you've checked what it proposed against something it can't touch.**
