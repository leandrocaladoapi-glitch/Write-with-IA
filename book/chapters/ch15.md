## Opening hook

The rejection email used three sentences on the story and one on the *form*. A contest finalist's slot, withdrawn: "Applicants must disclose the use of generative AI in the creation of submitted work; no disclosure was present on your entry." Your story used AI exactly as this book teaches — brainstorming, continuity passes, one line-edited chapter — and you *knew* that, in the way you know your own birthday. You did not know that *this* contest's entry form, in 8-point type below the upload button, wanted that knowledge **in writing before submission**, and that "I figured it was obvious from the drafts" is not a disclosure.

Here's the quieter horror underneath the anecdote: nothing about the *story* was dishonest. The failure was **provenance hygiene** — the habit of knowing *where your assistance came from, what it touched, and what each venue needs to be told* — a skill adjacent to everything this book already trains (session logs, decisions files, pass records: you have *been* keeping provenance; this chapter teaches you to *surface* it, to whom, when, and in what words). The stakes are concrete and asymmetric: get it wrong and you can lose a slot, a contract clause's protection, a reader's trust, or — at the sharp end — a copyright registration's accuracy. Get it right and the paperwork takes ten minutes, because your logs were always there.

This chapter teaches three things in order: **authorship lines** (what plagiarism is, what AI-assisted authorship is, and why disclosure is hygiene rather than confession), **venue fluency** (finding and following any publisher's, contest's, or platform's current AI policy in under ten minutes — because they differ, and difference is the whole game), and **the legal floor** (copyright's human-authorship baseline with jurisdiction boxes, contracts with AI clauses, real people as material, and academic-integrity parallels). ## Plagiarism vs. authorship with AI in the loop

Draw the lines cleanly, because public discussion blurs them into one anxious smear. **Plagiarism** is *presenting someone else's expression as yours* — unattributed copying of a human's prose, ideas credited nowhere, a text lifted and signed. **Provenance fraud** is the new neighbor: presenting *machine-generated material as human-written where the venue's rules required disclosure* — the contest anecdote's actual species (note what it is *not*: not copying, not theft from a person — a *process* violation against a rule, which is why the sanction was withdrawal rather than plagiarism's moral register; mixing the categories rhetorically serves nobody and terrifies beginners into either silence-about-assistance or shame-about-use, both wrong). **AI-assisted authorship** is this book's entire practice: a human's aims, selections, revisions, and final responsibility with machine *labor* in the loop — and its authorship-claim (your name, alone, on your book) is *legitimate* under this book's working definition and under the copyright baseline you'll meet below — **provided the expressive choices that matter are demonstrably yours**: the voice-anchor, decisions-file, pass-log, and criteria machinery from Parts II–III isn't just productivity system — *it is the evidence trail of human authorship* (Chapter 7's row: "authorship evidence, keep factual" — the payoff arriving now: when a venue, a publisher's rights-and-warranties paragraph, or a future registration asks *what you did*, the answer is a folder, not a story you compose under pressure).

**Disclosure is hygiene, not confession.** The word "disclose" spooks writers because it *sounds like* an admission seeking forgiveness. Reframe it with precision: disclosure is a **provenance statement** — *this work was made with X assistance in Y areas, reviewed and revised by me* — the same species as "photos: archive of the author" or "typeset in Garamond": logistics of making, stated because the recipient's decisions depend on them (a contest's judging model, a publisher's risk assessment, a reader's expectations, *your own* registration accuracy). The stance this book takes, stated once and enforced throughout: **when a rule asks, answer fully; when a rule is silent, follow the venue's culture and your logs; when a rule forbids, don't submit — route around, don't rationalize.** Two supporting principles that make the stance *usable* rather than preachy: **never let disclosure be discovered for you** (the withdrawal-email's asymmetry — discovering *you* under-disclosed is infinitely worse than any honest disclosure would have been; over-disclosure costs a sentence, under-disclosure costs a slot), and **disclose at the granularity the rule asks** (some venues want a checkbox; some want a paragraph; some contract riders want *categories* of assistance itemized — the three-length statement you'll draft in the Stretch scales to all three; asking for *more* than the form's granularity wastes the reader's time and buries the answer).

> ETHICS NOTE — The mirror-image rule matters equally: **never claim assistance you didn't use as a badge.** "Written with AI" *brags* circulating as marketing are provenance fraud's backwards cousin — false statements about process corrode the disclosure channel *everyone* relies on (Ch 13's sim-vs-human warning at culture scale: if "AI-assisted" ever means *vibes* rather than *fact*, the term stops protecting the honest and starts indicting everyone). State what happened; let it be boring; boring accuracy is the whole ethic.

## Disclosure norms: verify each venue

Now the operational heart — because *rules differ, and the difference is the game*. **Claim C003 (register): major publishers, contests, magazines, and platforms have *differing* AI-disclosure policies, they change, and authors must check each venue's *current* rules.** The claim's evidence, dated: the policies demonstrably diverge — during the 2023 wave of consumer-model adoption, **Clarkesworld**, a leading SFF magazine, publicly *did not accept* AI-generated or AI-assisted writing per its then-current submission guidelines and, flooded with machine-submissions, *temporarily closed submissions* in February 2023 after over 500 account bans that month (reported Feb 2023 — Washington Post, Vice/Motherboard coverage; *and* the same month, other markets stated the opposite posture — e.g., **Metastellar** publicly announced it did *not* care what tools created a story, only whether it was good (their published position, Feb 2023)). Two reputable venues, *opposite rules, same week* — that's C003's thesis in one exhibit: **there is no industry default to default to.** (Perishable flag: every specific policy quoted here is *historical evidence of divergence*, not current permission — Clarkesworld's and any other venue's present rules must be read *at their guidelines page, dated by you, before submission*; the register's row carries the re-verify date.)

**The ten-minute policy hunt — taught as a repeatable procedure** (this is the actual skill; any example ages, the procedure doesn't):

1. **Find the *guidelines* page, not the marketing page.** Submission portal footer → "submission guidelines" / "author information" / "terms"; for publishers: the imprint's *authors* or *submissions* page; for contests: "rules" or "official rules" (the legally-operative document is usually the word *rules*, and it's where disclosure clauses hide). Screenshot or save-PDF *before* you draft your statement (policies change *while your story is in the queue* — dated evidence of *which* rules you filed under matters if a mid-queue rule-change email arrives).
2. **Ctrl-F the dialect:** "AI," "artificial intelligence," "generative," "machine learning," "ChatGPT"-as-verb (venues name tools when nervous), "disclose," "originality warranty." Read the *whole paragraph* around each hit — disclosure clauses live in *originality warranties* ("the work is your original creation…") more often than in labeled AI-sections; a silent AI-section with a strict originality warranty still *binds* — interpret silences against wishful readings (a venue that says nothing about AI but requires "entirely your original work" has *told you something*; whether assisted drafting breaches *that* wording is exactly the ambiguity you resolve by *asking*, next step).
3. **Check the *form* separately from the *guidelines*** (the hook's lesson: the 8-point clause under the upload button is a *fourth* document — rules page, guidelines page, portal checkboxes, and confirm-email template can each carry disclosure language; four surfaces, four reads).
4. **When silent or ambiguous: *ask in writing* — one email, specific, timestamped.** "Do you require disclosure of AI-assisted revision where all final prose was human-written? [your process in three clauses]" — *their reply is your policy* (file it in `research/venues/` with the date; a written answer beats any reading you could've done — and if they don't answer before the deadline, *disclose conservatively* under the broadest reasonable reading: over-disclosure's cost is one sentence).
5. **Re-check at submission *and* at contract/acceptance** (the mid-queue rule-change reality; also acceptance-time is when *contract riders* arrive — next section).

**The three-venue walkthrough** (the worked example compressed to its findings — one story, three simultaneous subs, the procedure run): *Venue A (magazine):* guidelines page — Ctrl-F "AI" → explicit prohibition *plus* form-checkbox requiring affirmation of no-gen-AI-use → **your process doesn't fit their rule → don't submit (route around: withdraw from consideration list, note in venues-file: "prohibitive — as-of date")** — the stance's third clause enacted without drama; *Venue B (press):* guidelines silent on AI; originality warranty in the contract draft says "your original work product" → **ask-in-writing** → editor's dated reply: "disclose assistance in a cover note; warranty covers final expression" → **cover-note sentence filed (your mid-length statement), proceed**; *Venue C (contest):* rules-page has a *required disclosure field* (character-limited) + portal checkbox + guidelines restatement → **three surfaces agree; fill field with short-form statement; screenshot everything pre-submission; save confirm-email.** Three venues, three behaviors, zero moral complexity — because *each was read at its own sources, dated, in writing* — versus the single behavior the under-checked writer defaults to: silence everywhere, discovered once, catastrophically.

## Copyright basics (with jurisdiction boxes)

The legal floor, kept to **official-source statements only** per the research protocol — with jurisdiction boxes as the outline's structure demands. **The framing everything else hangs on: copyright law is territorial** — rules below are *country-shaped*; your manuscript crosses borders, the *rights* don't travel with it automatically; when money, contracts, or registration enter, the lawyer-trigger (stated plainly in every box) is part of the advice, not an evasion of it.

**United States — the human-authorship baseline (C001).** The U.S. Copyright Office's registration guidance and its subsequent report on AI (Part 2, released January 29, 2025) state the operative rules: **copyright requires human authorship — purely AI-generated material is not copyrightable in the US**, and where a machine produced a work's traditional elements of authorship without sufficient human creative control, that material is excluded from protection; works *containing* AI-assisted material can be registered where the human contributions are sufficient, with **AI-generated portions disclosed and disclaimed** on the application (applicants must identify AI-generated content; the Office's guidance says prompts alone generally do not establish the necessary control over expression); human-authored elements remain protected, and selections/arrangements of AI outputs can qualify as human authorship of the compilation, *not* the underlying generated text itself. Appellate confirmation exists (the D.C. Circuit's 2025 decision in *Thaler v. Perlmutter* affirmed the human-authorship requirement on facts before it — full opinion at the court's official site). **Practical translation for your novel:** (1) *your* copyrightability rests on *your* human expression — the Parts II–III evidence trail is the point; (2) if you ever *register* (Ch 16's checklists touch registration timing), **disclose and disclaim AI-generated material as the guidance instructs** — accurate applications are the registration-side twin of venue-disclosure hygiene; (3) "the model wrote paragraph 3 unedited" = paragraph 3 unprotectable *as such* (your surrounding work unaffected); "the model proposed, I rewrote to my aims" = your paragraph, disclose per guidance as needed. *Confidence: high (official USCO guidance + report + appellate decision); perishable: guidance pages can be superseded — re-check USCO's AI-registration guidance page at use.* **This is information, not legal advice; for registration strategy on a mixed work, consult a US copyright attorney.**

> **Jurisdiction box — United Kingdom.** The UK's Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 contains (in its original provisions) a *computer-generated works* regime — ss.9(3)/12(7) attributing authorship of qualifying computer-generated works to the *maker of the arrangements* for their generation — a *statutory* structure without direct US-equivalence, whose *interaction with modern generative tools in practice* (what counts as "the arrangements," how it meets publishing reality) sits in professional-practice territory this box doesn't adjudicate. **Operative advice:** UK-facing publication or contracts → read the statute's current text at legislation.gov.uk yourself *and* have counsel sort your fact-pattern; disclose per venue rules regardless (disclosure duty and copyrightability are *different questions* — a work can be non-infringing, venue-compliant, *and* still raise authorship-attribution questions the statute words carefully). *Confidence: statute existence/text high; practical application: explicitly out of scope.*

> **Jurisdiction box — European Union.** No single "AI-copyright article" to quote: copyright authorship doctrine lives substantially in member-state law (EU-harmonization reaches *exceptions* and *infringement*, not a uniform authorship code), while the **EU AI Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) imposes *transparency* obligations in other gears — notably marking/identification duties for certain AI-generated or manipulated content (deepfake-class disclosures) as the Act's rules phase in, with the details and application dates at EUR-Lex. **Operative advice:** EU distribution → (a) check the AI Act's transparency obligations *as currently in force* for your content class (official text + the Commission's guidance pages), (b) check the *member-state* your contract is centered in for authorship questions, (c) venue-disclosure as always. *Confidence: Regulation number/text high; obligation-scope-per-content-class: re-verify at official sources at use (phased application dates make single-sentence summaries dangerous).*

> **Jurisdiction box — Brazil.** For this book's purposes the verified floor: **LGPD — Lei nº 13.709/2018** (Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados Pessoais), in force since 2020 after the 2019 sanctions trigger, governs *personal-data processing* by natural and legal persons (official text: Planalto; regulator: ANPD) — which binds Brazilian writers *directly* where your process handles **dados pessoais de pessoas identificáveis**: pasting real individuals' private correspondence, health details, or identifying letters into AI tools is a *data-processing act* with LGPD duties attached (purpose, good faith, necessity — Art. 6 principles) layered on top of the privacy hygiene of Chapter 5 and the fiction-ethics of this chapter's next section. For *copyright/authorship* questions under Brazilian law and for any *national AI-regulation* status (legislative files evolve — check current status at Planalto/Câmara/Senado official trackers rather than trusting any summary *including this one*), consult a Brazilian attorney; this box deliberately states **no rule it hasn't sourced.** *Confidence: LGPD text/force high (official); other BR AI/copyright specifics: unverified by design — pointer-only, per protocol.*

**The lawyer sentence, finally earned:** across all four boxes, the honest professional advice is identical in shape — *the official text beats every summary (including this chapter's), your specific facts beat every general rule, and counsel exists for where those two meet.* Use this chapter to arrive at the lawyer's door *informed* (your process described, your venues listed, your questions specific) instead of *alarmed* — the fee buys the part that *cannot* be sourced generically: your case.

## People, privacy, contracts (and classrooms)

Four adjacent domains, kept practical:

**Real people as characters/input — the fiction-ethics core.** *Publishing* a novel shaped by a real person is lawful in ordinary circumstances (fictionalization is a *craft* tradition — courts and customs protect expressive work built from life); *feeding a real, identifiable person's private material into processing tools* engages the Chapter 5 privacy floor *and* — for identifiable individuals' personal data — data-protection law where applicable (LGPD box above; GDPR-class regimes elsewhere: same pointer-discipline). The operational rules this book enforces: **public facts and your lived observations = normal novelistic material; private records, medical/financial/sexual details, and correspondence = don't paste into tools (fictionalize the load-bearing specifics beyond recognition first, per Ch 5's rule, or don't use the material at all)**; **the living and *identifiable* in *thinly-veiled* form, in a small community, with private details in the text** = defamation-adjacent territory where *accuracy of malice-sensitive inventions* becomes counsel's question (the classic "everyone will know it's X" fact-pattern: talk to a lawyer *before* publication, not after the letters arrive); and **the stranger's dignity test** as the craft-floor: if the portrayal's *distinctiveness* relies on a real group's stereotypes (Ch 11's specificity audit), the fix is craft first — three individual traits or the marker comes off. None of this is new morality; it's Ch 5's paste-rules and Ch 11's representation-rules *pointed at named humans* — the place where they stop being style.

**Contracts and AI clauses.** Rights-and-warranties sections in publishing and agency contracts increasingly *address* AI-assisted works in some of these forms (the clause families to recognize): *warranties that the work is original / free of infringing material* (ask how your disclosed assistance interacts — a disclosure letter *precedes and informs* the warranty reading), *affirmative **representations about AI use*** (categories disclosed, thresholds stated — answer with your Appendix-F-style log's categories, not adjectives), *indemnities tied to provenance claims* (the clause that makes *inaccurate* disclosure expensive — hence: accurate process knowledge before signing), and *positive **AI-use permissions/restrictions*** (some contracts now *grant* limited assistance use or *restrict* it per format/territory — check against your actual process). **The procedure is the same ten-minute discipline as venue policies:** read the *whole* clause family, map it against your logged process (the three-length statement's *long form* is your contract-conversation script), request revisions or clarifications *in writing where the map mismatches*, and — above a material contract's value — **counsel reviews the rider** (the lawyer-sentence's contract edition: you bring the *process map*, they bring the *risk reading*). Never sign a representation about process you can't *substantiate from your logs*; never let a *sales* conversation's casualness ("we don't care about tools") overwrite the *signed* document's carefulness — the rider wins, the email trail is evidence, the hallway is nothing.

**Academic-integrity parallels** (why this paragraph exists: many readers' *first* disclosure habits formed at school, and transfer poorly). Universities, journals, and conferences run the same spectrum as venues — prohibition, disclosure-with-assessment, tool-neutral — with *their own* current policies (institutional offices of research integrity, publisher after-publisher policies for journals); the *transferable skills* from the procedure above: find the *operative rules document* (course syllabus = contract; submission system checkbox = portal-form layer; journal's author-guidelines = guidelines layer — the *four surfaces* pattern again), *ask when silent*, *disclose at the granularity asked*, *keep the record*. The parallel's deeper lesson for fiction writers: academic integrity culture treats *process provenance* as constitutive of the work's validity — fiction's publishing world is arriving at the same place through contracts and contest forms; writers who built provenance habits *before* the pressure arrived (Parts II–III, exactly) experience this shift as *paperwork*, and everyone else experiences it as the hook's email.

### Contracts 101: the clause families a working writer actually meets

The section on AI clauses needs its plain-language foundation — the *rest* of the contract anatomy around which those clauses orbit — because a rights-and-warranties paragraph read *alone* is how smart people sign bad deals (the rider's power is contextual: each clause *buys* something from the other side, and only the *whole map* shows the exchange-rates). The five families, at the literacy level this book can honestly provide (your counsel provides the rest, per the lawyer-sentence):

**Family 1 — Grant of rights (what you give, for how long, where).** The paragraph that *defines the deal*: which rights (print, audio, ebook, film, translation, serial), in which territories (world, or language-by-language), for what term (fixed years, "life of copyright," *reversion triggers* — the out-of-print/sales-threshold conditions under which rights *come home* — the family Ch 16 flagged as small-press contract-craft 101, now at reading-depth: **a grant without a clear reversion path is a permanent donation with paperwork** — the literacy-move: read the grant *backwards* first (find reversion, then read forward — the sequence that keeps the shiny advance-figure from anchoring your read of everything after it); AI-clause adjacency: none directly, but *everything* in Family 4-5 interprets *through* the grant's scope (a warranty about AI-assistance means different risk at "life of copyright, world" versus "five-year English-only" — the map's first rule: **rights first, or rights never.**

**Family 2 — Advance & royalties (the money mechanics).** Advance = pre-paid future royalties (earned-against — your royalties *don't flow* until the advance "earns out" — the mechanism behind every "modest advance" shape in Ch 16, stated mechanically now: *the advance IS your royalty until it isn't*); royalty rates differ by format (print's *percentage-of-list* convention vs. ebook's *percentage-of-net* convention — the two denominators' gap being why "30% ebook royalty" and "8% print royalty" aren't comparable numbers *until you know what they're percentages OF* — the literacy move: **for every rate, ask "percent of WHAT?"** — list price, net receipts, something else — the one question that collapses most royalty-confusion into arithmetic); escalations (rates rising at thresholds), reserves against returns (a withheld slice held back for bookstore returns' eventual accounting — the *quiet* subfamily: how much withheld, how long, reported-when — the clause where "why does my statement show sales I can't be paid for yet" gets answered *on paper or not at all*); AI-clause adjacency: none — but the **statements** (royalty reports) are where any AI-related *audit rights* in Family 5 would eventually *bite* — read the reporting clause while you're in the neighborhood.

**Family 3 — Editorial & production control (whose call).** Cover design, title, schedule, edits' *approval semantics* (consultation vs. approval vs. publisher-final — three *different* contracts wearing one friendly word: "your input matters" maps to any of them, and only the *document's* words count — the Ch 15 sales-conversation rule's structural home: **consultation = they decide after hearing you; approval = you can refuse once, with reasons; publisher-final = the paragraph you accepted by signing it** — the literacy move: find which of the three each key decision *actually* got — title and cover being the two where writers *believe* approval and *signed* consultation, the gap between belief and paper living exactly one clause-family over from the smile in the room); reversion-adjacent: manuscripts *rejected after acceptance* (yes, that's a thing — "we reserve the right not to publish" clauses — where the grant's scope meets *their* delivery obligations — check what happens to your rights if they *take the book and shelve it*: the Family-1 reversion triggers reading differently for *never-published* than *out-of-print* — two *distinct* homecomings, two *distinct* clauses, both worth finding *before* signing, not after the silence).

**Family 4 — Warranties & representations (what you *swear*).** The AI-clause *native ground* (already covered in depth above): originality, non-infringement, *now frequently*: assistance-disclosure representations — read here only to add its literacy-reframe: **warranties are *forward-looking law*** (they convert *your process history* into *contractual liability* — which is precisely why this book's provenance logs and the three-length statement aren't *bureaucracy*: they're the *evidence base* a warranty-reading depends on — the person who can *substantiate* "human-revised, machine-labor disclosed as per Rider C" from session-logs and AI-use rows *answers warranty questions like a professional*; the person *without* the trail negotiates the same paragraph *on vibes* — the contract-family's whole argument for Part II's ledger-discipline arriving four chapters late, right on schedule); indemnities (family-cousin: *you pay their costs* if the warranty proves false — the clause that makes *inaccurate* disclosure expensive in *their* currency — hence: warranty-reads always run *against your actual logs*, never against your *memory of* your process).

**Family 5 — The operational fine-print (assignments, audits, termination, reversion-mechanics).** The family nobody reads until they need: *assignment* (can they sell the contract to someone else — your reversion-terms travel with *whichever company now holds it* — check the assignee-obligations language), *audit rights* (whose books you may inspect to verify royalties — the Family-2 statement's enforcement mirror), *termination* (what breaches end *what*, and what happens to *rights already granted* — the asymmetry-tells: termination that ends *their* obligations but leaves your grant intact is a door built for one direction), and *reversion-mechanics* (how homecoming *actually executes*: notice periods, file-formats-of-return, non-compete-survival clauses — the fine print *around* Family 1's trigger, where "your rights revert" becomes either *two clean emails* or *eighteen months of defined process* — the literacy move: **reversion-mechanics read aloud at signing**, because this paragraph is where future-you's *relationship with the whole deal* gets conducted, and doing it *then*, alone, with a contract's full emotional weight, is exactly the situation the lawyer-sentence's *scheduled* version exists to prevent). The five families' closing pattern — *rights first, percent-of-what second, whose-call third, what-you-swear fourth, the fine print fifth* — a reading order, not a priority order (all five *bind*; the sequence exists because **each family's terms *interpret* the next** — a warranty's risk reads through the grant's scope; a royalty's meaning reads through its denominator; a control-clause's *consultation* reads through the termination-clause's *consequences* — contracts are *arguments*, and arguments have *reading orders*: yours is now the paragraph above, the AI-clause section you've already run, and — through the door marked on every page of this chapter — counsel, arriving *informed*, with the map folded open to the page that needs their eyes.)

### The quarterly provenance review (scheduling the stance)

The stance's instruments (dossier, statements, logs) were built; this section *schedules* them, because provenance rot is *time-shaped*: policies expire, statements drift from evolved processes, screenshots age past relevance — and every rot-pattern this book has catalogued (stale style sheets, un-versioned cards, un-audited instrument-rhythms) says the same thing: **without a calendar slot, maintenance becomes a thing you *remember at the worst moment*.** The quarterly review, specified at Ch 7's audit-rhythm granularity (45 minutes, same Sunday slot family as the monthly audits — the *quarterly* cadence chosen because venue policies and contract-templates update on *roughly* that weather-pattern while your *own* process evolves slower — monthly would waste the slots, annual would let rot outrun detection — and the review inherits the audit's standing format: **instrument rows first, narrative never**):

**Rows (the checklist that IS the review):** **(1) Dossier sweep** — `research/venues/` rows older than ~12 months: re-open each guidelines page *now, dated*, update posture-or-tick the re-verified box (the row's `as-of` field *is* the alarm; the sweep *is* the response — ten venues ≈ fifteen minutes at Try's practiced pace — *this row alone* justifies the quarter); **(2) Statement-to-process drift** — your three-length statements read *against* your current actual process (new mode adopted? new tool category? pass-system changed? — a statement claiming "final prose human-revised" while your practice evolved some new category of machine-touch = the representation *quietly aging into inaccuracy* — the fix's shape: **one paragraph updated per drifted clause, version-dated, old version archived** (append-only, like everything with a shelf-life worth respecting — the statement's *version history* itself becoming provenance-evidence of your attention to accuracy); **(3) Contract-map refresh** — Family-1–5's literacy notes re-read *if* any submission's advancing (active-negotiation quarters run this row *per-opportunity* instead: the review's flexible child — no pending contracts = row ticks "none active" in ten seconds, guilt-free; the row exists so the *first* contract-notice doesn't *become* the review, panicked and 1 a.m.-shaped); **(4) Log-integrity spot-check** — one random week of session-logs + decisions rows *audited for the appendix's own standard* (would these rows *substantiate* a warranty answer? — the self-as-subject discipline: **your provenance trail gets the same quarterly inspection you'd give any instrument claiming to be evidence** — three rows read, gaps noted, cause-rows filed if the trail's been thinning: the FAQ from Ch 7's neighboring territory — *logs kept for capacity* double as *logs kept for accountability*, and this row keeps the double-duty *honest*); **(5) Perishables-bridge** — Appendix E's table *consulted* (not re-run: that's pre-publication's job — this row just checks: any *new* claim-rows added since the last review that lack source+dates? — the register's **append-hygiene** caught quarterly rather than at the worst possible deadline; **(6) Calendaring the next one** — the review's *own* re-appearance scheduled before you close the laptop (the calendar-closes-the-loop rule from Ch 16, applied to *this* system: a provenance review that doesn't book itself becomes a *nice intention with a folder* — the review's deepest pattern is its *boringness*: six rows, forty-five minutes, one dated-tick per instrument — the stance from the chapter's opening *operationalized into maintenance*, which is what "hygiene" meant all along: **not a virtue, a schedule — not confessed, performed — quarterly, dated, boring, and the reason the hook's withdrawal-email finds *someone else* in its inbox for the rest of your working life.**

## Worked example: checking three venues for one story

The procedure at full resolution (Example A's "Low Tide" going out — the *walkthrough structure* you'll copy; findings abridged to what teaches):

**Day 1, Venue A (the prohibitive magazine).** Guidelines page saved PDF (`venues/A-guidelines-2026-09-2X.pdf`); Ctrl-F "AI" → hit in originality section: *explicit non-acceptance of AI-generated or AI-assisted writing* + portal shows the affirmation checkbox (surface 3 — found *because* the hunt checked the form separately) + confirm-email template read (surface 4, via last year's confirmation saved in `venues/`): restates affirmation. **Verdict: prohibitive → do not submit → `venues/` row: "A · prohibitive · as-of 2026-09-2X · screenshot ✔".** The route-around logged *without* hand-wringing (the stance's third clause — and a *data point for C003's appendix note*: divergence documented with a dated artifact, the register's evidence-chain satisfied by *your* hunt, not a remembered blog post).

## Pitfalls

> PITFALL — **Assuming the rules ("everyone does it" ≠ allowed).** *Symptom:* submission decisions made from forum vibes, writers'-group anecdotes, or a *different* venue's permissiveness ("Clarkesworld-*style* markets don't care anymore" — venues aren't styles, they're *documents with dates*); disclosure skipped because the process "felt normal"; contracts signed because the call was friendly; the deep version: *policy-surfaced-by-panic* — discovering a rule only at the rejection/withdrawal/claim letter, then interpreting it in the worst possible light at 1 a.m. with no artifacts. *Why it happens:* **normalization drift** (assistance became habitual in your *studio* — you measurably forgot which parts were even *assistance-worthy* of disclosure without your logs — the forgetting is invisible until a form *asks*, which is why the logs were never bureaucracy); **ambiguity-optimism** (silence read as permission when silence actually means *ask* — the motivated reading of an ambiguous warranty is *always* the permissive one, by definition of motivation — hence the written-ask rule *mechanizing* what intuition will always distort); **category collapse** (plagiarism-panic generalizes to disclosure-panic and vice versa — the smear from the chapter's opening: writers either hide *routine* assistance as if it were theft, or *rationalize* wholesale unattributed generation as if venues owed them assumptions — both are the *same* error: no working vocabulary for the actual distinctions (plagiarism / provenance fraud / assistance) the chapter installed); and **artifact-absence** ("I didn't screenshot the rules page" — mid-queue policy changes then become *their word against your memory*, and memory always loses contractually). *Fix (the procedure, one more time as a checklist — it *is* the fix):* (1) **posture-before-submit, every time:** four-surface hunt → dated row in the dossier → verdict from the *document's own words* (prohibitive / disclosure-required / ask / silent-warranty) — fifteen minutes, Try's target pace; (2) **silence → written ask → conservative answer if silence persists** (B's template; over-disclosure's one sentence beats under-discovery's withdrawal); (3) **three-length statement maintained from *actual logs*** (the Stretch's drafts, refreshed at each quarterly review — a statement that can't be substantiated row-by-row isn't a disclosure, it's a hope); (4) **artifacts at every layer** (PDFs/screenshots dated at *submission*, saved at *acceptance* — the dossier's evidence-chain feeding C003's own re-verification the way Appendix E prescribes: your hunts *are* the counter-evidence-gathering the register asks for); (5) **vocabulary discipline in your own communications** (say which of the three species you mean — "disclosed assistance" ≠ "plagiarized," and *both* directions of the conflation get *corrected once, politely, in writing* — public discourse will keep smearing the categories; your records and your sentences *won't*); (6) **contract/warranty map before signing** (long statement × clause family × counsel where value warrants — the lawyer-sentence *scheduled*, not summoned); (7) **the quarterly dossier review** (policies rot — dates expire — the review lives alongside Ch 7's audits because *staleness is an instrument-maintenance problem, not a morality problem*). *Prevention (steady state):* the whole chapter collapses into four habits once internalized — **posture-hunt before every submission; ## Key takeaways

- **Three species, one vocabulary:** plagiarism (someone else's expression, unattributed), provenance fraud (process violations against a venue's disclosure rule), AI-assisted authorship (your aims + machine labor + your responsibility, *evidenced* by the Parts II–III trail) — confusion between them is the public smear *and* the private panic; precise words dissolve both.
- **Disclosure is retrieval, not confession:** statements drafted from logs at three lengths, submitted at whatever granularity each rule asks, never discovered for you — over-disclosure costs a sentence; under-disclosure costs the slot (and mid-queue rule-changes cost the *screenshot*).
- **C003 is the operational thesis: venues differ, policies rot, the four-surface hunt with dated artifacts is the only reliable method** — rules pages, guidelines, portal forms, and confirm-emails each *bind separately*; silence means *ask in writing* (their reply becomes your policy), prohibitions mean *route around*, and your dossier *is* the dated evidence-chain the claim register wants.
- **The copyright floor is territorial and human-authorship-shaped:** US baseline (official USCO guidance/report + appellate confirmation: purely AI-generated material uncopyrightable; disclose/disclaim on registration; prompts alone ≠ control; consult counsel for your facts), with honest jurisdiction boxes for UK (statutory computer-generated-works regime — text at legislation.gov.uk + counsel), EU (member-state authorship + AI Act transparency as-in-force at EUR-Lex), and BR (LGPD floor verified at Planalto — personal-data processing binds your *process*; everything else pointer-only) — **official text beats every summary including this chapter's.**
- **People, contracts, and classrooms run the same drill at higher stakes:** private material about identifiable humans never pasted (fictionalize or don't), thin-veils before publication get counsel, contract clause-families get mapped against your substantiable logs before signing — provenance habits formed *before* the pressure arrive as paperwork; everyone else meets them as the hook's email. Four habits close the chapter: **posture-hunt, ask-on-silence, statements-from-logs, artifacts-dated** — boredom as the target feeling of every disclosure you'll ever file.
