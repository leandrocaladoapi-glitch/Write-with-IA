## Opening hook

Two beta readers finish your manuscript the same evening. Reader one texts: *"Loved it!! Couldn't put it down 😍"* — you feel nine seconds of warmth and zero information. Reader two texts: *"Not sure it worked for me — the middle dragged a bit, maybe the sister? Anyway, congrats!!"* — you feel six hours of spiraling and still zero *usable* information, because somewhere in "not sure" and "maybe" and "dragged a bit" are three different complaints, at least one of which might be about a chapter that doesn't exist in this draft.

These are the two useless feedbacks — **empty praise** and **unlocated unease** — and they are not reader failures. *You* produced them, by asking badly: "what did you think?" is a question that returns mood, not diagnosis, because mood is the only instrument an unbriefed reader knows how to hand you. A reader with three *specific* questions — "at any point did you skim? name the paragraph you noticed it" / "whose side were you on in the kitchen fight, and where did that shift?" / "what did you believe Mara wanted by the midpoint, and which line told you?" — returns something else entirely: *located testimony*, data you can triage, argue with, and convert into a revision plan with your name on it.

This chapter is the design of that asking. **Briefing humans well** (PC-23's question craft — who to ask, what to send, how to receive), **simulated perspectives as a pre-flight check** (PC-24 — with the caveats this book owes you in full: what an AI "reader" can and *cannot* tell you, stated twice), and **triage** (PC-25 plus the feedback-to-changes table — the instrument that turns contradictory opinions into an ordered revision plan instead of a popularity contest). Example A runs one full round — real-pattern feedback, real contradictions, table and all. By the end: your draft has *questions written for its betas*, one round triaged into rows, and your own stated position on what simulated feedback cannot know.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- [ ] Brief beta readers with questions that yield usable answers (PC-23).
- [ ] Use AI-simulated reader perspectives (PC-24) — knowing exactly what they can't do.
- [ ] Triage contradictory feedback into a revision plan (PC-25).
- [ ] Maintain a feedback-to-changes table and derive questions from your own pass logs.

## Briefing beta readers

**The question design principles** — five, in order of how much they change the quality of what comes back:

1. **Ask for *events in reading*, not *evaluations of the book*.** "Where did you skim?" not "was the pacing good?" — "which line told you what Mara wanted?" not "was her arc clear?" — testimony about *what happened to you* is checkable against the draft; evaluation is a mood with a review's vocabulary stapled on. The rule behind the rule: **every question must be answerable with a location + a memory** (a paragraph, a moment, a line). If the answer can't have an address, rewrite the question until it can — address-less questions are the "not sure… maybe the sister" species being invited in for dinner.

2. **Ask about the *reader's* behavior and belief, never about *your* craft.** Readers are reliable witnesses to themselves (what *they* did, felt, assumed, predicted) and unreliable critics of technique (they'll say "the dialogue was exposition-dumpy" — a craft verdict their evidence doesn't support — when the *real* data was "I skimmed Theo's second speech about the roof"). Design questions that harvest the witness domain: *what did you expect? when did you stop believing? what were you thinking during the slow part?* — then *you* do the craft translation, because you have the pass logs to translate *with* (PC-20's sag-locations meeting a reader's skim-confession: now two instruments triangulate the same paragraph).

3. **Cap the ask, rank the ask.** Ten questions maximum — a fifteen-question beta questionnaire returns *answers to questions 1–3 and vibes about 4–10* (respondents' attention is a budget; respect it or get mush). Within the ten: *ranked* ("if you could fix only ONE thing, what? — the single most valuable beta question ever designed, because forced ranking *cannot* hide behind politeness: whatever tops the list IS their top finding, full stop"). The ranked-one also calibrates everything else they say: if their #1 is something your pass logs already flagged, the human and your instruments agree (corroboration — the pattern Ch 12 taught you to treasure); if it's a surprise, the surprise gets top triage priority (new external information outranks internal consensus — the cycle-breaker rule).

4. **Brief on *context*, blind on *answers*.** Send: the read-before's (what stage this is — "draft post-pass-5, structure locked, line pass done — I want *reader* experience, not proofreading"; a one-paragraph story context only if the story needs it; length expectations + a *deadline* — even a soft one, because betas without dates are betas in the void). Send *without*: your anxieties ("I'm worried the middle sags") — the moment you name a fear, you *buy* a finding of that name from every agreeable reader in your stable (sycophancy's human cousin: you've given them a script); your preferred interpretation ("I *meant* the journal to feel ambiguous") — you'll get your interpretation back, laundered as theirs, and the round's entire information value dies. *What you want is their honest first architecture of your story — which only exists if you didn't help them build it.* Two audiences can be briefed differently (a craft-literate friend may take craft-worded questions; a "reader's reader" gets pure witness questions — matching the instrument to the person is good design, not bias).

5. **What to send, how to receive.** Send the *manuscript file* (not screenshots, not paste-in-chat walls), with a companion one-pager: the questions verbatim, the deadline, "wrong-and-specific beats right-and-vague every time," "please quote line numbers when you can," and explicit permission to be critical ("I will not be hurt by 'I stopped caring on page 40' — I will be hurt by silence"). Receive: in *writing* first (voice notes and calls are richer *after* you've got their written pass — people soften live, and you'll spend the call managing their feelings instead of harvesting data), and receive *gratitude separate from analysis* — thank them warmly in a reply that does NOT ask them to revise their opinions ("really appreciate it" — full stop; "appreciate it — were you *sure* about the sister note?" is how you accidentally negotiate findings into something comfortable). Keep their words *verbatim* in `feedback/round-1/` — the table's source layer, never paraphrased at intake (triage runs on quotes; paraphrase is already your interpretation contaminating the data).

What you do *not* need: ten readers, a writing group's full roster, or anyone you'll be unable to disagree with later (triage's vision-check is impossible against your thesis advisor — choose betas you can *overrule without dread*; the relationship cost of overruling some readers is a real design constraint on who gets to read).

## Simulated perspectives (with caveats)

Now the chapter's most careful section — PC-24, used honestly. **What the tool actually is:** an AI-simulated *reader persona* run against your pages: you instruct the model to respond *as* a specified reader-profile ("avid cozy-mystery reader, 58, hates on-page violence, reads 60 books a year") and report their *simulated* experience using the same witness-forms as your human questionnaire (skim-locations, belief-states, predictions, residue). **The legitimate use — a pre-flight check, only:** (1) **questionnaire rehearsal** — run PC-23's output *against a simulated target-reader* first: if the witness-forms confuse a simulation, they'll confuse humans; revise wording *before* sending (the cheapest possible pilot); (2) **blind-spot hunting on YOUR flags** — simulated readers are *very* willing to skim-report on command (no social cost), so "locate three skim-candidates in chapter 7" returns candidates you then *verify yourself against your pass logs and eyes* — the simulation's value is *proposal generation* (Ch 1's golden rule in feedback-clothes: it proposes, you verify); (3) **prediction-rehearsal** — "as this profile, what would you predict the ending does — now read only the outline's ending line — did the prediction survive?" (a fast way to find *mis-signposted* turns: if the simulated reader's prediction was never made *possible* by your pages, your signs are missing or wrong — then *you* re-read and decide — the check proposes, you adjudicate); (4) **coverage-gap questions** — "what would a reader in profile *notice that my ten questions didn't ask about*?" (questionnaire QA again — meta-questions, not draft verdicts).

**What it cannot do — stated plainly, because the outline demands hedging or cutting and this book hedges *once, hard,* then enforces:** simulated perspectives **cannot read**, in the human sense: they have no *stakes* in your characters (no remembered sister of their own surfacing at your family scene), no *boredom* (a simulated "I skimmed here" is *generated text about skimming* — pattern-completed from what skimming-reports look like, not *produced by attention failing*), no **taste-histories** (your target-reader's 60 books a year built *expectations in a nervous system*; the persona prompt builds *a paragraph of description* of such a person), and no **money, evening, or goodwill** at risk (the one resource honest readers spend — "I gave this my Tuesday night" — cannot be simulated because it's the *source* of testimony's authority). Consequently: simulated findings about *your draft's quality* carry **no independent evidential weight** — they are *distorted mirrors of your own prompt and the model's prior about readers like the persona*, and the model's prior is (Chapter 11's thesis, reader-edition) *agreeable-average*: simulated readers skew kind, skew toward *your implied preferences* (the persona you write is flattered by construction — "well-written literary pacing enthusiast" will *find* well-written literary pacing; you built the confirmation into the casting call), and skew *literacy-forward* (they "read" like fast, verbal, patient people — never like your actual least-verbal target reader). **One field note carries the whole warning:** every simulated finding worth acting on must pass *your* verification against pages and human logs — and if a simulated finding ever *contradicts* a human beta's, the human's testimony is evidence and the simulation's is *chat*; there is no scenario in this book where it reverses.

> ETHICS NOTE — Never *represent* simulated feedback as human in any shared context — not to your writing group ("my readers said…" when your readers were a prompt), not to an agent or publisher's questionnaire ("beta-tested with n readers" — the n *counts humans*), not even in your own session log (the Mode line says `simulated pre-flight`, never "beta"). The reason is Ch 15's seed: readers, collaborators, and venues are owed *accurate provenance* about who experienced the text — and a writing culture where "betas" quietly means "chat" burns the very testimony your craft depends on. Log them separately (`feedback/sim-preflight/`), count them separately, and let their folder name enforce the honesty you'll be tempted to blur at 11 p.m.

> REALITY CHECK — The deepest limit: simulated feedback **cannot surprise you with someone else's life.** Its surprises are *your prompt's* surprises (and the model's priors) — compressible back into things you already half-knew. Human betas surprise you with the sister *they* had, the father-death they skipped their own book tour for, the page where *their* marriage made them look away — irreducible data from outside your system, which is what feedback is *for*. That's the whole asymmetry in one line: **simulations stress-test your questions; humans stress-test your story.** Use PC-24 as the rehearsal room before the audition — never confuse the rehearsal with an audience.

## Triaging contradiction

Round one returns. Three readers, ten questions each, thirty answers — and they *conflict* (they always do: one says Chapter 7 is the heart, one says cut it; one read Mara as hero, one as villain-in-waiting — the latter two *thrilling* rather than problematic: **contradiction means your story has voltage** — readers wrestling the same text from different positions is the literature doing its job; unanimity, by contrast, is either a masterpiece or a questionnaire you designed too softly). The instrument that converts thirty answers into a plan:

**Step 1 — Intake verbatim, one row per finding.** `feedback/round-1/` files + the table born: every *located* finding gets a row: `ID | reader (R1/R2/R3) | question# | VERBATIM quote | location | type (experience/belief/prediction/accuracy/rank-1) | first-read`. Unaddressed-feeling answers ("loved it!!") enter as `PRAISE-ONLY` rows — kept for morale and *pattern-counting* (three praise-rows naming the *same* scene = that scene works; praise has data *if you tabulate it*), never for revision. Verbatim rule again: the quote is the evidence your later vision-check stands on; paraphrase at intake poisons every step downstream.

**Step 2 — Cluster by *location*, not by *theme*.** Tempting to group "pacing comments" together; instead group by **paragraph/scene address** — why: the same location accumulating *different complaints* ("dragged" + "beautiful" + "confused me") reveals the real phenomenon (a scene doing two jobs badly or one job controversially), while *theme-clusters* ("three readers mentioned pacing") launder the fact that they meant three *different* sections. Location-clustering is Ch 12's corroboration instinct (independent instruments on same address) at feedback scale: **same-address findings from different readers = highest-confidence rows in the entire round**; same-theme-different-address = separate problems wearing a shared word.

**Step 3 — The vision check (the anti-F-08 core).** Before *any* row becomes a change: hold it against your **stated vision** — the skeleton's stakes sentence, the done-criteria file, the outline's deliberate waivers, style-sheet bans — and assign one of four verdicts:

- **CHANGE** — finding agrees with vision + appears at an address (change is now *execution*: the row drafts its own fix note from the quote+address).
- **DISCUSS/DEFER** — plausible, vision-silent: queue for your own re-read *at that address only* (not a spiral — a located re-read with the row's quote in hand), verdict inside one week or it dies to `parked-feedback`.
- **OVERRIDE** — finding contradicts vision *by design* ("7th reader ever to say 'cut the slow chapter' — vision says the slow chapter *is* the book's argument; waiver from pass 1, cited"): **the row stays in the table with the override reason** — overruling *documented*, never deleted (kindness to future rounds: next beta asks again, the pre-written reason answers them in seconds).
- **FIX-THE-QUESTION** — finding reveals *their* misreading that your *signs* caused (reader thought Mara was villain-in-waiting because chapter 3's POV withheld *one* piece of information the design said they'd get — the finding is a *signposting defect*, not a character defect): verdict changes *what* changes (add the sign, not the trait). This fourth verdict exists because beginners default to the first three and **reader misreadings are overwhelmingly design data** — but the design being wrong and the *reader* being wrong are different diseases with opposite treatments; naming verdict four forces you to diagnose which before prescribing.

**The vision check's arithmetic:** CHANGE-rows sorted by *cluster size × address-criticality* (same-address-multi-reader + climax-adjacent = top), OVERRIDE-rows never re-litigated in-session (the reason cited is closed), and **no round ever reopens frozen structure without a Ch 9-style mint** (feedback proposes; the two-rule stand governs structure edits — beta-driven outline changes go *through* v-next + changelog like any discovery-draft disproof: "reader round 1 suggested X → adjudicated CHANGE at structure altitude → outline v4" — the table *feeds* passes; it doesn't *replace* their discipline).

The manual version (spreadsheet, no card) works fine at n=30 — the card's real gift is **rounds 2, 3, and the novel-length future** where intake rows hit three figures and clerical体力 (clerical体力 → clerical *labor*) is where tired triagers start *merging contradictions by accident*. Restate the standing order: **triage proposes, vision disposes, passes execute, table remembers** — four verbs, one owner.

## Worked example: Example A's feedback round

The system end-to-end on your running story — intake through table, abridged where the card-section specified the format:

**Setup (the briefing done right):** three betas — R1 (target audience: literary-short voracious, our "hungry reader"), R2 (craft-literate: runs a critique group), R3 (honest-stranger: novelist-friend who owes no one agreement); R4 (domain-expert: retired harbor pilot — dock accuracy Q only, briefed *separately* with just S-type questions, explicit "not a reader-response round — accuracy rows only"). Questionnaire: PC-23's ten, designed against draft-09's open flags (the card's context pack = Ch 12's blind-spot section + tracker edges + the ch7 PC-21 spike — *three* flags hiding in Q2's neutral wording, per the design rule). Simulated pre-flight ran *first* (one persona: "avid literary-short reader, skims anything with explained feelings"): its Q4-flagged question ("Q5's 'whose side' wording — persona answered vaguely") → *reworded* before sending to humans ("at what moment did you know whose side you were on — and what told you"); three meta-missed witness-questions *considered*, one added (Q6-trust moved from pass-derived to persona-suggested pure-witness form). **The pre-flight's entire yield: two questionnaire edits** — rehearsal room used as rehearsal room; the sim folder says so on its face (disclaimer line intact).

## Pitfalls

> PITFALL — **Pleasing every reader (F-08's root).** *Symptom:* contradictory notes applied *blindly* — both readers' fixes attempted, producing a draft that hedges both ways (Chapter 7 cut *and* expanded, ending image both trimmed *and* added — the compromised mush that reads as no one's decision); or the adjacent paralysis: thirty findings, zero verdicts, the table rotting as "still reviewing" for a month — revision anxiety wearing *thoroughness*'s clothes; or its mirror failure: *one* loud reader's taste colonizing the book (the most agreeable beta becomes de-facto co-author because contradicting them felt rude). *Why it happens:* **no stated vision to check against** (without your stakes sentence + criteria + waivers *written down*, "the reader wants X" has no counterparty — their opinion fills the vacuum by default — F-08 isn't a reader problem, it's an *empty-chair* problem: the chair is *yours*, and you weren't sitting in it); **no verdict vocabulary** (rows that can only be "do / not do" force binary panic on findings that are *four different kinds of problems* — the vocabulary is what turns thirty vibes into a week's schedule); **no clocks** (addressless and expired findings live forever in an ungoverned table — paralysis is *stasis by no-expiry*, the mirror of blind-pleasing's *motion by no-vision*); and **kindness without separation** (thanking *after* negotiating, following up "just to check if they were sure" — every soft re-contact re-opens verdicts that needed closing; the *relationship* instinct deployed at the wrong *stage* of the pipeline). *Fix (operational, in pipeline order):* (1) **pre-write the vision pack** *before intake* — stakes sentence + criteria headline + active waivers + bans, one page, pasted at the top of every triage session (the chair gets sat in *before* the guests speak — PC-25's design enforces it by making the pack a required context); (2) **triage with the four verdicts *only*** — every row leaves a session with CHANGE/DISCUSS/OVERRIDE/FIX-THE-QUESTION or an expiry date stamped — the two failure states are structurally impossible once verdicts *and* clocks exist (unverdicted >7 days = the F-08 alarm trips in your weekly review — Ch 7's audit catches it like any instrument); (3) **contradictions stay pairs through the vision check** (never averaged at intake — PC-25's merge-ban; the *vision* decides which side serves the book, and often neither verbatim: verdict-four's "the fight needed a *sign*, not a side" pattern); (4) **rank-1 arithmetic over loudness** (forced rankings *democratize* the table — three quiet readers' aligned rank-1s outweigh one vivid monologue — and the corroboration-count column means *no single reader's prose style* can intimidate the plan); (5) **stage-separated gratitude** (thank at intake-close — warm, complete, *question-free*; re-contact only with the addressless-finding follow-up protocol, once, deadline'd; never to *relitigate* — a verdict re-opened by charm is a verdict that doesn't exist); and (6) **the round-two verification design** (verdict-four fixes and sign-changes get *fresh-reader questions* next round — because the deepest form of pleasing-everyone is *assuming you fixed what they felt*: measure, don't assume — the table's Status column closes on *evidence*, not on effort). *Prevention* (the steady state): the vision pack *versioned alongside done-criteria* (aims documents co-evolve at deliberate mints only — criteria-v2 discipline from Ch 12); triage as a *scheduled block* with the weekly audit counting unverdicted rows (instrumentation again — F-08 is invisible without a denominator); and the standing identity-check: **betas are instruments with feelings you owe kindness to, not *audiences whose approval you need* — the moment their verdict feels like a verdict *on you*, close the table, walk, re-open when the sentence "I decide what this book is" is remembered rather than performed.** The golden rule, at feedback altitude: *readers testify, the table triages, vision disposes — you decide, they propose.*

## Key takeaways

- **Unbriefed readers return mood; briefed readers return testimony.** Design questions for *events in reading* — answerable with location + memory, ten max, forced ranking at Q1, your own known flags converted to *witness-form at design time* (never "did it sag?" — "name the paragraph you sped through"), and one explicit permission to destroy; brief on stage, blind on your anxieties and preferred readings.
- **Simulated perspectives are rehearsal, not audience:** legitimate yields are questionnaire pilots, verified skim-candidates, prediction-rehearsal, and meta-questionnaire QA — findings carry *no independent evidential weight* (generated about skimming ≠ produced by attention failing; personas inherit agreeable-average priors *and your own casting*); humans can surprise you with their lives, mirrors cannot — **if a simulation contradicts a human, the human is evidence and the mirror is chat.** Provenance stays honest in every log (sim ≠ beta), and privacy rules bind persona-writing like all character work.
- **Triage is a vocabulary plus clocks:** intake verbatim → cluster *by location* (same-address corroboration is the round's gold) → four verdicts (CHANGE / DISCUSS-with-deadline / OVERRIDE-with-cited-reason / FIX-THE-QUESTION — misreadings are *design data* more often than reader error) → feedback-to-changes table with statuses, passes named, rank-1 arithmetic over loudness → passes execute what the table schedules.
- **Contradiction means voltage, not failure** — the vision pack (stakes, criteria, waivers, bans — written *before* intake) is the chair you sit in while readers speak; without it, any reader's prose becomes policy; with it, even your favorite beta gets overridden *documented, kindly, and finally*.
- **Iteration is a designed loop, not a panic:** round 1 pre-passes, round 2 verifies verdict-four fixes with *fresh* readers, praise-only rows tabulate as positive controls, and the round closes with the only metric that matters — zero unverdicted rows over seven days. You decide; they testify; the table remembers — and the finished pages now carry external evidence into Part IV: your voice to protect (Ch 14), your rights to declare (Ch 15), your path to choose (Ch 16).
