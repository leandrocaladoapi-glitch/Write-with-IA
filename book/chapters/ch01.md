## Opening hook

You have a premise. A good one, you think: *a woman inherits a house whose previous owner left a letter in every wall.* You sit down to write the opening, and an hour later you have four sentences you are not sure about and a growing suspicion that the idea was better before you touched it.

Now imagine opening your AI assistant instead. In eight seconds it gives you a polished opening paragraph: the wind, the gothic house, the letter "clutched in her trembling hand," the darkness "like a shroud." It is fluent. It is atmospheric. It is, in a way that is hard to pinpoint, about nothing. You have read a thousand paragraphs like it. So has every editor alive.

Here is the same premise handled a different way. You do not ask the assistant to write. You ask it three questions. *What can the letter's previous owner plausibly want from a stranger a century later? Give me five answers that are selfish, not noble. Which of these five creates the worst possible conflict with my protagonist's situation — she is mid-divorce and needs cash?* Twenty seconds later you have a premise with teeth: the letters are instructions for hiding a debt that is legally hers now.

Look at what happened in the second version. The machine did not write a word of your story. It did the job a writing partner does at a café: it held the mirror, generated options, and took the hits when you said "no, boring, next." You made every decision that matters — what counts as selfish, what counts as conflict, which answer becomes canon. That split of labor is the entire book in one gesture: **you decide, AI proposes.**

Most writing advice about AI falls into one of two ditches. In one ditch, AI is a ghostwriter — press a button, receive a novel, collect royalties. In the other, AI is a contaminant — real writers never touch it, and anything it produces is automatically soulless. Both ditches are comfortable, and both are wrong. The interesting territory is the road between them, where a beginner with no experience and no mentor can have, for the first time, a tireless idea partner, a structural critic available at midnight, and a continuity checker with perfect memory for the facts they feed it — *and* remains, at every step, the author.

This chapter sets the terms of that arrangement. You will learn what the tool is genuinely good at and genuinely bad at, meet the golden rule that governs every stage of the workflow, and see the whole map of the book before we start walking it.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

- [ ] Describe the co-creation model: you decide, AI proposes.
- [ ] Name three things AI does well and three it does poorly for fiction.
- [ ] State the book's golden rule and how it applies at every stage.
- [ ] Map the full workflow (idea → outline → draft → revision) before starting.

## What AI is genuinely good at

Before you can use a tool well, you need an honest inventory of it — not the brochure and not the horror story. Language models (the technology inside AI assistants; Chapter 5 explains the mechanics) have a shape. Used along the shape, they feel like magic. Used against it, they feel like arguing with a confident stranger.

Here is the inventory as it applies to fiction writers, earned from how these tools actually behave in writing workflows.

**Variation, at volume.** Ask a human writing group for five ways a scene could go wrong and you will get five good answers by tomorrow. Ask an assistant for fifty and you will have them before your coffee cools — and while most will be mediocre, the *distribution* is the value. Options you would never have generated yourself appear: the letter is a confession, a map, a bill, a love letter to the protagonist's mother, a receipt. You are not outsourcing taste; you are widening the search space that taste selects from. Chapters 8 and 9 lean on this constantly.

**Brainstorming without ego.** Your writing group gets tired. Your partner tunes out. Your assistant will interrogate your premise for an hour without taking your rejection personally. Crucially, it will also generate *against* itself: "here are three objections to that idea," "what would a skeptical editor say?" Adversarial prompting — making the machine argue the other side — is one of the highest-value tricks in this book, and it works because the machine has no stake in being liked. (It has a *tendency* to be agreeable anyway; Chapter 8 shows how to beat that out of it.)

**Consistency checks over long material.** Humans miss things. You will not remember that your protagonist's dead father had blue eyes in Chapter 2 and brown in Chapter 19, or that the harvest festival could not have happened in March, or that you already used the exact phrase "a silence stretched between them" four scenes ago. Given your story bible (a reference document holding your story's facts — you will build one in Chapter 7) and your chapters, an assistant will catch continuity errors with a patience no human reader possesses. It is the single most reliable AI contribution in the entire workflow, and Chapter 12 puts it to work systematically.

**Tireless revision help.** "Try a version where the dialogue is colder." "Same paragraph, half the length." "Read this chapter and tell me where you got bored." Each of these is cheap for the machine and expensive for humans. The economics of iteration change: you can afford to see ten versions of a paragraph where you would have settled for two.

**Low-stakes practice partner.** You can ask the questions you would be embarrassed to ask a mentor. *What is a logline? Why is my ending unsatisfying? Explain that critique like I'm five.* It never grades you. For beginners — who often quit not from lack of talent but from the loneliness of not knowing the vocabulary — this is quietly revolutionary.

A specimen of the third item, because consistency checking is so reliable it is easy to under-appreciate until you watch it work. Suppose your story bible says: *Mara's brother Theo — mechanic, left hand scarred from a garage accident at 17.* You paste chapters 4 through 9 and ask:

```text
Using only the facts in the story bible section below, list any passage
in these chapters that contradicts a bible fact. Quote the passage, quote
the conflicting fact, chapter number. If none, say "no contradictions."
Do not comment on style.
```

The assistant returns, in part: *Ch. 6, para. 2: "The scar on Theo's right palm" — bible: left hand. Ch. 9, scene 3: Theo says the accident happened "the summer I turned eighteen" — bible: at 17.* Both are real. Both are invisible by chapter 9 to the author who wrote them in a Tuesday haze. Note the prompt's guardrails: "using only the bible" prevents the machine from inventing *new* facts about Theo, "quote both sides" makes every hit auditable in seconds, and "no style commentary" keeps the pass single-purpose — the discipline you will systematize in Chapter 12. This is not a hypothetical demonstration; it is the routine shape of every continuity pass in this book, and it works on mainstream assistants today without any special configuration.

What do the productivity studies say? Be careful here; numbers travel badly. A randomized experiment by researchers Noy and Zhang, published in *Science* in 2023, gave 453 college-educated professionals mid-level writing tasks: those with ChatGPT access finished about **40% faster** and their outputs were judged about **18% higher in quality** than those without. A separate field study of **5,179 customer-support agents** (Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond) found AI assistance raised issues resolved per hour by **14% on average — and about 34% for novice and low-skilled workers**, with minimal effect on the most experienced. Two things follow for us. First: for *writing-like tasks*, assistance reliably saves time and often helps quality — especially for beginners, which describes you. Second — and this is the part that never makes the headlines — these were short professional tasks, not novels. Customer support tickets are not literary fiction. The honest reading is "assistants help measurable writing work, most of all for novices," not "AI will finish your book for you." We will keep repeating that distinction for the rest of the book.

> REALITY CHECK — Every figure in the paragraph above is perishable: it describes tools and studies with dates attached. The claim register (Appendix E) carries the sources and the re-verification dates. When a capability or statistic is load-bearing for your decisions, date-stamp it — or don't use it.

## What AI is genuinely bad at

An honest inventory has a second column. Some of these limitations are engineering problems that may shrink; some are structural to how the technology works; one is so fundamental it defines the division of labor in this book. Learn the column well — it is where your authorship lives.

**Taste.** The machine cannot tell you whether your ending is *good*. It can tell you whether an ending resembles endings it has seen, and "resembles what has been seen" is the opposite of the judgment you are trying to exercise. Taste is the ability to prefer the right risk — to sense that the quieter ending is truer even though the explosive one is more conventional. That ability comes from your reading, your values, your obsessions. When you outsource it, you do not get the machine's taste (it has none); you get the statistical average of everyone else's. This is the failure we file as *letting AI set the taste*, and it is the first pitfall of this chapter.

**Lived experience.** The assistant has not been betrayed, has not buried anyone, has not stood in a hospital corridor at four in the morning deciding what to say next. It has read accounts of these things — an ocean of them — and can remix their *signatures* convincingly. But a story's power usually comes from the specific detail that only a witness would know: the exact way the vending machine light hums while you wait for news. The machine can invent plausible details; you must supply true ones. This is why the book's exercises keep pushing you toward your own memory, even in the AI-heavy chapters.

**True novelty.** Closely related: the model's job is prediction, and prediction favors the probable. Left alone, it drifts toward the center of the distribution — the twist that has been used, the backstory with the rounded corners, the villain whose motivations arrive pre-sanded. "Surprise me" in a prompt helps less than you would hope, because the surprise it picks is the most *statistically available* surprise. Novelty has to be engineered in by you: through constraints, through combinations, through the collision of your weird specific interests with the machine's range. Chapter 8's ideation prompts do exactly that.

**Knowing when to stop.** Ask for one more pass and you will get one more pass — a slightly different paragraph, marginally blander, because each pass toward "polish" sands another edge off. The machine has no signal for *done*. Revision loops that never converge (a failure we will catalog as F-07) usually start with a human who forgot what done looks like and a machine that will happily churn forever. You set the done criteria. Chapters 12 and 14 give you concrete ones.

**Confident error.** The assistant does not experience uncertainty the way you do. It emits fluent, well-formed, wrong statements — about your plot's internal facts if you gave it conflicting notes, about history, about law, about how tides work — with the same smooth register it uses for truths. Fluency and accuracy are uncorrelated in these systems, and this single fact earns its own chapter (Chapter 5) plus a standing rule: verify before use, whenever the claim leaves the four walls of your story.

> REALITY CHECK — The machine's errors arrive grammatically perfect. Never let its confidence serve as your evidence. For anything factual that reaches the page — dates, laws, medical details, cultural practices — the check is yours.

Two more bad-at items that matter more than they sound. **Ethics**: the tool will not warn you that your "exotic" culture is a pile of clichés, that your disabled character is a lesson plan, or that the real person you pasted in as a character might recognize themselves. It optimizes for your instruction, not for someone else's dignity. And **privacy judgment**: it will happily let you paste your unpublished manuscript, your diary, and your ex's name into a text box without reminding you that what you paste goes somewhere (Chapter 5 covers the rules). A tool with no sense of consequences needs a human who has some.

## The co-creation model: you decide, AI proposes

Here is the golden rule, and it governs every stage of the workflow you are about to learn:

> **You decide. AI proposes.**

It sounds simple enough to be a poster on a classroom wall. Its implications are more radical than they appear, so let's unpack three of them.

**Proposals are not decisions — until you make them one.** When the assistant offers five versions of your inciting incident, the offer itself is not creative work; your selection is. Writers who internalize this stop asking "is the AI's version good?" and start asking "which of these fits the story I am writing, and what does the best one *lack*?" Those are author questions. The second question — what's missing — matters more: you can splice, reject, and invert proposals, and the splice is often stronger than anything offered whole.

**The machine works for an author who has aims.** This is why Part I of this book teaches premise, stakes, character, and scene *with the tool closed*. Not because AI cannot discuss those concepts — it discusses them beautifully — but because a partner who cannot tell good from mediocre cannot select. Your aims are the selection function. Without them, "you decide" degenerates into "the loudest suggestion wins," and the loudest suggestion is usually the most generic one in the room.

**Delegation has a gradient, and you choose your position on it deliberately.** At one end: pure ideation, where the machine may draft freely because nothing it says is binding — you are mining options. In the middle: co-drafting (a defined term in this book: *producing prose with the assistant, then revising it as your own*), where sections start from machine text but end only after your rewrite passes, always anchored by your own sentences (the voice anchor technique, Chapter 10). At the other end: fact-checking and continuity passes, where the machine's independence is exactly what you want. The gradient is not a moral scale. A paragraph you accept with light edits after heavy machine drafting is not cheating — *provided* you would have staked your name on it, and provided disclosure norms (Chapter 15) are respected wherever you publish. What *is* a failure is drifting to the right end of the gradient by accident: accepting because it is Tuesday and you are tired.

What does the human add that the machine structurally cannot? Four things: **aims** (what the story is trying to be), **taste** (which risk is the right one), **experience** (the true details), and **accountability** (your name on the cover). The machine adds range, patience, and recall. The collaboration works when each side stays in its lane — and the arrangement collapses, always, the moment taste is outsourced.

Be clear-eyed about what the arrangement *costs*, because it is not free even when it works. It costs the romance of untutored inspiration — you will rarely again get to pretend the story arrived fully formed from the ether; there will be transcripts, logs, a visible trail of offers and refusals. It costs some speed at first: constraining a prompt, adjudicating suggestions, and keeping the decisions file all take minutes that pure improvisation skips, and for the first few weeks you may produce *fewer* words than before while producing better-directed ones. And it costs comfort: an auditable process means your judgments are visible to you, including the lazy ones — there is no hiding "I accepted it because it was late" from a file that asks one line of reasoning. In exchange you get what untutored inspiration never provided: repeatability. The writers who finish books are not the ones who feel most inspired on good days; they are the ones whose process still works on the mediocre days that make up most of a writing life. The co-creation model is built for those days.

> CRAFT NOTE — "Taste is the job" predates AI by centuries. Every writer you admire has a high rejection rate: they cut more than they keep and know why. The machine raises the number of options crossing your desk; it does not lower the standard of the gate. If anything, the gate matters more now.

### Why beginners specifically have the most to gain

It is a fair question, asked with a shrug: if taste and lived experience are the irreplaceable parts, and beginners have the *least* of both, is this tool aimed at exactly the wrong people? The studies we just cited hint at the opposite answer — the biggest measured gains went to novices — and the reason is worth understanding, because it shapes how you will use the assistant for the rest of this book.

Beginners lose time to three specific frictions that experienced writers have already solved. First, **vocabulary poverty**: you know something is wrong with your scene, but you do not yet have the words "protagonist's scene goal" or "stakes ladder," so you cannot fix it and sometimes cannot even locate it. The assistant is an instant dictionary of craft terms, and unlike a mentor, it will explain "psychic distance" eleven times without a flicker of impatience. Second, **option poverty**: the lonely blank page multiplies into a thousand undecided possibilities, and a beginner — lacking a catalog of finished stories in their hands — stalls. The machine converts stalls into menus: five possible next scenes beat one imagined one. Third, **feedback poverty**: beginners are told "keep going" or "it's great" by friends, neither of which is information. The assistant, prompted with skepticism (you will learn exactly how in Chapters 6 and 8), produces *specific, arguable* notes — and arguable notes are the raw material of craft growth.

What beginners lack — the taste to filter, the experience to supply truth — they can build *faster* with the machine in the loop, provided the loop is designed correctly. Every constraint you write into a prompt is a compressed act of taste. Every suggestion you reject with a one-line reason in your decisions file is a rep at the gate. Every continuity error you catch the machine missing, or the machine catches you making, teaches you something about how stories hold together. Used this way, the assistant is less a ghostwriter than a piano in the room: it does not play for you, but it makes scales audible and practice less lonely, and you still have to move your hands.

The design principle falls out of this: **let the machine handle range and recall; keep judgment reps for yourself.** Part I's unaided exercises are not a tax before the fun begins — they are you building the filter the machine cannot have. Writers who skip them do not get faster stories; they get faster stories they cannot evaluate, which is the most expensive kind.

## Tour of the workflow

You are about to spend this whole book inside one process. It is worth seeing the whole map once before walking it. Every story you ever write passes through these stages, with or without AI — our project is pairing each stage with the techniques that genuinely fit it.

**1. Idea and premise (Chapter 8).** From raw seed to a tested premise: protagonist, goal, obstacle, stakes — stress-tested, stress-tested, logged in a line, and filed. *AI's role: option generation and adversarial testing. Your role: selection.*

**2. Outline and structure (Chapter 9).** Premise → one-page skeleton → beat-level outline, through critique passes until you trust it. *AI's role: structure critic, pacing checker. Your role: adjudicating every note in writing.*

**3. Drafting (Chapter 10).** Scene by scene, from beats to prose, in one of five drafting modes, voice-anchored. *AI's role: expand, rephrase, draft on demand. Your role: the opening sentence of every scene, at minimum — and the final say on all of it.*

**4. Characters (Chapter 11).** Interviews, arc tracking, voice differentiation — run alongside drafting, because characters deepen as the pages accumulate. *AI's role: sparring partner and scribe. Your role: the human truths the machine can only imitate.*

**5. Revision (Chapter 12).** The pass system: structure → character → scene → line → continuity, one job per pass, all suggestions logged. *AI's role: developmental reader, line editor, continuity checker. Your role: running the passes and adjudicating.*

**6. Feedback (Chapter 13).** Human beta readers, briefed with real questions, plus — carefully bounded — simulated perspectives for pre-flight checks. *AI's role: question designer and triage assistant, never the reader. Your role: the reader of readers.*

**7. Publication and practice (Chapters 14–16).** Protecting your voice, handling disclosure and law, choosing a path, and building the 90-day plan that turns one finished story into a practice.

Notice the pattern in the italic lines. At every stage the division holds: the machine's freedom is widest when stakes are lowest (brainstorming) and narrowest when the page is final (line editing, facts). Chapters 5–7 — Part II — give you the toolkit this workflow assumes: how the model works, how to prompt it, and how to set up a project that survives months of work.

> TRY IT — Take sixty seconds and mark your position on the map. Which stage hurts most right now — ideas, outlining, drafting, revision? Write it down. By the end of the book, every stage should have at least one technique of yours that makes it less scary. You will come back to this sentence after Chapter 12 and be surprised which stage changed places.

## Worked example: one weak premise, three improvements

Let's expand the gesture from the opening hook into a full annotated demonstration, because this small loop is the molecular unit of everything in Part III.

**Starting material (one sentence, deliberately weak):**

> A young man discovers he has a superpower and must decide whether to use it.

This is not a premise; it is a genre-shaped hole. Note what it lacks: a specific person (he could be anyone), a concrete goal (decide *what*, exactly?), a cost (using a superpower how — what can go wrong?), and time (by when?). An untrained writer can stare at a hole like this for a week. Let's run three AI-assisted improvements, each adding one missing element, each with the machine doing only its lane.

---

**Improvement 1 — specificity via constraints.**

```text
Take this weak premise: "A young man discovers he has a superpower and
must decide whether to use it."

Give me five versions that each pin down ALL FOUR of these, with no
gaps: (1) a specific protagonist with a job and a daily embarrassment;
(2) the exact power, with one hard limitation; (3) a concrete goal he
must achieve in the next 30 days that the power affects; (4) what he
loses personally if he uses it. Keep each version under 60 words.
Do not make the power fight-related.
```

*Assistant output (abridged):*

> [...] 3. **Night-shift toll collector Ana Sofía Ríos** can hear the last lie a person told. Her brother's custody hearing is in thirty days, and she can wreck his ex's testimony in one sentence — but every lie she hears aloud makes her forget a day of her own memory. She is already forgetting their mother's face. [...]

**Commentary.** The constraints did the work: the job, the limitation, the deadline, the cost — each was demanded, not hoped for. Notice how the "no fight-related power" constraint and the "under 60 words" constraint prevented the default superhero answer. Also notice what the machine still can't judge: whether forgetting-a-day is *too* gimmicky, whether a custody hearing is the right pressure for your purposes. Selection remains yours.

---

**Improvement 2 — stakes via adversarial pressure.**

Take whichever of the five you liked (suppose you kept Ana Sofía) and make the machine attack it:

```text
You are a skeptical fiction editor. Attack this premise: [paste premise].
Identify: (a) where the stakes are merely emotional but never become
concrete; (b) the most generic version of this story an AI or a human
would write by default; (c) one question the premise refuses to answer
that a reader will ask by page 3. Be specific. No praise.
```

*Assistant output (abridged):*

> [...] (b) The generic version: she uses the power once, feels guilty, learns power-corrupts lesson, ends with her choosing memory over revenge. Readers have seen it. (c) The premise won't say: why now? Why does the hearing need *her* lie-hearing — what stops her from hiring a lawyer and never touching the power? [...]

**Commentary.** This is the adversarial move: by assigning the editor role and forbidding praise, you bypass the machine's agreeable default (the failure catalogued as F-05 in Chapter 8). The "why now?" question is genuinely good — not because the machine is wise, but because you forced it to work against the text instead of around it. Your adjudication: the lawyer objection is fixable (no money; legal aid waitlist is six months — one sentence of story fixes it); the generic-version warning is real, and it now goes into your outline as the version to *avoid*.

---

**Improvement 3 — selection and stitch, by you.**

The third improvement is not a prompt. It is you writing two sentences no model generated:

> Ana Sofía cannot afford the lawyer, and the legal-aid letter on her desk says the wait is seven months. The hearing is in thirty days.

Now paste the whole package — your stitched premise plus your two sentences — into a final pass:

```text
Here is my premise, with a fix I added. In 90 words max, state the
central dramatic question of this story in one sentence — the question
the ending must answer. Do not add new plot. Do not praise it.
```

*Assistant output:*

> When Ana Sofía can buy her brother's future with one stolen lie — and her own past one lie at a time — which memories will she trade away before the custody hearing, and will she recognize herself when it's over?

**Commentary.** The machine compressed your aims back into a single interrogative you can hold in your head while drafting. That is a *logline function*, and it is one of the assistant's better jobs: not inventing, but stating. When the compressed version misstates your story, you have learned what your story actually is — the disagreement is information. Chapter 8 builds this loop into a full premise workflow; the reason we ran it small here is that everything bigger is the same shape: **constrain → generate → attack → select → stitch → verify.** Five of those six verbs are yours.

Total time for the three-pass loop: under five minutes. Total value: a premise that went from genre-hole to interrogable in one sitting — with a record, in your decisions file, of every offer you rejected and why. That record is not bureaucracy. It is the growing proof, written in your own judgments, that you are the author.

### The same arrangement, scaled up

One more stop on the tour, because it addresses the fear under most beginners' questions: *if the machine can write paragraphs, what is left for me — proofreading its novel?* The scale answers that. Watch what each stage's prompts actually ask for in this book.

At premise stage (Chapter 8), the machine is a **quarry**: raw material you blast and shape yourself, and most of what it offers gets parked or smashed. At outline stage (Chapter 9), it is a **structural engineer inspecting your drawings**: it finds the hole in Act II, but it does not get to move the load-bearing walls — you adjudicate every note in writing, and half of them will be wrong for your story. At drafting stage (Chapter 10), it is a **stunt double**: it performs the dangerous take, sometimes better than you could that day, but the star's close-up — the scenes only *you* could have wanted — starts with your sentences, and every take gets your performance pass. At revision stage (Chapter 12), it is a **line of alert readers** who never tire: developmental reader, continuity hawk, second pair of eyes at 2 a.m., each deployed on one pass with one job. At feedback stage (Chapter 13), it is a **rehearsal audience** — useful for detecting your blind spots, forbidden from being your only audience.

Notice the trajectory: from raw material to trusted checker, never from author to subcontractor. At no point does the machine hold the aims of the book in its — it cannot hold aims at all. The closest thing it has to aims is the sum of your instructions, which is just your aims, reflected back. That is the co-creation model at full scale: an author with aims, steering a tool with range, all the way from seed to shipped story. Chapters 5 through 7 give you the tool, the prompting language, and the filing cabinet. Then Part III builds the machine's contributions up, stage by stage, with your name on every decision.

### Common objections, answered honestly

Before closing the chapter, three questions that live in the back of most readers' minds. Better to answer them now than to let them sabotage your exercises.

**"Won't I become dependent?"** Dependency is a risk, and this book's structure is partly an antidote. Part I is entirely unaided, so you will have proven to yourself that you can generate and revise without the tool; Chapter 14 returns you to your unaided baseline for a direct comparison of what changed. Dependence shows up as inability to proceed when the tool is unavailable — notice that, log it, and run a session unaided to break the spell. Healthy use looks different: the assistant speeds up stages you *could* do yourself, and you take periodic unaided reps the way a driver still learns to park without the camera's guidelines. The decisions file keeps you honest here too: an author who can articulate every acceptance has not outsourced the process, whatever the transcript's length suggests.

**"Is this even allowed?"** Sometimes yes, sometimes no, and the answer is venue-specific and changing: some magazines ban AI-assisted submissions outright, some permit them with disclosure, some don't care about your tools and care intensely about your story. Chapter 15 teaches you to find and follow each venue's current policy in under ten minutes, plus what copyright does and does not protect when AI is in the loop. The short version, until then: **check before you submit, disclose when required, and never paste other people's unpublished work or private details into a chat box.**

**"I'm not technical."** Nothing in this book requires it. If you can write an email and copy-paste a paragraph, you have every prerequisite skill. The prompts are templates with blanks; the project setup is a folder; the "versioning" is numbering files like `draft-03`. The technical complexity of the underlying models is real and fascinating, and Chapter 5 spends a few pages on exactly as much of it as changes your decisions — no more.

If one of those three answers was the thing keeping your baseline unwritten: good. It is written now, or it will be within fifteen minutes of finishing this chapter. The tool can wait.

## Exercises

- **Try (15 min):** Write 200 words of a story, unaided — no AI, no looking anything up. Pick any idea you have ever had and go. Save the file with today's date: `baseline-YYYY-MM-DD`. This is your control sample; Chapter 14 will have you revise it and compare. Do not judge it. Measure it.

- **Stretch (1–2 h):** Take that 200-word baseline and run a light AI-assisted revision on it. Use the structure from the worked example: ask for three targeted improvement options (one on pacing, one on specificity, one on a sentence-level tighten), attack the draft once with a skeptical-editor prompt, then apply *only* what you agree with, by hand. On a separate line, write four sentences: what improved, what got worse, what you rejected and why, and whether you would submit the revised version or the original. Keep this note — it is the first row of your judgment log.

- **Project (ongoing):** Start a **decisions file** (`decisions.md` in your project folder — Chapter 7 shows the full folder). Every time you accept or reject a substantive AI suggestion, add one line: date · what was suggested · accepted or rejected · one-line reason. Keep it forever. It is your taste, made visible; it is also, should you ever need it, your record of human authorship (Chapter 15).

A note on doing the Stretch honestly: the point is not to prove that AI revision is better or worse than your original — it is to *generate disagreement you can inspect*. If the revised paragraph beats yours, you owe it a sentence in the log about why (specificity? cutting your throat-clearing intro?). If yours beats the machine's, you have just learned which of your instincts to protect going forward — that is the more valuable row. If it is a tie, the honest entry is "tie; kept mine because it is mine" — authorship includes the right to keep an equal paragraph for reasons of voice. None of these outcomes is failure. The only failed Stretch is one where you pasted the machine's version in without reading it.

## Pitfalls

> PITFALL — **Letting AI set the taste.** *Symptom:* your draft is fluent, competent, and somehow not yours; you catch yourself defending sentences you don't remember choosing; every scene feels like it could be in anyone's book. *Why it happens:* the machine proposes constantly and rejection costs effort, so acceptance becomes the path of least resistance — taste quietly outsourced while you were busy. *Fix:* reinstate the gate. For one full session, reject every suggestion until you can articulate in one sentence what the story is trying to be; then accept only proposals that serve that sentence. Rebuild your decisions file with the reasons. *Prevention:* the voice-anchor habit from Chapter 10 (your paragraph first, always) plus the Chapter 14 style sheet — structural defenses so the drift has to fight to get in.

Related failure for Appendix C: agreeable brainstorming with zero decisions (F-05); voice erosion (F-09).

## Key takeaways

- **You decide, AI proposes.** The rule holds at every stage: the machine widens options; your aims, taste, experience, and name choose among them.
- **The honest inventory:** AI excels at variation, patient brainstorming, continuity checks, and tireless iteration help — and fails at taste, lived experience, true novelty, knowing when to stop, confident error, ethics, and privacy judgment.
- **Beginners benefit most — when they keep the gate.** The productivity studies that show the biggest gains for novices studied short tasks; they support "assistance helps," not "assistance replaces."
- **The workflow is a loop with a fixed division of labor:** widest machine freedom at brainstorming, narrowest at the final page — idea → outline → draft → characters → revision → feedback → publish.
