## Opening hook

Here is a paragraph about a woman arriving at her dead mother's house:

> Mara drove up the coast in February, thinking about everything she had to do. When she reached the house, she went inside and found that Theo had already started packing up their mother's things. They argued about the sale, as she had expected, and she realized that nothing between them would be easy.

Nothing is *wrong* with it, exactly. Every fact is present: arrival, season, motive, discovery, conflict, emotional weather. It is also dead on the page — a stage manager's report of a play no one will attend. Now the same content, staged:

> The key stuck twice before the door gave, and the smell hit her first — cedar and the cold-sweet rot of a drain nobody had run in nine months. Half the kitchen was already in boxes. Theo had done them in the wrong order, lids on the bottom shelf, and their mother's good soup pot sitting on top of a carton of towels, as if it were the treasure of a shipwreck.
>
> "You started," Mara said.
>
> "Somebody had to." He didn't turn from the window. "You'd have made a spreadsheet."
>
> "A spreadsheet is how things get —"
>
> "Done. Yeah." Now he did turn, and his hands were the wrong kind of clean. "Everything gets done. She asked for you in December, by the way. Thought you should know that before you inventory the pots."

Same facts. But now there is a *scene*: a goal (assess the house, get the conversation onto the sale), a conflict that arrives through behavior instead of narration ("the wrong order" of boxes; "the wrong kind of clean"), and an outcome (the December information lands — and changes what the next scene must be about). The transformation between those two paragraphs is the skill this chapter installs, in four parts: **scene anatomy** (goal, conflict, outcome), **show and tell as tools** (not religions), **point of view as camera and consciousness** (chosen and sustained), and **setting as pressure** (places that limit choices). This is the last chapter of Part I, and by its end your Project tier has you doing what the whole part promised: finishing a short story draft *without AI*.

## Scene = goal + conflict + outcome

The **scene** is fiction's unit of experience. Chapters are filing tabs; paragraphs are breath; the scene is where a reader either leans in or starts shopping. The good news is that scenes have a skeleton as reliable as Chapter 2's — and most beginner scenes fail for one of three missing bones.

**Goal.** Someone in the scene wants something *checkable* before it ends: a signature, an answer, a borrowed tool, a confession, a moment of peace that the scene's other occupant refuses to grant. The goal can be tiny (get through dinner without the subject coming up) but it must exist, and it must sit in a specific character — usually the POV character, sometimes the one pressing against them. Test: *could a director stage "scene works" as a visible action?* "They reconnect emotionally" fails; "she signs the paper; she doesn't" passes.

**Conflict.** Something opposes the goal *before the scene ends* — another want, a physical limit, a rule, the weather, the self. Opposition must be *active*, not atmospheric: a sad room does not conflict; a brother packing boxes in the wrong order does. Notice the hook's revision — the conflict arrived as detail *plus* dialogue turn, never as authorial warning ("they argued, as expected" was the dead version's sin: announcing the weather instead of letting it rain).

**Outcome.** The scene ends with *something changed*: goal achieved, refused, redirected, or superseded by worse news. The change can be a whispered reversal or a slammed door; what it cannot be is *nothing* — no-resolution scenes are the leading cause of mush in middle drafts. The outcome should also *load the next scene*: December's revelation in the hook demands a response scene, which is momentum built from scene furniture instead of plot contrivance.

Three bones, then a fourth concept that beginners use without naming: **sequel beats.** Real scenes breathe as *scene → reaction → dilemma → decision*, where the reaction beat (the character alone with what just happened: stunned, reordering the day) and the decision (which launches the next scene) are where interiority lives. You will not always write all four parts — flash compresses them into a sentence of stunned silence; thrillers truncate reaction to a single line of action — but knowing the full cycle lets you choose *where* you're cutting, instead of leaving your protagonist reacting nowhere on the page.

### Scene endings: how to turn off the light

If outcomes are the scene's third bone, the *last line* is where the bone meets the reader's nervous system. A scene ending is a contract for the next thirty seconds of attention, and the catalog of honest ways to honor it is shorter than you think:

- **The door-slam turn:** a line or action that physically or socially ends the beat while opening pressure on the next (Theo's "Does 'when' go in the list?"). Your workhorse; strongest when the line is *about the scene's subject* while *refusing to resolve it*.
- **The loaded decision:** the POV character chooses — even microscopically ("she did not type"). Interiority as cliff: the reader leans toward the action the decision implies.
- **The reversal of information:** something is learned that re-prices everything previously said. Ends must land *after* the scene's largest change; ending before it is how scenes die in the middle of their own event.
- **The image close:** a concrete object absorbs the emotion (the soup pot on the shipwreck carton). Literary fiction's default; works only if the image was *loaded* earlier — unloaded images at scene ends are decoration with a bow on it.
- **The callback rhyme:** an echo of an earlier line, now changed in meaning ("Everything gets done." → "Yeah."). Cheap in words, expensive in planning — put the setup beat in your outline.
- **The fade on decision pending:** rare, deliberate: scene ends *inside* the dilemma, mid-breath, when the sequel beat itself is the point. Overused by anxious writers; if your scenes all end mid-breath, you are postponing outcomes, not pacing them.

The failure modes to hunt in revision: **the double-ending** (strong last line followed by a summary sentence that explains it — delete the explanation; the line already did the job), **the trailing-adverb exit** ("...angrily."), and **the authorial fade-out** ("And so the evening passed, with much left unsaid" — narration stepping in to collect a scene it didn't stage). Test every scene's last line by covering everything above it: does the line alone make a curious person want the next line of the *next* scene? If it needs its own paragraph of context to land, either move the landing earlier or cut the context.

**Dialogue mechanics, the working subset.** Dialogue in scenes does three jobs simultaneously: *pursues the goal* (every line is either advance, defense, or feint), *reveals voice* (Chapter 3's field 10 — diction, syntax, habits), and *manages information* (who knows what, who learns what, who is lying). The mechanics that serve those jobs: attribute with action beats more than "he said/she said" tags when the cast exceeds two; let subtext do the heavy lifting (characters argue about the soup pot when they are arguing about their mother); use interruption and non-answer as pressure tools; and — the test that saves every draft — read the speeches aloud with the tags covered: if two characters' lines swap without changing meaning, their voices are not differentiated yet (Chapter 11 fixes this systematically with AI's help; here you fix it by ear).

> TRY IT — Take your favorite scene from any story and label its bones in the margin: underline the goal (yours or theirs), bracket the opposition, box the outcome. Five minutes, no writing required. You will find the goal sometimes hides *inside* a line of dialogue and the outcome sometimes is a silence — that's fine; you're training recognition, not enforcing a straitjacket.

## Showing vs. telling, practically

Every beginner meets the commandment "show, don't tell" as absolute, follows it into purple monstrosities — three paragraphs describing a handshake — and then swings to pure summary and writes a synopsis with dialogue. The mature version is unexciting and unstoppable: **tell to orient, show to matter.**

**Tell (summary, statement)** is efficient and honest: it covers time, sets context, delivers facts the scene's pressure doesn't need to stage. "They argued about the sale, as she had expected" is perfectly good *summary* — it belongs in transition, in chapter openings, anywhere the reader needs the map before the terrain. Telling also controls *pacing*: fast periods of telling (weeks pass) make the shown moments (the kitchen, the December line) feel like slow-motion — which is exactly what slow-motion is for.

**Show (scene, dramatization)** is expensive and therefore should be spent where the story's value concentrates: on decisions, turns, wounds activated, relationships bending. The rule of thumb — if the reader would shrug at the outcome, don't stage it; if the reader's pulse should change, stage it to the bone. Show the *consequences* of what telling summarizes: we tell that Mara and Theo haven't spoken in eleven months, and show the specific way he doesn't offer her a cup of the tea he's drinking.

Two practical diagnostics keep the balance honest. **The import test:** does this moment reprice a relationship, reveal a choice, or land a reversal? Stage it. **The redundancy test:** are you telling *and then* showing the same beat? Cut the telling (or cut the showing) — doing both is how drafts double in length while losing force. And a third guard against the show-dogma's most common crime: **sensory overload as substitute.** Sweat, trembling hands, and "her heart hammered" are not showing if they appear twelve times as generic distress signals — generic sensation is telling with a thesaurus. Specific sensation (the wrong kind of clean on his hands) shows; repeated symptom-vocabulary tells.

> CRAFT NOTE — The maxim's lineage is workshop shorthand for a real principle (dramatize what matters), not a law of nature — published fiction tells constantly, and the best tellers (Hemingway's summary, Didion's chill, Le Guin's parable mode) make telling an aesthetic. What beginners need first is the *discrimination* skill: which moments deserve budget. Earn that with the tell/show rule; then, when you choose to tell something enormous in one cold sentence, it will read as mastery rather than fatigue.

## POV and psychic distance

**Point of view** answers: whose consciousness is delivering this story, in what grammatical person, with what degree of intimacy? Three standard choices, all legitimate:

- **First person ("I").** Maximum intimacy and voice; a built-in unreliability filter; cost: everything must pass through one narrator's knowledge, and the narration must sound sustainable for the whole book. Best when voice *is* the story's engine.
- **Close third (he/she, anchored).** The default of modern literary and genre fiction: third-person grammar with the camera locked to one character's perceptions *per scene* — we see what they see, learn what they learn, and (in the tightest versions) word the world in their idiom. Versatile, controllable, and the POV of Example A.
- **Omniscient (the narrator who knows).** Broad, knowing, often wry; can move between minds and across time; cost: it demands a *narrator voice* distinct from any character's — the hardest trick to fake. Best when the story's subject includes the society, not just a consciousness.

**Psychic distance** is the finer instrument that works inside any choice: *how deep into the character's head is the sentence right now?* A classic ladder runs from outside observation to raw interiority:

1. *Mara looked at the boxes.* (camera outside)
2. *Mara scowled at the boxes.* (outside + readable emotion)
3. *The boxes annoyed her — he'd started without her.* (interior summary)
4. *He'd started without her. Of course he had.* (interior voice, the character "speaking" in free indirect style)
5. *Of course. Logistics first. Always.* (deep idiom — the wound's own words, barely translated)

Free indirect style — narration that borrows the character's diction without quotation marks (rung 4–5) — is the workhorse of close third: it lets interiority arrive *as prose* instead of as reported speech ("she thought"). The skill is not "stay deep"; it is **distance by design**: pull out for action beats (a fight needs the camera at arm's length to show bodies), dive deep for decisions, and — the part beginners break — **do not hop minds mid-scene** unless you have consciously chosen head-hopping (some contemporary fiction does; most beginner "hopping" is accidental drift).

**Consistency drills.** Choose POV per scene *before* drafting, write it at the top of the document ("Close third — Mara — past tense"), and run two checks at revision: (1) **Pronoun audit** — highlight every interior access ("she remembered," "he couldn't stand"); all must belong to the chosen anchor; (2) **Oracle check** — delete any sentence that reports a fact outside the anchor's perception ("Theo felt guilty as he watched her" — unless Theo's scene, Mara cannot know it). Tense locks with POV: past or present, chosen once, defended in Chapter 10's prompt constraints when AI joins the draft.

**Choosing POV for your story: a decision procedure.** When in doubt, run the questions in order — each answer eliminates options:

1. *Whose life does this story's meaning live in?* If the theme is one consciousness's transformation (Example A: Mara's logistics-versus-presence), the anchor is non-negotiable: close third or first, that character only. If the meaning lives in a *community* or a *situation* (a town's fall, a crew's dilemma across stations), omniscient or rotating close third enters the table — but rotating anchors means one scene per anchor, hard breaks, and an explicit style note.
2. *How much should the reader know that the protagonist doesn't?* Need dramatic irony (reader sees the trap before Mara does)? Close third tightens beautifully — withhold the oracle, let the reader outrun her. Need the reader *inside* the trap with no warning? First person or maximally tight close third. Need dual knowledge as a structural engine (mystery, conspiracy)? That's the rotating or omniscient answer — knowing the cost, which is voice dilution.
3. *Can you sustain this narrator's diction for 80,000 words?* Read your three candidate voices aloud from a 300-word sample *you* wrote (not the machine's — voice sustainability tests must be yours). First person fails here more than people expect: the quirk that charms on page 3 can be a prison by page 300. Close third fails in the other direction — if your sample keeps sliding into the character's idiom anyway (rungs 4–5 living rent-free), you may be a first-person writer wearing third's clothes; either commit to free-indirect-heavy close third deliberately, or switch.
4. *What will you lock in the AI prompts later?* Not because the tool decides, but because Chapter 10's constraints are only as clear as your own answer: "close third, past, Mara-anchored, distance range 2–4" is a lockable instruction; "third person, kind of close" is not.

Write the decision — with question 1's answer highlighted — into your project notes today. Everything downstream (audits, prompt locks, your own re-reads) inherits it.

> REALITY CHECK — POV errors are invisible to their authors and glaring to readers — the two-sentence head-hop at 2 a.m. feels natural because *you* know both minds. This is why revision passes (Chapter 12) exist, and why a simple mechanical audit catches what re-reading cannot: your brain patches continuity the way it patches typos.

## Setting as pressure

Setting is not backdrop; it is **the set of choices currently available.** A porch with one chair argues differently than a porch with two. Porto Alvaro in February — one road out, storm season, a ferry schedule printed but rarely kept — does not "create atmosphere"; it *removes options*, and removed options are plot. The working question, whenever you place a scene: **what can't happen here, and what must?**

Three ways setting earns its rent:

1. **Constraint.** The house with one bathroom forces the morning collision. The sinking research station forces the proximity. Constraint also *compresses* scenes economically: characters trapped by setting skip small talk because the setting already did the introductions.
2. **Pressure on the flaw.** Best settings attack your protagonist's Chapter 3 pattern directly. The storm answers Mara's logistics with logistics that fail — the perfect counter-weapon: her methods work everywhere except the one place the story needs them broken.
3. **Meaning that predates the plot.** A cannery that closed the year her father died is not decoration; every scene in it plays over that fact whether or not anyone mentions it. Anchor setting meanings *early and small* (a sign, a smell, a locked wing), and the climax can cash the check without a speech.

**Worldbuilding on need, not on enthusiasm.** Genre writers know the trap intimately: the 4,000-word history that delays Chapter 1, the magic system's rules explained before we know a character's name. The discipline: reveal setting **as the camera needs it** — in the order the POV character would encounter it, in doses sized to the scene's pressure, with the fascinating-but-unpressured details parked in your notes (they'll season later scenes). If a detail doesn't constrain a choice, constrain a perception, or mean something by story's end, it's not worldbuilding yet; it's a hobby. AI will happily generate encyclopedias of your world in Part III — the skill you're installing now is knowing which three sentences of them your next scene actually needs.

### The concrete-detail ladder

"Be specific" is the most repeated and least operational advice in fiction. Here is the operation, as a ladder you can run on any paragraph:

1. **Abstraction:** "The kitchen was depressing." (Theme stated; nothing seen.)
2. **Generic concrete:** "The kitchen was dirty, with old dishes and peeling paint." (Seen — by anyone, anywhere; the reader's placeholder kitchen.)
3. **Owned concrete:** "Theo had packed around the mildew ring under the sink instead of cleaning it." (A *specific* neglect, caused by a *specific* person's behavior — this detail could only exist in this story.)
4. **Pressured concrete:** the same mildew ring noticed *by Mara while she's pretending to inventory*, so the detail carries character, relationship, *and* time's passage in one beat. (Setting as pressure and detail as meaning arrive together.)

Most first drafts live at rung 2 — correct, interchangeable, forgettable. The climb from 2 to 3 asks one question (*whose fingerprint is on this mess?*); the climb from 3 to 4 asks another (*whose perception needs this, and why now?*). Run the ladder on your Part I milestone's ten plainest paragraphs; you will usually find five details worth keeping, three worth deleting outright, and two that upgrade to rung 4 and start paying rent. When AI joins in Chapter 10, this ladder is also the sharpest instrument you have for diagnosing the machine's output: its default description is almost always rung 2 with nicer adjectives — which is exactly why "description sharpener" earns a prompt card (PC-16).

> CRAFT NOTE — Specificity is not the same as quantity. A paragraph of six owned details beats a page of fourteen generic ones, because the reader's attention is finite and *ownership* is what converts seeing into remembering. The deepest trick of the masters is subtraction: one scar, one wrong-order box, one wrong-kind-of-clean — chosen so precisely that the reader supplies the rest of the room for free.

### The orienting-tell cookbook

If telling-orients-show-matters is the rule, beginners reasonably ask: *what do healthy tells actually look like?* Here is the cookbook, four recipes with Example A's material, so the "tell" side of the ledger is a craft skill instead of a guilty default:

1. **Transition-tell:** covers time and relocation in one or two lines — "By Thursday the kitchen was mapped and the harbor had given up pretending the ferry would run." Entry only: no dialogue, no conflict, ends where the next scene's goal can start.
2. **Status-tell:** updates the ledger of relationships and plans between scenes — "They had settled, by unspoken treaty, to discuss the sale only when the notary was present." Use when the *arrangement itself* will be broken later; the tell is the rule the scene then violates.
3. **Backstory-tell:** delivers the wound's facts efficiently, when staging them would steal the present's budget — "Her mother had died in March, on a Tuesday Mara spent in a survey review." Two sentences maximum per dose; any longer and you've left telling for reenactment with the wrong POV.
4. **Narrative-voice tell:** summary filtered through the anchor's idiom (free indirect summary: *Of course the road was closed; the town closed roads the way other towns threw parties*). The most dangerous and most delicious type — it builds voice *while* compressing — and it must stay strictly inside the anchor's knowledge and diction.

Each recipe passes the import test by *not applying to imports*: the moment a beat reprices a relationship, it leaves the cookbook and gets staged. Run this test while revising: highlight every tell in a chapter; each one should be one of these four types, and any tell that is *also* the scene's emotional climax is misfiled — convert it to scene, or move the scene's real climax after it.

### Distance-by-design: the rung schedule exercise

The ladder's practical instrument, specified so "psychic distance by design" becomes a *plan* rather than a vibe: before drafting any interiority-heavy scene, write a **rung schedule** — a four-to-six line table saying *which rung each beat-block occupies and why*. What the schedule *teaches while you write it*: **(1) rungs move for reasons** — every change in the table cites a pressure-event (the door-line, the decision), so distance stops being ambient and starts being *caused*; **(2) depth is a resource spent where the wound is** — B3's 4→5 excursion would be *exhausting* at scene-length (the ladder's "too deep is unreadable" rule aimed at *scheduling*, not just sentences: one deepest-rung block per short scene is usually the *budget*); **(3) the decision-block pulls back deliberately** — a pattern worth memorizing: *interiority rises into emotion, falls into action* (B5's 1→2), the inverse of beginners' instinct to narrate choices *from inside* (which blurs them — the hand-on-keys *close external* beat is where readers lean forward, because external *shows* commitment without the narrator's commentary hedging it in real time); **(4) distance-slips become *checkable* violations** — after drafting, the pronoun-and-perception Oracle runs *against the schedule* (a 5-rung sentence in B2's block = *either* a schedule error *or* a drift — one of the two, adjudicated consciously, never left as "felt right at the time" — the schedule converting Ch 4's detective-work from *ambush* to *audit*). The exercise's five-minute version for scene-days: three columns (block, rung, why) on the beat list's margin — and the compounding effect across a draft: distance patterns become *legible in the margin-note archives* (B-blocks clustering at 2 in act-one scenes, 4→5 excursions *scheduled* for the fight-beats — the scene-level craft graduating to *structure-level style*: which is what "psychic distance by design" meant all along — **not a per-sentence feeling, a per-scene intention with the intention written down where your next session can find it.**

### Scene-starting drills: five openings, one situation

Because beginnings are where scene-craft is *decided* (readers shop early — the chapter's opening premise), here is the standard drill at full specification: take **one situation** — our reader's storm-kitchen scene, pressure-loaded — and write **five different scene-openings for it**, each locked to a different starting species, each ≤120 words, each ending where the *next* beat would take over (the drill's constraint: you may not *reach* the argument in any of them — openings must *load* it, not *have* it):

**Species 1 — mid-action cold open:** start inside a physical act already underway ("The box was already open by the time she heard the car…" — no establishing, no weather, no throat-clearing: the reader arrives *holding something*; the craft-risk to notice while writing: cold opens *owe* orientation within a paragraph or the disorientation curdles into confusion — your test: read species-1 aloud with a stranger's patience, mark where they'd ask "wait, who?"); **Species 2 — object-first:** open on a *thing* under pressure ("The sale notice had been on the table so long the coffee ring had darkened around its corner" — the prop as thesis: scene's stakes *externalized into one artifact* before any person speaks; craft-risk: object-openings slip into *inventory* — if sentences 3–5 are also the table's contents, you've written a still-life, not a load; one object, one *stain of time*, then a person must enter); **Species 3 — dialogue-first:** open on the door-line itself, tags minimal ("— just things, Mara. We're not keeping *things*." — conflict-as-entry: the scene's argument *already running* before narration gets a word; craft-risk: the *unanchored volley* (Ch 11's quip-treadmill's opening hour) — the first two lines must *locate* us (who's in the room, what's at stake physically) *without* an establishing paragraph — one action-beat woven into the exchange does the loading: the hand that doesn't let go of the keys); **Species 4 — interiority-first:** open *inside* the wound's register ("Her father's rule had been clear about boxes: label by room, never by hope" — the self-story voiced *before* the event it will frame, so the reader receives the argument *through* the pattern; craft-risk: diary-trap adjacency (interiority-first *must* terminate in an external pressure-line within the block or it becomes narration-about-narration — the pressure: a car door outside, a voice, a *knock*); **Species 5 — time-stamp/pressure-clock:** open on the deadline ("Eleven days to the notary's second letter" — the clock as entry: temporal pressure *established before character*, every subsequent beat inheriting urgency; craft-risk: clock-openings *borrow* suspense rather than *earn* it — if the eleven days don't *touch* the first paragraph's physical scene (the notice, the boxes, the keys), the number is *decoration* — tie it or cut it).

**The debrief (what five-in-a-row actually teaches):** writing all five *for the same situation* reveals which species your habit defaults to (the one you wrote first is your comfort — log it in the mode/style notes: habit-awareness), which species the *scene's pressure actually favors* (the kitchen scene likely wants 2 or 3 — object-loading or door-line-cold — *not* 4, whose interiority-first shape belongs to the quieter journal-scenes — the species-choice is *scene-specific craft*, discoverable *only* by trying each and hearing the difference at 120 words' resolution), and — the drill's deepest layer — that **the five openings are five *stories about the same story*** (species 1's version foregrounds *action-legacy*; 2's, *property-and-time*; 3's, *relationship-conflict*; 4's, *inherited-pattern*; 5's, *deadline*): POV and structure questions arriving *disguised as opening sentences*, which is why experienced hands "just know" where a scene starts — they've *silently run this drill* enough times that the species-selection has become pattern-recognition; your version runs it *on purpose* for a month (five openings per major scene, two minutes each, keep the winner in the beat list's margin), until the recognition shows up on its own — the standard this book keeps: **drills until the drill disappears.**

## Worked example: a scene of Example A, before and after (unaided)

The hook showed a compressed version; here is the full pass, because the delta between summary and scene is where Part I's skills converge — and because Chapter 10 will later ask the machine to draft *this same beat* three ways, and you cannot judge its attempts against a scene you never wrote yourself.

**Before — Draft 0, summary mode (~170 words):**

> Mara arrived at the house in the afternoon. She found that Theo had already begun packing up their mother's belongings, which upset her, though she tried not to show it. They had a tense conversation about whether to sell the house. Theo was angry that she had not visited more often, and Mara felt guilty but did not admit it. He revealed that their mother had asked for Mara before she died, which made Mara feel even worse. After the argument, Mara went outside to think about what to do.

**After — staged scene, close third (Mara), past tense (~640 words):**

> The key stuck twice before the door gave, and the smell hit her first — cedar and the cold-sweet rot of a drain nobody had run in nine months. Half the kitchen was already in boxes. Theo had done them in the wrong order, lids on the bottom shelf, their mother's good soup pot balanced on a carton of towels like the treasure of a shipwreck.
>
> Mara set her own bag down slowly, the way you set down something you've decided not to fight with. *Inventory first,* the surveyor in her said. *Then the conversation.*
>
> "You started," she said.
>
> "Somebody had to." He didn't turn from the window; outside, the February light was doing its thin best over the harbor. "Figured you'd want a list."
>
> "I would, actually." She took out her phone like a badge. "If we're doing it this way, rooms in order. Kitchen's fine, but the lids go with the —"
>
> "With the lids." He turned. His hands were the wrong kind of clean, scrubbed raw at the knuckles, the scar on his left palm gone white. "That's very you, Mara. Boxes and lists. You know she kept your old schedules? Taped them up. By the phone. Every posting you sent, going back years —"
>
> "That's not —" *Don't,* the surveyor said. *Stick to the inventory.*
>
> "— and in December she asked where you were, and I had to say, again, that you were busy." He picked up the soup pot, considered it, put it down wrong on purpose. "She asked for you. Thought you should know that before you price the pots."
>
> The kitchen ticked. Somewhere a gutter was working loose in three-four time. Mara looked at the phone in her hand — the list already open, *KITCHEN* at the top, a cursor waiting like a small, patient animal — and did not type.
>
> "When?" she said.
>
> "Does 'when' go in the list?"

**Commentary (the curriculum).** *Goal:* Mara enters wanting the house assessed and the talk steered to sale logistics — visible in the phone-badge, the room order, the bitten-off "stick to the inventory." *Conflict:* Theo opposes not with refusal but with *packing* (action as argument) and then with information — his lines advance while hers defend; note that neither says the word "guilt," which stays subtext's job. *Outcome:* the December fact lands; her logistics machine visibly stalls (the cursor "like a small, patient animal" — her own metaphor turning feral on her); the scene ends with a question that opens the next one instead of resolving this one. *POV discipline:* every interior beat is Mara's — his guilt is only *shown* through "scrubbed raw" hands and the deliberate wrong-packing; the Oracle check passes: she cannot know why he chose that order, only that he did. *Tell/show balance:* telling survives in transitions ("Figured you'd want a list" does three jobs of summary inside dialogue), and the scene spends its show-budget on the one repricing moment — the December line. *Setting as pressure:* the failing gutter-clock and the thin harbor light pressure her *before* Theo does; the one-room kitchen guarantees collision. Distance ladder: runs 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 across the scene by design, deepest at "the list already open" — interiority saved for the turn. That last move — spending your deepest rung exactly once, on the outcome — is the entire economy of psychic distance in one scene.

## Exercises

- **Try (15 min):** Rewrite a summary as a scene. Take any paragraph of summary from something you've written (or the "Before" above) and stage it: write 250–400 words containing one checkable goal, active opposition arriving before the end, and an outcome that loads what comes next. Label the three bones *underneath* in one line each — goal, conflict, outcome — so the labels and the prose learn each other. If the scene wants more than 400 words, you've found your Project material.

- **Stretch (1–2 h):** Same event, two POVs. Take one charged moment (an argument, a receipt, an announcement) and write it twice: ~400 words anchored in character A, then ~400 in character B — different document, same event, no cheating into omniscience. Under each, list three facts only the POV could know and one thing the other POV knows that this one can't. Compare: what does each POV gain (access, voice) and lose (dramatic irony, the other's truth)? The better version is usually the one with more *pressure* inside the head, not more information outside it — argue your choice in five lines; this is taste rep #1 with your name on it.

- **Project (ongoing): Part I milestone.** Draft a complete short story *without AI* — 1,500 to 5,000 words, from a Chapter 2 skeleton and a Chapter 3 sheet, POV chosen and sustained, scenes built on the three bones. Rough is permitted; unfinished is not. Save it as `baseline-story-draft1` with the date. You have now done what Part I promised, and every technique in Parts II–III will be tested against this draft: you'll know exactly how much faster the machine makes stage X, and what it costs. Nothing in the rest of this book works as well for readers who skip this milestone — the comparison it gives you *is* the method.

## Pitfalls

> PITFALL — **Head-hopping.** *Symptom:* mid-scene the narration borrows another character's knowledge or feelings ("Theo, watching her, felt the old anger rise" inside Mara's scene); readers report "confusion" without knowing why; emotional access shifts to whoever needs it for the speech. *Why it happens:* omniscience is the default storytelling mode of human speech, and close third is an *artificial* restraint — drift is the natural state unless a rule is imposed; drafts written tired drift hardest. *Fix:* the POV header on every document (anchor + person + tense), then the two audits: interior-access highlight reel (every "felt/remembered/knew" must belong to the anchor) and Oracle check (delete facts no one in-scene could perceive). Rewrite offenders either from the anchor's *perception* ("His jaw did the thing it did before he said the unforgivable thing" — observable) or as pure external behavior. *Prevention:* decide the anchor per scene *before* the first sentence (Chapter 10 locks it in the prompt too, when AI drafts alongside you); if you truly want multi-POV, do it *scene by scene* with hard breaks and state the rule in your style notes — deliberate multiplicity is architecture; accidental hopping is a leak.

Related failure for Appendix C: POV/tense drift mid-scene (F-04) — the AI-drafting face of this same discipline, where Chapter 10's "locks" and Chapter 12's audits share this pitfall's fix.

## Key takeaways

- **A scene is a machine with three bones:** a checkable goal, active opposition before the end, an outcome that changes something and loads the next scene. Reaction/sequel beats carry the interiority — cut them by choice, not by drift.
- **Tell to orient, show to matter.** Summary is efficient honesty; staging is expensive and reserved for moments that reprice relationships or land turns. Generic sensation is telling in costume; import and redundancy tests arbitrate.
- **POV = camera choice; distance = lens focus.** First, close third, or omniscient — chosen per scene, defended by header + audits. Free indirect style is your interiority gear; head-hopping is your leak.
- **Setting is pressure, not decoration:** it removes options, attacks the flaw, and carries meaning that predates the plot. Worldbuild on camera need, not enthusiasm.
- **Part I's milestone stands:** you can now write a complete short story unaided — premise, sheet, scenes, POV. Keep the draft; it is the control sample the rest of this book will be measured against.
